Sargas follow the road taken. Passages marked SHADOW ROAD follow the other path: the same nights, under a different hand on the glass.
---
CHAPTER I: The Open Gate
> \*\*\[THE ROAD TAKEN]\*\*
The men on the wire had lowered their rifles by the time the boats came in under the shore. Ram saw it from the stern of the Dasharatha as the hulls grated over the last of the shallows: a long grey line of coveralls along the fence, two hundred men or more, standing motionless with their weapons pointed at the sand. Not at the boats. At the ground. Their faces were the colour of wet ash, and they did not look up as the eleven hulls came to rest on the beach in a ragged row, and their silence was so complete that Ram could hear the surf sucking at the stones beneath the hull.
Then the gate opened.
It was not a grand thing. It was a plain iron frame set into the wire, twice the height of a man, and it had been built to be closed rather than opened, so that its hinges shrieked as the two halves swung apart, a sound like a wounded animal being dragged out of a ditch. It swung wide. It stopped. And into the gap, slowly, with his hands raised and a square of white cloth tied to the end of a bamboo pole in his left fist, walked a tall slender man in a grey coat stained with salt.
Tavish stopped at the edge of the sand. He looked at the boats. He looked at the figure standing in the stern of the Dasharatha, with the sun on his neck and a plain black bow across his back, and his narrow face did not change, except around the eyes.
"You came," he said.
"I said I would," said Ram.
"You said a great many things at the terrace. Most of them were insults." Tavish's voice was dry. "My brother thought you would die on the reef. He had the whole western wire pulled back for you, so the machines could cover the channel. He said it would be a waste of good iron, and he was annoyed about it. But he gave the order."
"Where is he now?"
"Upstairs." Tavish's eyes moved to the tower behind him, a grey monolith of stone and glass that rose out of the centre of the island like a finger pointing at the sky. Its upper floors were lit. "In the top room. He's been there since the wind changed. He doesn't sleep any more. He says the sea talks to him at night, and he wants to hear what it says before anyone else does."
"And Sita?"
Something moved in Tavish's face. It was not pity. It was closer to a kind of wary respect.
"She's in the top room too," he said. "On the other side of the glass. He keeps her there because he likes to watch her sing. He says it's the only music on the island he doesn't have to pay for."
Lakshman, in the bow of the Dasharatha, made a small hard sound in his throat. His hand tightened on the black bow.
Ram did not look at him. He was looking at Tavish.
"You came out under a flag," he said.
"I did."
"Why?"
Tavish drew a breath. For a moment his mouth worked, as though he had rehearsed a speech on the way down the stairs and had forgotten every word of it. Then he let the bamboo pole fall into the sand, and the white cloth lay across the wet stones, and he said, very simply:
"Because I'm not my brother. And I'm tired of being the one who waits for someone else to decide whether I'm allowed to walk out of a room."
"Then walk out," said Ram.
Tavish looked at him.
"Here?" he said.
"Here. Onto the sand. Where the men on the wire can see you do it."
Tavish hesitated. Then he stepped forward over the wet stones, and past the pole, and out onto the open beach, and when he stood in the full light of the morning sun, a few paces from the boats, he drew himself up very straight, the way he had stood in the Shadow on the salt flats, waiting for a king to make him a promise.
"There," he said quietly. "I'm out."
"Yes," said Ram.
"And now?"
"Now you'll tell me about the wire."
---
Tavish told him.
He told it standing on the sand, with the men on the fence listening and the sun climbing slowly over the eastern cape, and his voice did not rise once. He said the wire was not a single fence but three, each one with its own pulse-lock and its own bank of machines, and that the locks answered to the Lord's own heartbeat, so that when the Lord was in the top room, every machine on the island was listening to him. He said the men on the lower wire had been conscripted from the coastal villages a decade ago, when the Lord's first ships went out to the mainland for timber, and that most of them had never fired a rifle at anything but birds. He said the men on the stair were the Lord's personal guard, and that they were not frightened of the sea or the wire or the boats, but of their master, who had once had a guard whipped to death in front of the others for failing to close a door.
"And the giant?" asked Lakshman, from the bow.
Tavish's eyes flicked to him, then away.
"Kumbh," he said. "My brother's elder. The one who was born first, and whom my father put in charge of the stair because nobody else could carry the keys without bending. He has six iron arms, grafted to his shoulders in the Board's clinics before the stripping began. He doesn't speak much. He doesn't need to. He lives on the lower keep, behind the second gate, and he is waiting for someone to tell him whom to kill."
"And Megh?" said Ram.
Tavish's jaw tightened.
"My brother's son," he said. "He's clever. He keeps the ledgers. He can calculate the tide-tables and the engine-loads in his head and he never once makes a mistake. He hates the sea, and he hates his father, and he hates the fact that he's been told he will never be the Lord because his father thinks the boy's too much like his uncle." He glanced at Ram. "He'll be waiting at the causeway. He knows you're on the sand. He's counted your boats."
"Of course he has," said Ram quietly.
---
The causeway ran from the beach to the foot of the keep, a mile of raised stone that crossed the lagoon in a long low curve, and at its midpoint, where the old sea-wall had once been, there was nothing at all.
The stone had been blown out. Ram could see the raw edges from the boats, jagged and black where the explosives had torn through the causeway's span and left a gap twenty paces wide, with the grey lagoon water sliding through it in a steady sluggish current. On the far side the causeway picked up again and ran on to the keep's lower gate, a squat iron-barred tower that stood at the water's edge like a fist.
"The Lord did that," said Tavish, beside him on the sand. "Last winter. When the coastal men first started talking about coming to the wire. He said if the island couldn't be reached by boat, nobody could reach it at all."
"And how do we get across?"
"You don't," said Tavish. "Not by boat. The lagoon's too deep at the gap, and the current will drag you under the wire if you try to swim it. You'd need an engine to pull you across, or a rope a mile long, or a bridge."
"A bridge of what?"
Tavish laughed. It was not a happy sound. "Stone. Iron. Whatever's lying around. My brother never built a single thing that didn't come out of a salvage yard."
Nalu came down the causeway from the beach, at a steady limping walk, with his hands in the pockets of a grey coverall and a battered canvas bag over one shoulder. He was a thin stooped man with grey hair cropped close to his skull, and he had the careful, wary face of someone who has spent a long time being told what to do by people who did not understand what they were ordering. He had come out of the coastal conscript barracks two days before, when the boats first came in sight, and he had asked for nothing except paper and a pencil. Ram had given him both.
Now he stopped beside the boats, and looked at the gap in the causeway for a long time with his head tilted, and then he said, without any preamble at all:
"The old freight rails."
"What about them?" asked Ram.
"They're still in the salvage yard behind the coastal barracks. Two hundred yards of them. The Board laid them to bring the timber down from the forest, back before the Lord started shipping through the lagoon. They're heavy. Good steel. Nobody's touched them in ten years because the Lord thought the scrap wasn't worth the labour." Nalu's eyes narrowed. "And there's a stripping rig in the yard too, the big one, the tracked one with the arm. I saw it when they marched me past. It's not dead. Its hydraulics still hold pressure. If we can get it to the causeway and rig its arm across the gap, and lay the rails on top, we can make a span."
"How long?"
"Two days. Three if the men are slow."
"And the men?"
Nalu's mouth tightened. "They'll be slow," he said. "They're conscripts. They don't like working for the Lord, and they don't like working for anyone else either. They like it even less when somebody with a face they recognise tells them to do it."
Ram looked at the gap in the causeway, and the grey lagoon sliding through it, and the squat iron tower on the far side with its bars and its silent guards. He thought of the engines on the terrace, and the pulse that could have turned them in a single breath toward the keep. He thought of the Lord's machines on the wire, and the men who had been told to close the door.
"Then we ask them," he said.
Nalu stared at him.
"Ask them?" he said.
"Yes," said Ram. "Tell them the bridge is theirs. Tell them the island is theirs. Tell them that nobody is going to be whipped for holding a spanner."
"They won't believe you."
"Then I'll tell them again," said Ram, "and I'll tell them with my hands."
---
They did not believe him. Not at first.
The coastal conscripts came down to the salvage yard in ragged twos and threes, staring at the boats and the strangers and the prince with the closed face who had walked out onto the sand without a weapon. They stood at the edge of the yard in their grey coveralls and watched the stripping rig, and the rails, and the long low mass of the causeway, and nobody moved toward any of it. The Lord's guards had been left on the wire. There was no one to make them work. They only stood, and waited, and watched Ram walk across the yard with his sleeves rolled up and a crowbar in his hands, and set the crowbar against the first of the rails, and begin to lever it up off the ground.
It was heavy. It was the weight of a small boat. He felt it bite into his shoulders and his back and the long tendons of his arms, and he felt his feet slide in the wet gravel, and he kept pushing.
Nobody helped him.
He pushed until the rail came up a hand's breadth off the stones, and then he stopped, and held it there, shaking, while the sweat ran down his face in streams.
"Hold this," he said, to nobody in particular.
A man stepped forward from the crowd. He was a broad-shouldered fellow with a shaved head and a burn scar across one cheek, and he did not say anything. He only came and took the other end of the crowbar in his hands, and set his feet, and pushed.
The rail rose another foot.
Then a second man came forward, and a third. Then the boy with the cracked voice, who had come down the beach with the others and had been standing at the back of the crowd all morning, looking at the water. Then an old man with a limp, who had been a crane-operator once and knew how a load should be balanced. Then twenty of them, then forty, until the whole crowd had surged forward out of the shadows of the barracks and into the yard, and the rail was in the air, swinging gently between their hands, and no one was shouting any more, and the only sound was the scrape of boots on gravel and the hard breathing of men who had not expected to be asked.
They laid the rail on the wooden trolley that Nalu had rigged at the water's edge. They pulled it out along the causeway in a long grunting line, a hundred men with their shoulders bent and their faces set. They did not stop once to rest.
By dusk they had carried twelve rails to the gap.
By the second dawn the stripping rig stood at the end of the causeway with its great steel arm stretched out over the lagoon like the neck of a heron, and its hydraulic hoses had been bled and refilled and cut and welded together from the parts of three other rigs, and its cab was full of the coastal men's coveralls, rolled up and tucked under the seat for a pillow. Nalu sat in the driver's chair with his hands on the controls and his face grey with exhaustion, and when he pulled the lever, the arm groaned, and lifted, and swung slowly out across the water until its steel tip hung directly over the centre of the gap.
"Now," said Nalu.
The men came forward. They carried the rails out along the arm, hand over hand, lashing each one to the steel with heavy iron chain, and the span began to grow across the lagoon, a long dark ribbon of metal laid over the grey water like the spine of a sleeping animal.
Ram stood on the causeway beside Lakshman and watched them work. He did not give orders. He did not need to. Each man knew what he was doing, and each man was doing it with a grim and terrible patience, the patience of people who had been ordered to do a thing for too long and had finally been allowed to do it for themselves.
"They're building it," said Lakshman quietly.
"Yes."
"Not for you."
"No," said Ram. "For the island. They'll walk across it when it's done and they'll never once have to look back at the wire."
Lakshman was silent for a long moment. Then he said, very low, "You know this is the thing that'll get you killed, don't you. Not the machines. Not the Lord. Not even the giant. Just this. Standing here and letting people decide for themselves."
"I know."
"Good," said Lakshman. "Then I'll stand next to you while it happens."
---
Kumbh came down the far side of the keep at noon on the third day, and the bridge was still two rails short.
Ram heard him before he saw him. It was a sound like the stripping rig's hydraulics, only deeper and more irregular, a metallic clank and grind that came rolling across the water from the iron gate of the lower keep, each step a heavy ringing blow on the stone. Then the gate opened. And out of its shadow, slowly, stooping a little under the low arch, came the largest man Ram had ever seen.
He was taller than the stripping rig's cab. He was built like a cliff. His body was bare to the waist, grey-skinned and streaked with old scars, and from his shoulders sprouted six long arms of dark iron, each one jointed at the elbow and the wrist and articulated like the legs of a spider, each ending in a hand that was not a hand at all but a steel claw as broad as a shovel. The arms moved independently. They rose and fell and turned in slow, deliberate, unhurried motions, and the iron joints clanked with every movement.
His face was calm. It was an ordinary face, broad and heavy and faintly bored, the face of a man who has been asked to stand in one place for a very long time and has come to find the waiting almost restful.
He looked at the bridge. He looked at the men on it. He looked, last of all, at Ram.
"Nobody told me to stop you," he said. His voice was slow and deep and oddly soft.
"No," said Ram.
"Then I'll stop you myself."
Kumbh came down the causeway at a walk. His iron arms swung in a loose rhythmic cadence, and the men on the bridge began to scatter, shouting, dropping their chains and their spanners and running for the shore. Nalu had already jumped down from the rig's cab and was running too. Only Ram and Lakshman stood their ground, and Hanu, who had come back from his second trip across the reef two days before and had been sitting on the rails all morning, laughing at the men who tried to tell him how to balance a load, and who now stood up slowly with the coil of fine wire at his waist and his eyes narrowing with a look Ram had not seen before.
"Brother," said Hanu, quietly. "That one's built to crush things. He doesn't know how to stop."
"Then we'll teach him," said Ram.
He unslung the bow. It was his own, the old yew bow from the Chamber of Oaths, strung with sinew and worn smooth at the grip. He nocked an arrow and drew, slowly, and felt the old familiar stretch of the string against his cheek. The count came. It was quick and clean, the way it had been on the hill of Rishyamuk, before the shot that was not his.
Four, two, six.
He loosed.
The arrow did not strike Kumbh's body. It struck the iron joint of the nearest arm, at the elbow, where the metal was thinnest, and it burst through the housing in a shower of sparks and dark fluid. The arm jerked. It locked at an angle, half-raised, its claw hanging loose and twitching, and the giant made a low grinding sound of surprise and looked down at it.
"Again," said Lakshman, beside him, quietly.
Ram drew. The second arrow took the opposite arm at the wrist, and the claw went limp.
Kumbh stopped. He stood on the causeway with his two damaged arms hanging useless and his four remaining arms raised slowly, and for the first time his calm face showed something other than boredom. It was not pain. It was puzzlement, the puzzlement of a man who had been told he was invincible and was now discovering the exact dimensions of the lie.
"You're not doing it right," he said slowly.
"No," said Ram. "I'm not."
Kumbh came on again.
He came faster now, his four good arms swinging wide, the iron claws biting into the causeway's stone and tearing great chunks from its surface as he lurched forward. Ram loosed again. The arrow took a knee-joint. The giant stumbled, and went down on one hand, and his four arms scrabbled for purchase on the rails, and Lakshman's bow sang beside him and a third arrow took the last hydraulic line under the giant's left shoulder. A fountain of hot oil sprayed across the bridge.
Kumbh roared.
It was a sound that rolled across the lagoon and shook the water into little grey peaks. He heaved himself up, all six arms braced against the causeway, and for a moment he was almost upright again, the great grey bulk of him swaying above the rails like a tree in a gale.
"Now," said Nalu's voice, from the rig's cab, very loud.
The stripping rig's arm moved.
It swung down out of the sky with a sound like the end of the world. The steel tip, the one that had been holding the span across the gap, came crashing down onto the causeway in a long flat arc, and the whole bridge shuddered and rang like a bell. The arm's hydraulic ram slammed down on Kumbh's shoulders, on the still-living iron of his upper arms and the grey-scarred back beneath them, and drove him down onto the rails with a crushing, grinding, metallic roar that went on and on and on until the rails themselves began to buckle.
Then it stopped.
The rig's arm held its position. Its ram quivered with the strain. Beneath the steel, Kumbh lay sprawled across the bridge with his limbs twisted and his face turned sideways against the rail, and his eyes were open, and they were looking at Ram with the same mild, faintly puzzled calm that had been on his face at the start.
"That's not fair," he said quietly. Blood came from his mouth in a thin bright line.
"No," said Ram. "It isn't."
"I was only standing here."
"I know," said Ram. "That's why I didn't want you to die standing."
Kumbh's face softened, very slightly. He closed his eyes.
The arm lifted. The giant's body did not move again. Ram went down the causeway toward it, slowly, over the scattered rails and the spilled oil and the torn chunks of stone, and when he reached the body he knelt down beside the great grey head and laid his hand, very gently, on the broad forehead, the way a man might touch a sleeping animal that has been hurt by a falling tree.
Behind him the men on the bridge had stopped moving. They stood frozen at the edges of the causeway, watching, and nobody spoke.
"Get the rails," said Ram, without turning round. "We've got two left to lay."
Nalu's voice came from the cab, thin and shaking. "My lord—"
"Get the rails," said Ram again. "And bring the men back. He was the one they were afraid of. Now they can be afraid of something else."

Shadow I: The Terrace at Dawn
> \*\*\[SHADOW ROAD]\*\* \*The terrace at dawn, and the man at the wall.\*
The strike came down on the island at first light, and Ram did not need the feeds to tell him that it had worked.
He could see it from the terrace. He stood at the top of the salt slope with the staff officers at his back and the flatbeds lined up along the water's edge, their black barrels still smoking faintly from the night's firing, and he watched the island burn. The lower towers were gone. Their glass had shattered outward in sheets, and the stone beneath had cracked and slumped, and a column of black smoke rose out of the centre of the rock and spread across the sky in a long choking pall that blotted out the sun. Along the shore the wire lay in ruins. The machines behind it were silent, their barrels twisted and split. Nothing moved on the island's eastern face except flame.
But the tallest tower still stood.
It was scorched and streaked with soot, and its upper windows were black, but its walls had not come down. The engines had not reached it. Ram had made sure of that. He had given the order to hold the barrels at the lower shafts, to take the wire and the stair and the outer keep, and to leave the top room standing, because he had wanted the Lord to come out alive.
Now a figure came down the stair.
It came slowly, unsteadily, through the smoke. It was a tall man in a long torn coat, with his left arm hanging broken at his side and his face black with soot. He was missing one shoe. His hair had been burned away over the right side of his skull, and the scalp beneath was raw and blistered. But he walked with a kind of terrible deliberation, one step after another, and when he reached the foot of the stair and stepped out onto the scorched stone of the courtyard, he lifted his good hand and turned his face toward the terrace, toward the line of engines and the grey figure standing at its head.
Ram did not move.
The Lord of the southern island stared up at him through the smoke. His eyes were red-rimmed and bloodshot, and there was no fear in them. There was only a cold, exhausted, bitter contempt, the look of a man who has spent ten years building a machine to protect himself and has just watched it turn round and eat him.
"Ram," said Vashkar. His voice was hoarse, but it carried. "I can hear you breathing. You always were a loud breather. It's the only thing the feeds never caught."
Ram said nothing.
"You burned my island," said Vashkar.
"I didn't burn it. The engines did."
"You gave the order."
"Yes."
"Then you're the same as me," said Vashkar softly, and smiled, a terrible crooked thing that did not reach his eyes. "They tell me your brother died in a ravine under your engines. Four hundred of his men, the feeds said. I heard it on the wire and I laughed. I thought, here's a man who finally understands what the machines are for. And then I found myself wondering who in the three worlds would ever have the courage to tell you what you were."
"No one," said Ram quietly.
"No?" Vashkar laughed. It was a broken, bitter, wheezing sound. "Then you're going to be very lonely, aren't you, Returning Prince. Standing on your little terrace. Pointing your guns at a dead man's tower. Counting."
He took a step forward, out of the courtyard and onto the scorched slope.
The staff officers moved. The nearest engine's crew-chief shouted and reached for the panel. Ram lifted one hand, very slightly, and the crew-chief froze.
"Let him come," said Ram.
Vashkar came. He came down the slope with his broken arm swinging and his bare feet blistered on the hot stone, step by painful step, until he stood a few paces from Ram at the foot of the terrace. Up close his face was grey beneath the soot, and the lines round his mouth were deep and white, and his eyes were the eyes of a man who had been told for ten years that he was a god and had only just discovered the truth of it.
"You'll have to do it yourself," he said. "I'm not going to give you the satisfaction of watching me fall. Not from the tower. Not from the engines. Not even from the sea."
"I wasn't going to ask you to fall," said Ram.
"Then what were you going to do?"
Ram did not answer.
Behind him, on the terrace, a new voice spoke. It was flat and dry and rang with the particular pleasure of a man who has been waiting a long time to be asked a question he can answer.
"He's going to do what the house requires," said the voice. "Aren't you, my lord?"
Ram turned his head.
The man who stood at the head of the staff was thin and pale, in a long black coat with silver at the shoulders, and he held a leather folder in his hands. His face was familiar. Ram had seen it in the Board's towers, in the Chamber of Oaths, in the glass room at the top of the stair, where he had stood with his hands folded and his voice pleasant and even, offering the north its own kind of justice.
"Harit," said Ram.
"My lord." Harit bowed very slightly. "I came south with the second wave. The Board's offices have been reorganised. The lower ward is quiet now. The stripping has stopped everywhere, as you asked. But the people are still asking who gave the order, and the feeds need someone to point at. I thought you'd want me to bring you the ledger of the island's dead, so you could read the names over the dawn."
He held out the folder.
Ram did not take it.
"You should have stayed in the north," he said.
"I was there," said Harit calmly. "I was there all along. Every time you looked at the glass, I was on the other side of it, waiting for you to put your hand down. You never did. So I came to see what you would do when someone else put their hand on it for you." He smiled. It was not an unfriendly smile. "This is what you do, my lord. You let the men on the terrace decide what the king's justice should look like. And then you stand back and pretend it wasn't you who asked."
Ram looked at the folder in Harit's hand. He looked at the Lord of the southern island, broken and burned and waiting at the foot of the slope. He looked at the black smoke rising from the tower, and the silent engines, and the staff officers standing frozen along the terrace with their faces turned toward him, waiting for a sign.
He could feel the pulse in his wrist. It was steady. It always was now.
"Take him," he said, very quietly, "to the wall."
---
They took Vashkar to the sea-wall at the end of the terrace, where the salt-crust ran down into the water in a jagged black ledge. The engines' crews formed a half-circle around him in the grey dawn light, rifles raised, faces blank. He stood between them with his broken arm bound to his side and his bare feet on the cold stone, and he did not struggle. He only looked out at the sea, very calmly, the way a man looks out at a horizon he has been expecting all his life.
Ram walked to the wall.
He did not draw the bow. He did not carry a weapon of any kind. He walked across the scorched stone with his hands at his sides, past the staff officers and the flatbeds and the long black line of barrels, and he stopped an arm's length from the island's lord, and looked into his face.
"Say something," said Vashkar softly.
"No," said Ram.
"Coward."
"Maybe."
"You won't even look at me while you do it."
"I'm looking at you now," said Ram.
Vashkar's mouth twisted. Then, very slowly, he lifted his good hand, and held it out, palm up, toward the sea.
"There," he said. "Feel that? The water. It's cold. It's always cold. It doesn't care who's standing on the wall. It never did." He let his hand fall. "Go on, then, Returning Prince. Be what they've been telling everyone you are."
Ram stepped forward.
He did not hurry. He did not hesitate. He took the Lord of the island by the shoulder with his right hand, and turned him gently toward the edge of the wall, the way a man turns a child toward a view, and he said, in a voice so low that only the two of them could hear:
"I'm not doing this for the feeds."
Then he lifted his left hand, and laid it flat against the back of Vashkar's skull, and pushed.
The Lord of the southern island went over the wall without a sound. He did not scream. He fell, arms flailing, his broken limb swinging, into the grey cold water below, and the sea closed over him in a single soft hissing splash.
Nobody moved.
On the terrace, Harit had lowered his folder. His face was pale and utterly still.
"You didn't have to do that," he whispered.
"No," said Ram, without turning. "I didn't."
He stood at the edge of the wall and looked down at the water. It was calm. It was already smoothing over, the grey ripples spreading outward from the place where the body had gone under, and then fading, one by one, until the surface was flat and empty again, reflecting the first pale light of the morning.
Behind him, somebody began to clap.
It was one pair of hands, then two, then a third. The staff officers were watching him. Their faces were white and their eyes were wide, and some of them were nodding, very slowly, as though they had just been handed the answer to a question they had been asking for years. The clapping spread along the line of engines, ragged and uncertain, and then grew, until the whole terrace was thundering with the sound.
Ram did not turn round.
He stood with his back to them and his hand still outstretched over the water, and he listened to the clapping rise and roll across the salt flats and out over the sea, and he thought, very clearly, They wanted this.
He thought, They are happy.
He thought, This is what the glass is for.
He lowered his hand. He did not look at it.
---
They brought Tavish out at noon.
He had been held in the old guardhouse at the foot of the terrace since the night before, with a pair of officers at the door and a bowl of cold rice on the floor beside him, which he had not touched. He came out blinking into the bright sun with his hands bound in front of him and his grey coat torn at the shoulder. His face was pale. He did not look at the sea.
He looked at Ram.
"You're going to kill me," he said. His voice was flat and resigned. "I knew you would. Harit told me on the way up from the cell. He said the house can't afford to keep a man who once offered it the keys and then tried to make a deal with the wire."
"Harit was wrong," said Ram.
Tavish's eyes narrowed.
"Then why am I in chains?"
"Because the men of the terrace need to see that the house is not afraid of its own shadow," said Ram quietly. "And because I'm tired of being the one who decides whether a man is allowed to walk out of a room."
He turned away. He walked back up the slope toward the engines, toward the long black line of barrels and the silent staff and the cold grey gleam of the sea.
Behind him, Tavish shouted something. It was not a plea. It was a single hoarse word, torn out of him by a terror so raw and total that the officers at his back flinched and stepped away.
Ram did not look back.
He heard the shot a moment later. It was a single flat crack, clean and final, and it rolled across the salt flats and out over the water and faded into the wind. He did not count it. He did not need to. The number was already written, in the same clean modern script as the feeds, on a grey folder in the hand of a man named Harit, who stood at the top of the terrace with his leather case under one arm and his face very calm and very satisfied, watching the prince of Kosala walk away from the sea.
The sun was high now. It fell across the terrace in long bright bars, and the black barrels of the engines gleamed like the backs of sleeping beasts, and somewhere far to the north, in a glass room at the top of a spire that had been painted gold for the occasion, a new face appeared on every screen in the city, smiling down over the crowds at the foot of the towers.
The caption beneath it was short. It was written in the Board's pale modern script.
THE MASTER OF THE SOUTHERN SEA.
The chant rose up from the square, and went on, and on.
Ram did not hear it. He was standing alone at the top of the salt slope with the sun on his neck and his right hand held loosely at his side. He was looking at it. He could not stop looking at it. It was a perfectly ordinary hand, dry and calloused and faintly stained with salt. It did not shake. It had never once shaken since the morning he came down the north rampart with the ash in his lungs.
It would not stop. It would never stop. Somewhere, a pulse would always be waiting for it to lift.
He closed his fingers into a fist, very slowly, and then opened them again.
The pulse went on counting.
---
CHAPTER II: The Bridge of Iron
> \*\*\[THE ROAD TAKEN]\*\*
The bridge was finished on the fourth day, at dusk, and the first man to walk across it was the old crane-operator with the limp.
Nobody had asked him to. He simply got up from the bench in front of the coastal barracks, where he had been sitting all afternoon with his leg stretched out in front of him and his face turned toward the lagoon, and he walked down the causeway, slowly, dragging his bad foot over the iron rails. The men watched him go. Nobody called out. He reached the centre of the span, over the gap where the stone had been blown away, and stopped there with the grey water sliding beneath him and the squat iron tower of the lower keep rising in front of him across the lagoon. He stood looking at it for a long time.
Then he took one step forward, onto the far side of the bridge, and kept going.
The men followed him. They did not run. They walked in ragged twos and threes, slowly, with their faces grave and their hands at their sides, and as they crossed the span their boots rang on the steel, a steady deep sound like a heartbeat, and the sound rolled out over the water and back off the walls of the keep.
Ram stood at the near end of the bridge with his hand on the cold iron of the rail and watched them go.
Nalu came and stood beside him. The engineer's face was grey with fatigue and something else that might have been wonder.
"They didn't need you to do that," he said.
"No."
"They could have walked across without the speech. Without the bridge. Without the Lord's guard on the wire being shot to bits. They could have walked across any time in the last ten years."
"They couldn't have walked across alone," said Ram. "That's the difference."
Nalu was quiet for a while. Then he said, very low, "The Board never asked us that. Not once. Not in all the years I worked on the causeway. They just told us where to put the stone."
"I know."
"Do you?"
"I've been told the same thing by a great many people," said Ram. "Mostly by people who were right."
---
The keep was another matter.
The iron gate at its foot had been shut since the morning Kumbh died, and the men who had been holding the lower tower's walls since then had not come out. They watched from the arrow-slits and the parapet, faces pale and hard in the shadows, and when the bridge's first rails came within sight of the gate, a rifle cracked from one of the upper slits, and the men on the causeway ducked and scattered.
Ram did not wait for orders. He went out onto the span alone.
He walked slowly, over the scorched rails and the chain-lashed joints, and when he was halfway across, a second rifle fired. The bullet struck the steel an arm's length from his boot and went whining off over the lagoon. He did not stop. He kept walking, with his hands at his sides and the sun on his face, until he stood at the very end of the bridge, below the gate, with the iron bars rising above him and the faces in the slits staring down.
"I'm not here to take the tower," he said.
Nobody answered. The silence on the wall was total.
"I'm here to ask the men inside to come out," said Ram. "Not to surrender. Not to be whipped. Just to come out, and walk across the bridge, and go home."
A figure appeared on the parapet above the gate. It was young. It was lean and straight-backed and dressed in a clean grey coverall, with the sleeves neatly rolled to the elbow and a long-barrelled rifle held easily across one arm. Its face was narrow and fine-boned, very pale, with dark intelligent eyes and a mouth that was set in an expression of cold, quick, almost bored amusement.
Megh.
"You're not a very good negotiator," said the young man. His voice was clear and carried easily. "My father always said you'd talk until someone was obliged to shoot you, just to stop the noise. He was right, I see."
"Your father is dead," said Ram.
"Yes. I heard. The whole island heard. They say you pushed him off the wall with your own hand." Megh's mouth curved. "I'd have done the same, I expect. But I'd have enjoyed it more."
"Come down," said Ram. "Walk across the bridge with the others. You don't have to fight any more."
Megh laughed.
It was a short sharp sound, without any warmth. "Don't I?" he said. He raised the rifle, very slowly, until its muzzle rested on the stone of the parapet, pointed down at the causeway, and his eyes never left Ram's face. "You see, I'm the one who keeps the ledgers. I know exactly how many men you've got on that bridge, and how much iron they've used, and how long it'll take them to walk across. I know the tide. I know the current. I know that the span will hold for exactly as long as the rig's ram is locked, and that the rig's ram will hold for exactly as long as the lagoon stays calm." He smiled. "And I know that if I shoot the man at the end of the bridge, the men on the bridge will turn and run, and the span will be a bridge of nothing but empty rails and dead iron."
"Then shoot me," said Ram. "And see whether the bridge still stands behind me when the men come walking."
For a long moment Megh did not move.
Then the rifle's muzzle dipped. Not much. Only an inch. And in that inch, Ram saw something flicker across the young man's face, a crack, a fault, the look of someone who has calculated every variable in the equation and has just realised that one of them is a human being who does not care what the calculation says.
"You're mad," said Megh softly.
"Maybe."
"My father wasn't mad. He was ambitious. He wanted the keys, and the engines, and a throne in a glass room at the top of the world. I understood him. I even admired him." Megh's voice was very quiet now. "But you don't want anything at all, do you? That's what frightens people like me. You just keep standing in the middle of the road, asking them to get out of the way."
"I want them to go home," said Ram.
"And where's home, for a coastal conscript who's never seen anything but the wire?"
"I don't know," said Ram. "But I'll help them look."
Megh's face twisted. Something raw and furious rose up through the cold mask of it, and his hand tightened on the rifle, and for one long terrible heartbeat Ram thought he would fire anyway, out of sheer contempt for the thing he could not understand.
Then, from the bridge behind Ram, a voice spoke. It was calm and clear and slightly bored.
"Megh," said Lakshman. "You've been counting too long."
Ram did not turn round. He heard the bow string creak, and the faint sharp hiss of an arrow being drawn back to the cheek. He heard his brother's breath go out, slow and steady, and felt the air change around them both.
Megh looked past Ram's shoulder at the man on the bridge with the black bow raised, and his face went very still.
"You're the one who cut the Mistress's face," he said.
"Yes," said Lakshman.
"I heard you did it with a knife. A toll-man's blade, not a warrior's. Did you enjoy it?"
"No," said Lakshman. "I've been sorry for it every day since. But I'd do it again, if she came for the furrow-girl with a blade."
Megh's mouth tightened. He looked at the rifle in his hands, and then at the bridge below, and then at the faces on the wall around him, one by one. Some of them were already turning away. Some of them were lowering their weapons. A few had begun to drift back from the parapet, quietly, toward the stairs.
"Come down," said Lakshman, very gently. "There's nothing left in this tower but the dead. The men know it. You know it. Your father's ledgers are in the river, and the wire's in pieces on the shore, and there's no one at the top of the stair any more to tell you what to count."
Megh was silent for a long time.
Then, slowly, he lifted the rifle off the parapet and held it out, muzzle down, over the edge of the wall, and let it fall. It clattered on the stone below and slid off the end of the bridge and disappeared into the grey water with a single small splash.
"Fine," said Megh. His voice was hoarse. "Fine. You win."
"No," said Ram quietly, from the end of the bridge. "Nobody won. Come down."
---
Megh came down.
He came through the gate alone, walking slowly, with his hands held out at his sides and his face white and set. He did not look at the bridge. He looked at the water.
He stopped in front of Ram at the end of the span, and for a long moment neither of them spoke. Then Megh raised his eyes, and Ram saw in them not fear, not hatred, but a dark, bewildered grief, the look of a man who has been told that the rules he lived by were only a story for children.
"I'm supposed to hate you," said Megh quietly.
"You don't have to."
"I don't know how not to." He swallowed. "My uncle, my father, the Mistress at the ford. They taught me to count. They taught me that the world was a set of numbers and the numbers never lied, and the only way to survive was to make sure you were always on the side of the sum. And now you've walked into my tower and given the sum away to a crowd of coastal conscripts, and I don't know what I'm supposed to be any more."
"Then don't be anything," said Ram. "For a while. Just walk across the bridge."
Megh looked at the iron span stretching away across the lagoon, its rails gleaming red in the last of the sun, the men on it moving steadily toward the shore.
"Will they let me?" he asked.
"They'll let you," said Ram. "Some of them will look at you like a ghost. Some of them will spit. And then one of them will offer you a cup of water, because he's tired and it's hot, and you'll have to decide what to do with that."
Megh let out a short, shaky breath. Then he turned and walked out onto the bridge, slowly, alone, and the men on the span parted to let him through, and nobody spoke, and nobody touched him, and by the time he had reached the far end and was walking up the causeway toward the barracks, a figure in a coverall had come down from the crowd, a thin young man with a burn scar across his wrist, and was holding out a battered tin cup in both hands.
Megh stopped.
He stood very still for a long time, staring at the cup. Then he took it.
---
Lakshman came down from the bridge with his bow across his shoulders and his face streaked with dust and the beginnings of a smile.
"That was ugly," he said.
"It was necessary."
"It was ugly and necessary, which is the worst combination in the world." He stopped beside his brother and looked at the keep, the squat grey tower with its open gate and its empty slits, and his smile faded a little. "They'll talk about this for a long time. The Lord's son walking out with a cup in his hands."
"Let them."
"You don't mind?"
"I mind a great many things," said Ram. "But I don't mind that."
Lakshman was quiet for a while. The sun was sinking now, behind the cape, and the lagoon had turned the colour of old bronze, and the far end of the bridge was thronged with the coastal men, sitting on the rails in small grey clusters, passing bowls and cups and bits of bread from hand to hand.
"Sing something," said Ram, suddenly.
Lakshman looked at him in surprise.
"You said you'd been singing since the ford," said Ram. "I'd like to hear it."
"I don't sing."
"You've been humming under your breath for a week. I've heard you at the rig when you thought nobody was listening. It's the tune she used to hum in the vault." Ram did not look at his brother. "Hanu says it's the same one. The one she sings from the top of the tower."
Lakshman was silent for a long time. Then, very low, almost under his breath, he began to hum.
It was not much of a tune. It was rough and tuneless and broke off in the middle of phrases, and his voice was not steady. But it rose out over the lagoon in the dusk, thin and clear and strangely sweet, and as it rose, the men on the bridge turned their heads toward it, one by one, and fell quiet.
Ram stood with his brother on the stone causeway, in the fading light, and listened.
Somewhere far above them, at the top of the tallest tower on the island, a single lamp burned behind a grille. And from it, very faint, on the cool evening air, came the answer: a woman's voice, low and steady, taking up the tune where Lakshman had left it, and carrying it on, out over the water, toward the men on the bridge.
Lakshman closed his eyes.
"Four, two, six," he said, very softly.
"Four, two, six," said Ram.
They stood there together until the last of the sun had gone, and the lagoon turned black, and the song went on rising from the tower in the dark, not loud, not quite steady, but never once stopping.
---
CHAPTER III: The Fire-Court
> \*\*\[THE ROAD TAKEN]\*\*
The island had never had a court. It had only had the Lord.
Tavish told them that on the morning after the bridge, sitting on a stool in the shade of the coastal barracks with a bowl of rice on his knees that he had not yet touched. He had been released from the guard-room the evening before, when the lower wire was finally cut and the last of the Lord's officers had put down their rifles on the sand. His wrists were still raw from the cords. He had asked for nothing, and no one had offered him anything, and now he sat with his shoulders bent and his voice low, the way a man speaks when he has been alone with a thing for a long time and is only now finding someone who will listen.
"The Lord judged everything," he said. "Every theft, every debt, every quarrel between the men on the wire and the women in the kitchens. He sat on the stone in the square with his arms folded and he looked at you, and whatever he decided was the law. There were no books. There was only the stone, and the furnace-grating beside it, and the men who stood on the grating when the Lord said they had lied." He glanced up. "My brother liked the grating. He said it was the only honest thing on the island. You either walked across it, or you didn't."
"And the men who didn't?" asked Ram.
"Didn't come back." Tavish looked at the rice. "That's what the grating was for."
Nalu, who had been sitting on the step behind them with a clipboard across his knees and a stub of pencil between his teeth, took the pencil out of his mouth.
"There's no grating in the Board's law," he said. "Or in the old house law, as far as I've read it."
"No," said Tavish. "It's older than both. It's older than the island. My brother's grandfather brought it from the mainland when they were still building the first towers, and he said it was the way the gods separated the honest from the rest. He said the fire was just a fire. It was the walking that told you who was lying."
Ram said nothing. He was watching the square.
It was filling. The coastal men had been coming up from the causeway since dawn, in their grey coveralls, with their children on their hips and their wives holding their hands, and they stood now in a loose crowd around the old stone dais at the centre of the keep's courtyard, and they were not talking. They were watching the grating.
It lay at the foot of the dais, a rectangle of black iron bars set into the flagstones, perhaps twice the length of a man, with a shallow pit beneath it and a row of soot-stained vents along its sides. Someone had lit a fire in the pit an hour before. Now the bars were glowing faintly, dull red at the edges, and the air above them shimmered in a thin wavering haze that made the crowd look as though it were standing under water.
On the stone dais, under a canopy of old grey cloth that had been strung between two pillars, sat six men. They were the surviving officers of the Lord's household. Some of them had been on the stair when the tower was taken. Some had come down from the lower wards with their hands raised. They had asked for a court, and Ram had given them one, because he had not known how to refuse. He had only asked that Tavish sit at the centre of it, and that the coastal men be allowed to speak.
The eldest of the officers rose. He was a thin grey man with a ledger in his hand and a face like old leather. He did not look at Ram. He looked at the crowd.
"The Lord is dead," he said. "The Lord kept a woman in the top room. She was not his wife. She was a prisoner from the mainland, a furrow-girl who came out of a boat under the gold filigree. She was never once allowed to leave the tower. But the island has heard her singing for two months, and the island has heard the men on the wall say that she sang to the Lord every night, and the men on the wall are not always honest." He turned the ledger in his hands. "The law of the island is old. It asks for a walking. The woman who lived in the Lord's tower must cross the grating, and if she is honest, the fire will not touch her. We ask for the walking now."
A murmur went through the crowd. It was not a cheer. It was closer to the sound of a breath being drawn in by a great many people at once.
Ram stood up.
He was standing at the edge of the dais, below the canopy, and he did not move any closer to the grating. He looked at the old officer, and then at the crowd, and then, very briefly, at the bars of the grating glowing red in their pit.
"No," he said.
The officer's eyes narrowed. "The law—"
"The law is yours," said Ram quietly. "It isn't mine, and it isn't hers. I won't have her walk on iron to prove a thing about herself that nobody in this square has any right to ask."
A sharp voice came from the back of the crowd.
"Then how will we know?"
It was a woman's voice. Ram turned his head and saw her: a young coastal mother with a child on her hip and a face worn thin by long nights on the wire. She was not angry. She was tired, and frightened, and she was looking at him with a fierce, honest, pleading attention.
"How will we know she didn't lie?" the woman said. "We heard her singing. We know she was up there. We don't know what else she did, or what the Lord made her do, or whether she wanted it. If the grating doesn't tell us, then what will?"
Ram did not answer right away.
He looked at the crowd. He looked at their faces, the wary faces of people who had been told a story about the Lord's tower for ten years and had never once been allowed to see the inside of it. He thought of the fire-court at Siddhashrama, and the children sorting berries under the neem tree. He thought of Kesh's caves, and the ledger of the boats, and the cairn of stones on the summit of Rishyamuk.
He thought: Somebody will always need to see the proof. That's not the same as needing a fire.
"Ask the people who were in the tower," he said.
The old officer blinked. "What?"
"The people who lived with her. The kitchen women. The boy who carried the water. The guard on the top stair who watched the door for two months." Ram looked at the crowd. "Ask them what she did. Ask them whether she ever lied to the Lord, and what it cost her when she didn't. Ask them whether she ever asked anyone on the wire for a single thing that wasn't freedom."
"And if they don't remember?"
"Then we'll ask the walls," said Ram. "They've heard every word."
---
They did not ask the walls. They asked the kitchen women.
The kitchen women came out of the lower keep in a long grey line, twelve of them, with their hands folded in front of their aprons and their eyes on the flagstones. They were old and young, plump and thin, and some of them had been crying. They stood in front of the dais one by one, and one by one they told the court what they had seen.
They said the furrow-girl had come up the stair on the first night, with a guard at either elbow and her hands bound, and had walked straight past the Lord without bowing. They said she had asked for water, and when the Lord laughed at her, she had asked again, very quietly, until the guard brought her a cup. They said she had sat on the edge of her bed in the top room with her back to the door for three days and had not once looked at the Lord when he came in. They said she had sung every night, low, so the men on the wall could hear.
They said she had never once touched him.
One of the women was the oldest of them, a small hunched figure with white hair knotted at the nape. She had served in the top room herself for twenty years, and her voice shook as she spoke, but she did not look away from the officers' canopy.
"The Lord tried to make her eat," she said. "He sent the best food up from the lower kitchens. Fish from the coast, and fruit from the mainland, and sweets from the market at the causeway. She wouldn't touch any of it. She said she'd eat when she was ready, and not before. He had her starved for a week, to see if she'd break. She didn't break. She just sang louder."
"Did she ever try to escape?" asked the young coastal mother from the back of the crowd.
The old woman's mouth worked. "Every day," she said. "She'd stand at the grille and look out at the sea and say the wire was too high and the lagoon was too wide and the tide was too strong. And then she'd sit down and start humming, and she'd say, very quietly, He'll come. Someone will come." She shook her head slowly. "We thought she was mad. We thought she'd lost her mind in the wind. But every night she sang, and every night the men on the wall stopped to listen, and one by one they stopped laughing at her."
The officers were silent. The crowd was silent.
Ram stood at the edge of the dais and felt the silence settle over the courtyard like the thick grey dust of the Janasthan wards, soft and suffocating and complete.
Then Sita came out of the lower keep.
---
She walked alone.
She came across the flagstones from the far doorway with her plait tied up under a plain grey cloth and her feet bare on the cold stone. She wore a loose coarse cotton dress, the kind the kitchen women wore, and her hands were empty. She did not look at the crowd. She did not look at the officers under the canopy, or at Tavish on his stool, or at the old woman who had just finished speaking and was now trembling at the edge of the dais with her hand over her mouth.
She looked at Ram.
It was the first time he had seen her since the morning on the terrace, when she had put two fingers against the inside of his wrist and told him his pulse was steady. Her face was thinner than it had been on the road. There were lines at the corners of her eyes that had not been there at Mithila, and a small pale scar along the line of her jaw, very faint, the kind that might have come from a grille. But her gaze was the same. It was steady and clear and utterly unafraid, and it was fixed on him now with a look that was not quite anger and not quite amusement, the look of someone who has been asked a question she has already answered many times, and is tired of answering it for people who will never listen.
"You didn't want me to do this," she said.
"No," said Ram.
"Why not?"
"Because it's a trick."
"Yes," said Sita. "It is. The Lord used it to frighten his wives. He stood them on the grating and told them the fire would take them if they lied, and he watched their faces while they stood there, and whoever flinched was whipped." She tilted her head. "I learned that from the kitchen women. They told me the first week. They thought it would comfort me to know I'd never have to walk it."
"Then why are you going to?"
Sita smiled. It was a small smile, sharp at the edges, and there was nothing comforting in it at all.
"Because they asked," she said. "And because I'm tired of being the thing that other people argue about in courtyards. I want to walk across the iron, Ram. I want to feel what it's like to stand on the thing the Lord built to keep his women quiet. And then I want to walk off the other side and watch every man in this square realise that the fire was never meant for the one who walks it."
She turned away from him, and walked to the edge of the grating, and stopped.
The bars glowed red in their pit. The heat came up off them in waves that made the air above them shake, and the faces of the crowd were red and shining in the light, and somewhere at the back someone had begun to weep.
Sita lifted one foot.
Ram did not breathe.
She set her bare sole down on the first bar.
For one long moment nothing happened. The iron did not sizzle. There was no smell of burning flesh. She stood with her foot resting on the glowing metal and her eyes closed, very still, the way she had stood at the edge of the Janasthan ford with her hand against the silt, listening to something far below. Then she opened her eyes, and lifted her other foot, and set it down on the next bar.
She walked.
She walked the whole length of the grating, slowly, one step at a time, her grey cotton dress stirring around her ankles, her arms loose at her sides and her head up. Her feet did not blister. Her face did not flinch. The crowd watched in total silence, and Ram saw the faces change as she passed over them, one bar after another, the fear draining out of them and something else coming in, something dazed and wondering and faintly sick, like the face of a man who has been told all his life that a stone was a mountain and has just discovered, in the middle of a field, that it is only a stone.
At the far end of the grating, she stepped off onto the flagstones, and turned round, and looked at the officers under the canopy.
"It's cold," she said.
Her voice was clear and carried across the whole courtyard.
"The fire's on top," she said. "The grating's made of old iron, and the men who built it put a layer of ash over the bars, so the heat comes up slowly and the top is always grey. It's the same trick they use on the Board's furnace-rooms. Anyone who knows where to look can see the cold spot at the centre, where the grate is cracked. I could see it from the first step." She looked at the crowd. "The fire was never going to take me. It was never going to take anyone who knew how to walk. It only ever took the ones who were too frightened to look down."
Nobody spoke.
Then, slowly, from the back of the crowd, the young coastal mother began to laugh.
It was not a happy sound. It was raw and cracked and rang out across the courtyard like the first cry of a child, and when she stopped, her face was wet, and she was looking at the grating with an expression of such bitter, bewildered recognition that Ram had to turn his head away.
The old kitchen woman went down on her knees on the flagstones, at the edge of the dais, and put her forehead against the cold stone, and began to sob.
Sita walked over to her. She knelt down beside her and laid one hand on the old woman's back, very lightly, the way you would touch a horse that had been beaten. She said nothing. She only stayed there, kneeling on the stone, until the sobbing stopped.
Then she stood up, and turned, and looked at Ram across the courtyard, and her face was grave and tired and very clear.
"Now you can stop being angry at me," she said.
"I wasn't angry."
"You were terrified. It looks the same from the outside." She brushed the ash from her dress. "And you were right. It was a trick. But it was the only trick in the whole island that anyone had ever been afraid of. So I thought someone ought to show them what it looked like from the other side."
---
The officers voted. Ram did not watch them do it. He stood at the edge of the dais with his back to the canopy and his eyes on the faces of the crowd, and he heard the murmur of their voices rise and fall behind him, and then the old grey officer's voice, dry and grudging, announcing the result.
"The woman is honest," he said. "The island has seen the walking. The Lord's law is satisfied."
Then Tavish stood up on his stool.
He was pale and thin in the bright courtyard light, with his bound wrists raw at the edges, and when he spoke his voice shook. But it carried.
"I was the Lord's brother," he said. "I was the one who kept the keys. I offered them to a man on the sand, once, in exchange for a throne. He refused. He made me kneel anyway, and then he had me held in a guard-room and taken out to be shot, and he watched it happen with his face like a closed door. I don't know whether he forgave me for the offer. I don't think he cares. But I know what he did when the wire fell, and I know what he did when the men on the terrace began to clap." He drew a breath. "And I know that the island has never once in its history been asked what it wanted. So I'm asking. Who should hold the keys now?"
The crowd was silent.
Then, from somewhere near the back, a voice said: "Not you."
Tavish flinched. Then, very slowly, he nodded.
"No," he said. "Not me. Not my brother, or my nephew, or the men on the stair. I'll say that to the wire tonight, and to the sea." He turned on the stool, and looked for the first time directly at Ram. "But I'll say one more thing. The prince refused the keys, and refused the throne, and refused the stone, and refused the crown. He walked out onto the sand with no weapon and asked the men on the wire to put down their guns. He pushed my brother off the wall, and then he went back to the bridge and asked the coastal men to carry the rails. He's done more with his hands in three days than my brother did with the whole of his machine. And I still don't know what he wants."
He looked at the crowd.
"Does anyone?"
The silence stretched.
Then Lakshman stepped forward out of the crowd, with the black bow across his back and his face set and tired and oddly gentle, and stood beside his brother at the edge of the dais.
"He wants you to decide," said Lakshman. "That's the whole of it. He's been wanting it since the morning he walked out of his father's chamber. He's never once wanted to be the one who said what happens next."
He turned and looked at Ram, and something in his expression softened.
"Which is why it's going to be very hard for him," he said quietly, "to stand on that stool and say he wants to rule anything at all."
---
They built the new council that week, in the square, out of the things that had been left lying around the keep. There were no books and no stone. There was a table made from the Lord's old desk, dragged down from the top room and carried out into the sun, with the drawers still full of ledger-slips and ink. There were fourteen chairs, all different, taken from the barracks and the kitchens and the houses of the officers who had fled or been taken. There were the coastal men, who came to the table one by one and sat down, and argued, and sometimes shouted, and went away again, and came back.
Ram did not sit at the table. He had been asked to. He had said no.
"You'll be the one they listen to," Tavish had said, furious. "You're the face. Everyone knows you. If you don't sit, they'll think you don't care."
"I don't want them to listen to me," Ram had said. "I want them to listen to each other."
It was slow. It was infuriating. The council spent its first two days arguing about the wire, and whether it should be dismantled or kept for the boats, and whether the coastal villages should be paid for the men they had lost, and who would pay, and how. It spent its third day arguing about the kitchens. It spent its fourth arguing about the sea-lanes, which the Lord had closed to every ship but his own for ten years, and which the fishermen wanted open again before the next storm season came.
On the fifth day, the old kitchen woman got up from her bench at the back of the square and walked to the table and sat down in an empty chair, and said nothing at all, and the arguing stopped.
After a while she said, in a small clear voice, "The grating should come up."
Everybody looked at her.
"The iron," she said. "It was built to frighten the women. It's been frightening them for forty years. My daughter used to walk round it on the way to the well, so she wouldn't have to look at it." She folded her hands on the table. "I want it dug up. I want it melted. I want the iron made into hoes for the field-women, who've been working the lagoon edge with their hands since the wire went up. And I want the pit filled with water, so the children can drink from it on the hot days."
Nobody spoke.
Then the young coastal mother, sitting two chairs down, nodded slowly. "Yes," she said. "Yes. That's right."
And somebody else said, "Yes," and then somebody else, and then the whole table was nodding and murmuring and reaching for pencils, and the old woman sat in her chair with her hands folded and her eyes closed, and did not open them again until the work had begun.
Ram watched from the edge of the square. He did not join in. He did not need to. He only stood in the sun with his arms folded and listened to the sound of the council's voices rising and falling over the table, a sound he had not heard in the north for a very long time, and had not known he had been waiting to hear.
It was not the sound of an order. It was the sound of people deciding what they wanted, and then arguing about it, and then deciding again.
It was slow, and it was ugly, and it would never be finished.
He thought it might be the first true thing he had seen in a year.

Shadow II: The Feed
> \*\*\[SHADOW ROAD]\*\* \*The feed, and the voice through the grille.\*
The clerk who brought the bread was called Anand, and he had a stammer that got worse whenever he was frightened, which was always.
He came down the stone stair to the vault every morning at the seventh hour, with a basket of flat grain-cakes on one arm and a clay jug of water in the other hand, and he always stopped at the foot of the stair and stood very still for a moment, the way a man stands at the edge of a cold river before he steps in. Then he walked to the grille. He did not look through it. He set the basket down on the ledge beside the slot and the jug beside the basket, and he said, in the same thin nervous voice every morning, "F-food, my lady," and turned to go.
On the fourteenth morning, he did not turn to go.
He stood at the grille with his hands clasped in front of him and his face very white, and he said, quickly, before he could lose his nerve: "They've m-moved the feeds to the ground floor. The screen in the audit hall. They say you can see everything in the square from there, through the new lens on the spire. I thought you should know."
Sita, on the stone bench behind the grille, went very still.
"Why are you telling me this?" she asked.
Anand swallowed. "B-because I don't think they've ever thought about the grille," he said. "I think they forgot it was there. The lens on the spire looks down at the square, and the grille looks out at the corridor, and the corridor is where I stand every morning with the bread." He glanced over his shoulder, up the stair. "The camera in the corridor was taken out for repairs on the third day of the month. It's been dark ever since. Nobody's noticed. The guards don't come down here much any more. They say it's too cold."
He looked back at the grille, and his face was pale and desperate.
"There's a camera in the square," he said. "It's on the old pole by the fountain. It's still working. I checked it yesterday. It's pointed at the bell-tower, where they hang the men from the lower ward at dusk. And if you spoke very clearly, my lady, toward the corridor, and the corridor was very quiet, the square might hear you. Through the grille. Through the wall." His voice dropped to a whisper. "The feeds might too. If they were listening."
Sita said nothing for a long moment.
Then she stood up, slowly, and walked across the cold flagstones until she stood at the grille, close enough that Anand could see her face through the slot.
"Why would you do this?" she asked quietly.
"Because my daughter is nine," said Anand. "Her name is Meera. She goes to the new school on the Sarayu bank, and she came home last week with a drawing of a man on a rope, and she said it was the prince, and she said he was very brave, because he made the whole square clap." He swallowed. "I don't want her to grow up thinking the clapping was the same thing as being brave."
Sita closed her eyes.
"Thank you," she said.
---
The hanging was set for the dusk bell, in the square, as it had been every evening for a month.
Ram did not go down to watch. He stood on the roof of the east spire in the failing light, with the engines turning slowly on the north rampart below him and the glass of the spire burning gold, and he looked at the screen set into the parapet beside him. It was the new screen, the one the Board's engineers had fitted in the week after the ravine. It showed the square from above, from a camera that hung on the lens-pole over the fountain, and it was very clear. He could see every face in the crowd.
There were perhaps three thousand of them. They stood in tight packed rows on the grey stone, the lower ward's men and women and children, their faces turned up toward the bell-tower where the gallows-pipe had been set. Some of them were chanting. Most of them were silent. A few had brought banners, the old red-and-white of the Board's new order, printed with the face of the Victor of the Western Rise.
The face on the banners was smiling.
At the foot of the tower stood the guards, in black. At the top, on the gallows-platform, stood a man in a grey coverall with his hands bound behind him and a rope round his neck. He was old. His hair was white. Ram could not see his face clearly, but he could see the way the man's shoulders were set, straight and proud, and he thought, for a moment, that the old man was looking straight back at the lens.
The bell began to ring.
It was a single deep note, struck once, and it rolled out over the city and the towers and the grey-blue lagoon of the lower ward, and every screen in the square went silent for a heartbeat.
Then the feeds came on.
Not the feeds from the spire. Not the square's camera. A different one. It came from the corridor below the audit hall, a flat grey wall lit by a single bulb, and in the centre of the wall, pressed up against a heavy iron grille, was a face.
It was a woman's face. It was thin and pale and very calm, with a plait of dark hair tied up under a plain grey cloth, and a small pale scar along one side of the jaw. Ram had seen it a thousand times in his sleep. He had seen it on the bed at Mithila, and on the road from the palace gate, and in the gold glow of the copper roof where it had watched him break the bow.
He stopped breathing.
Behind him, the staff officer on duty stepped forward, very fast, and reached for the screen's controls.
"Don't," said Ram.
The officer froze.
On the screen, Sita's mouth moved. Her voice came through the square's speakers, thin and clear, carried by the old copper cables that ran through the walls of the lower ward. It was not loud. It did not need to be. The square had gone quiet, the way a room goes quiet when someone has walked in who was not expected.
"My name is Sita," she said. "I was found in a furrow. I was kept in a vault. I was asked to sing for the men who stood at the gate, and I did it, because it was the only thing in the whole country that anybody had ever asked me to do without asking for something in return."
The crowd did not move.
"There is a clerk in your audit tower," said Sita. "His name is Mahesh. He signed the stripping permits for the western rise, and then he was taken to the lower ward, and he was hanged from the bell-tower, and his daughter, who is nine, was made to watch from the front of the crowd. Her name is Tara. She draws pictures of the men on ropes. She drew one last week. She said it was a brave man."
On the gallows-platform, the old man in the grey coverall had lifted his head.
"He was not brave," said Sita. "He was a clerk who couldn't read the ledger he signed. And the man who ordered him hanged was not brave either. He was a man who had been given a glass room and a pulse that opened the doors, and who could not bear the thought that anyone in the city might one day refuse to clap."
Somewhere in the crowd, a woman began to cry.
Ram stood at the parapet with his hand on the cold rail, and he did not look away from the screen. He could not. He was watching the faces of the crowd turn, one by one, toward the bell-tower, and then, very slowly, toward the lens on the pole by the fountain.
They were not clapping. They were not chanting. They were looking at the woman on the grille, with her plain grey cloth and her thin white face, and the expression on their faces was not any expression Ram had seen before. It was not fear. It was not joy. It was closer to the look of a man in a dark room who has just heard a voice speak his name, and does not yet know whether it is safe to answer.
"Turn it off," said Ram.
The staff officer's hands hovered over the controls. "My lord—"
"Turn it off."
"They're still listening, my lord. The whole city's—"
"I said turn it off."
The officer turned it off.
The screen went black.
And from the square below, very faint through the thick glass of the spire, Ram heard the sound begin. It was not a chant. It was not a cheer. It was a low, rising, uncertain murmur, the sound of three thousand people beginning, very slowly, to speak to one another.
---
He went down to the vault that night himself.
The corridor was dark. The guards at the top of the stair had been doubled, and they stood aside for him without a word, their faces white. He walked down past them, and past the iron doors of the lower cells, and past the place where the clerk Anand had stood every morning with his basket and his jug, and he did not look at the empty ledge.
The grille was dark.
He stood in front of it for a long time. Then he laid his palm flat against the cold steel of the door, and waited for the panel to wake.
It did not wake.
He pressed harder. He felt the faint hum of the old house-lock under his hand, the familiar pulse that had opened every door in the citadel since the day the stone was carved. It was still there. But it was quiet. It was not reading him. It was waiting for something else.
"You can't cut the square," said a voice behind the grille.
Ram went still.
"You can cut the feed," said Sita, very softly. "You can cut the camera in the corridor, and the screen in the audit hall, and the lens on the spire. You can have every officer in the city stand at a glass wall and watch the crowd turn its back. But you can't cut the square, Ram. It's already heard me. It'll go on hearing me, long after you've hanged every clerk in the lower ward and burned every sheet of paper in the Board's archive."
Ram did not turn round.
"You're going to kill me," he said.
"Maybe," said Sita. "Would it make you feel better?"
"No."
"Then what would?"
He stood with his hand on the cold steel and his forehead bowed against the door. He thought of the bridge over the lagoon, and the old crane-operator walking across it. He thought of the morning he had stood on the copper roof at Mithila with the broken bow across his knees, and she had asked him whether he had broken it or it had broken him.
"Nothing," he said.
"Then stop asking me to be the thing that decides it," said Sita, quietly. "You've been doing that since the bed at Mithila. You asked me to forgive you for the rope, and for the square, and for the panel. And I did, every time, because I thought if I said it enough, you'd start believing that you were allowed to be forgiven. But you never once asked the square."
Ram lifted his hand from the door.
He stood very still in the dark corridor. Above him, through a hundred thicknesses of stone, he could hear the faint sound of the city. It was not the chanting. It was not the bell. It was something else, low and wide and uneven, rising from a thousand different voices at once, and it was coming up through the stone of the spire like the first roar of a river under the ice.
"Open the grille," he said.
There was a long silence.
"Why?" asked Sita.
"Because I want to hear you sing," said Ram, very quietly, "and I want to hear the square answer."
The panel on the door stayed dark. But after a moment, with a faint grinding sound, the grille slid open, and the cold air of the vault moved for the first time in months, and in the slot, pale and calm and utterly unafraid, Sita's face appeared, looking at him through the bars.
She did not sing.
She only looked at him, and her eyes were wet, and she said, in the same clear voice she had used in the square:
"Go up to the roof, Ram. Stand on the wall where they can see you. And for once, don't say anything at all."
He went up.
He stood on the rampart in the cold night wind with the engines silent below him and the city spread out in its grid of lamps, and he did not speak. He only listened. The sound from the square had grown. It rose out of the streets and the lower wards and the coastal lanes, a vast soft murmur that swelled and fell and swelled again, and somewhere far below, very faint, a single voice began to sing.
It was not a hymn. It was not a chant or a prayer. It was a short, low, wordless tune, the kind of thing a woman might hum while washing a child's hair in the evening.
And then, from the square, another voice took it up. And another. And another, until the whole city was singing, low and uncertain and out of tune, a sound that rolled up the towers and out over the lagoon and into the dark, and did not stop.
The engines on the north rampart stayed silent.
Ram stood with his hands on the cold stone of the wall and his face turned toward the sound. He did not count. He did not need to.
For the first time since the morning of the boons, the pulse in his wrist was not steady.
It was racing.
And he let it.

CHAPTER IV: The Glass Room
> \*\*\[THE ROAD TAKEN]\*\*
The lamp at the top of the tower had been lit every night for two months, and it was still burning when Ram climbed the stair.
He went up alone. He had told Lakshman to stay on the bridge, and Hanu to watch the lower gate, and Nalu to keep the rig's arm locked over the water, and no one had argued. They had known what he was going to do. He had known it too, from the moment Tavish said the Lord was in the top room, and Sita was on the other side of the glass.
The stair was narrow and dark and smelled of old smoke and wet stone. It wound up through the body of the tower in a long tight spiral, past empty guard-rooms with their doors hanging open, past a landing where a row of ledgers had been stacked on a shelf and set alight and then abandoned, the pages curling into black flakes on the floor. The walls were lined with glass slats, set into the stone at intervals of a few feet, and in each slat a faint blue light moved, very slowly, like the reflection of water on a ceiling.
He did not look at them. He knew what they were. They were the eyes of the house, the old men's windows into the rooms and corridors and courtyards of the island, and every one of them had been watching him since he came through the gate.
At the top of the stair, a single door.
It was made of iron, and it was not locked. It stood a few inches ajar, and a thin line of lamplight fell through the gap onto the stone floor, and from inside, very faint, came the sound of a woman's voice humming.
Ram stopped.
He stood at the threshold for a long moment with his hand on the cold iron and his breath coming slow and steady. He did not push the door open. He only listened. The tune rose and fell, low and clear and perfectly unhurried, and it did not stop when he moved, and it did not change when he touched the door.
Then it stopped, all at once, and a man's voice said:
"Come in, Returning Prince. I've been waiting for you to finish breathing."
---
The top room was round, and it was made entirely of glass.
It rose to a dome above him, a single curved sheet of dark grey pane held in a lattice of black iron, and through the dome the night sky showed in long bruised streaks of cloud and a few hard white stars. The walls were glass too. They were set in panels, each one as tall as a man, and beyond them, on every side, the island lay spread out in its dark ruined beauty: the broken towers, the cracked causeway, the lagoon with its bridge of salvaged rails glimmering faintly in the starlight, and far out beyond the reef, the grey dark line of the open sea.
In the centre of the room stood a single chair.
Vashkar sat in it.
He was not what Ram had expected. He had imagined a monster, from the stories and the feeds and the mouths of the coastal men on the bridge: a giant, perhaps, or a thing of iron and wire, something that could match the bulk of Kumbh. But the Lord of the southern island was only a man. He was lean and long-limbed, dressed in a grey silk tunic that hung loose across his shoulders, and his face was narrow and clever and very tired. His eyes were the colour of old copper, and they were fixed on Ram with a look of such calm, weary, almost affectionate interest that Ram felt his skin prickle.
Then he saw the frame.
It was fixed across the Lord's back and shoulders, a harness of black iron and jointed carbon, and from it sprang four long mechanical arms, each one folded neatly against his ribs like the legs of a sleeping insect. The arms were fine and graceful. Their hands were slender steel, the fingers tipped with delicate instruments, and each one twitched very faintly, as though it were waiting for an order.
"They're the old Board's," said Vashkar, lightly, following his gaze. "I had them grafted on the year the stripping began. The clinic said I'd never need them. I said, a man who rules an island should be able to reach the things he's been given to hold." He lifted one of the steel hands and turned it slowly in the lamplight. "Kumbh had six. He never once used the sixth. He said it made him feel like a clock."
"You kept my wife," said Ram.
It came out flat and cold and entirely without anger, the way the words had come out in the Chamber of Oaths. Vashkar's copper eyes narrowed.
"I kept a furrow-girl," he said. "She was never my wife. She refused me. She refused the food, and the bed, and the gold on the walls. She refused to speak to me for three days, and when she finally did speak, she told me the sea was too cold and the island too small and my men too frightened to do anything but count the days until I died." He smiled. It was not a cruel smile. It was almost fond. "I liked her for it. I'm not ashamed to say so. Do you know how long it's been since anyone told me the truth?"
"Since you took the throne," said Ram.
"Since before I took anything. Since my father hanged my mother from the lower gate for speaking out of turn. Since I was seven years old and learned that the only way to keep the world quiet was to be louder than everything in it." Vashkar set his hand on the arm of the chair. The steel fingers closed on the iron, very slowly, with a faint whine. "I built the wire because I was afraid of the sea. I built the engines because I was afraid of the men on the wire. And I built this room because I wanted to be the only thing in the three worlds that could see everything at once, and never once have to be seen."
"And now?"
"Now I can see you," said Vashkar. "Standing in my doorway, with your hands at your sides and your brother's bow across your back and the sea in your face. And I can see what you did to the men on the bridge, and the men on the wall, and the clerk in your own city who hangs from a tower with his daughter watching. I can see everything, Returning Prince. That's what the glass is for."
He lifted one of the steel hands, and pointed it past Ram's shoulder, at the iron door.
"Close it," he said. "We're going to talk about the island now. And about what you'll give me to leave it."
---
Ram did not close the door.
He stood where he was, on the threshold of the glass room, with the cold wind coming through the iron frame and the lamplight falling across his bare feet. He looked at the Lord of the southern island, and at the four steel arms folded against his ribs, and at the faint humming glow that rose from the floor beneath the chair, a circle of black glass set into the stone, pulsing very slowly like a heart.
It was a panel. It was the same as the one in the control room beneath the north rampart, the same as the one he had stood before on the morning of the boons, with his palm against its cold surface and his pulse rising through it. It was lit now with a faint blue radiance, and as he watched, a line of pale script rose slowly out of the black glass, the same clean modern letters he had seen a hundred times on the feeds in the north.
HOUSE CONFIRMED. ENGINES TO THE SOUTH SHORE.
"You see?" said Vashkar softly. "They're listening. They've been listening for ten years. The old men built them all from the same mould, you know. Every glass in the three worlds. The one in your city. The one in my tower. The one in the lower keep that the coastal men call the Lord's Mouth. They're all tied together, through the cables under the sea. One heart, many doors." He lifted the steel hand again. "And every one of them answers to whoever is standing in front of it, with a steady enough pulse."
Ram looked at the glass.
ENGINES TO THE SOUTH SHORE.
The south shore was the coastal villages. It was the fishing huts on the lagoon's edge, and the caves where the boats had been built, and the new schoolhouse where the coastal children were learning to read the sea-charts. It was the bridge of salvaged rails, and the men who had carried them. It was everything the island had begun, in the last six days, to call its own.
"You won't," said Ram quietly.
"Won't I?" Vashkar's copper eyes gleamed. "I think you'd be surprised what I'd do, if I needed the leverage. You've taken everything else from me. My wire. My men. My brother, who's been given a seat at a table I can't see. My sister's face, which is a thing I'll never forgive your brother for." He smiled. "Why shouldn't I take the south shore, too?"
"Because you don't need to," said Ram. "You're already losing."
Vashkar laughed.
It was a short sharp sound, and it rang off the glass dome above them and echoed around the walls, and the four steel arms twitched once against his ribs.
"Losing," he repeated. "Yes. I suppose I am. But I've lost before, Returning Prince. I lost my mother, and my sister's face, and my brother's trust, and the furrow-girl's voice, which I still hear every night when the wind comes round from the south. I lost my father's throne and my father's men and my father's sea. And every time, I got up again, and built a bigger glass." He leaned forward in the chair. "What have you lost?"
Ram did not answer.
He was looking at the panel.
It had changed. The blue radiance had deepened to a warm amber, and the pale script had faded, and now, very faint, rising slowly out of the black surface like breath on a cold window, a face was forming in the glass.
It was his own.
It was pale and thin, and it had the same grey eyes and the same closed mouth, and the same faint line of tiredness at the corners of the eyes. But it was not him. He knew that as clearly as he knew his own hands. The face in the glass was looking at him with an expression of cold, steady, unhurried attention, the expression of a man who has been waiting a very long time at the bottom of a stair, and has finally seen the figure he was waiting for come down it.
Behind it, very faint, Ram could see a room. It was a control room, low and round and lined with black glass panels. There was a long stone bench, and a staff of men in dark coats standing at their posts, and at the centre of the room, a single chair, and in the chair, lit by the amber glow of a panel, a man in a black coat with silver at the shoulders.
Harit.
And beside Harit, with his hand resting lightly on the back of the chair, watching the island through the glass with an expression of quiet, absolute satisfaction, the man in the glass looked up, very slowly, and smiled.
"You put your hand down," said the face in the panel.
Its voice was his voice. It was lower than Ram had ever heard it, and colder, and it carried across the distance between the two rooms with the flat clean clarity of a feed.
"Yes," said Ram.
"You refused the glass. In the north. You took your palm away when it asked you what it was for." The face tilted slightly. "I've been waiting for you to come back and tell me why. You never did. You just went south, and let the stripping rigs burn without anyone to answer for them. And then you walked out onto a sand-beach and asked a boy with a bow to hand you a crowbar."
"I didn't ask anyone to die."
"No. You never do. You only stand in the road, and let them walk into the gap you've made." The man in the glass leaned forward. His eyes were grey and cold and utterly without malice. "Somebody has to hold up the sky, Ram. You know that. It's what the stone was for, and the house, and the engines on the rampart. Somebody has to put their hand on the glass and say the word, and then carry the weight of it, every day, for the rest of their life. You can't keep pretending you're innocent. You don't get to walk away and call it mercy."
"I'm not asking to be innocent."
"Then what are you asking?"
"To put it down," said Ram.
The room was very still.
In the glass, the face of the Shadow looked at him for a long moment. Then, very slowly, it began to smile.
"Then you're going to fall," it said softly. "Right here, on the floor of a dead man's tower, with a dead man's brother in the next room and a furrow-girl singing in the cellar. You're going to fall, because you can't bear the weight any longer, and you're going to take the whole island down with you. And I'll be standing in my room, with my hand on the glass, waiting to see what the sky does when nobody's holding it up."
"Maybe," said Ram.
"Maybe?" The Shadow laughed. It was not a happy sound. "Is that all you've got?"
"No," said Ram quietly. "I've got this."
He stepped forward, into the room, and laid his right hand flat against the glass.
---
It was cold. It was colder than the river had been on the morning of the ghat, colder than the panel in the north, colder than anything he had ever touched in his life. The surface gave slightly under his palm, the way a skin of water gives under a leaf, and then it held him, and the pulse came up through it, through the glass and the stone and the iron of the floor, into the bones of his wrist.
It was racing.
He could feel it hammering in his chest, a wild uneven drumbeat that rose and fell with every breath. He had not felt it like this since the morning on the copper roof at Mithila, when the bow had cracked in his hands and the whole hall had gone silent. He had not felt it since the sandals were put into his hands at the gate. He had not felt it since the bridge, and the singing, and the young coastal mother laughing in the square.
Four, two, six.
It would not come.
The face in the glass was watching him. Its palm had come up, slowly, to meet his, and in the amber light the two hands seemed to hover on either side of the surface, a finger's width apart, like the reflections of two men looking at each other across a pool of still water.
"You can't feel it, can you?" said the Shadow, very softly. "The pulse. It's gone. You've been running on fear so long you've forgotten what steady feels like."
"I can feel it," said Ram.
"Then why is it racing?"
Ram did not answer. He stood with his palm against the cold glass and his eyes fixed on the face of the man who was himself, and he thought, very clearly, of a sentence he had heard once in a hermitage on the edge of a dead forest, from an old man with a staff and a face like a dried riverbed.
Then you have already learned something.
"Because I'm afraid of what you'll do," said Ram, "if I stop."
The Shadow's face changed.
It was only a flicker. A tightening at the corner of the mouth, a faint narrowing of the eyes. But Ram saw it, and he saw in it something he had not expected: not triumph, not anger, but a kind of weary, terrible recognition, the look of a man who has been fighting his own shadow for so long he has forgotten what he is fighting for.
"Then stop," said the Shadow, very low. "Take your hand away. Walk down the stair. Tell the coastal men to go home. And let me have the island, Ram. Let me hold it, the way you held the north. Let me be the one who decides what happens next."
"No," said Ram.
The word came out of him clean and steady and completely calm, and the pulse under his palm shuddered, once, and steadied.
The Shadow's eyes flared.
"You can't refuse me," it hissed. "You're nothing without me. You're the face on the spires. You're the stone in the square. You're the man who let the Lord's son walk out of his own keep with a cup of water in his hand. Do you think they remember you for that? They remember the clapping. They remember the rope. They'll remember you the way they remember every king who ever stood in a glass room and told them what the sky was for."
"Then I'll let them forget," said Ram.
He took his hand away from the glass.
---
The panel went dark.
The face in the amber glow dissolved, slowly, like a reflection on a pool when the wind comes across it. The control room behind it faded. The bench, the staff, the chair, the man in the black coat with silver at his shoulders: they vanished one by one, and the last thing to go was the Shadow's face, pale and grey-eyed and suddenly very young, turning away from the glass with an expression of bewildered grief, as though it had only just realised that the thing it was looking for had been lost somewhere in the dark long before it had thought to look.
Then the glass was just glass again. Black, and cold, and faintly reflecting the lamp and the dome and the two men standing in the centre of the room.
Vashkar was no longer in his chair.
He was standing. He had risen so quickly that the chair had toppled behind him, and his four steel arms were unfolded from his ribs and spread out in a wide, horrible fan, the slender fingers flexing and twitching with a faint high whine. His face was white. His copper eyes were fixed on Ram with an expression of pure, incandescent fury.
"You fool," he whispered. "You stupid, blind, self-righteous fool. You've just killed the only thing in the three worlds that could have saved you."
"No," said Ram. "I've just stopped it from saving anyone."
The steel arms moved.
They came at him in a single fluid rush, all four of them, slashing down from above and to either side with a speed and precision that no human hand could ever have matched. The first one struck the floor a hand's breadth from his bare foot and cracked the flagstone open. The second swept round at chest height, and Ram ducked under it, and felt the wind of it pass over his skull like the blade of a scythe. The third came down at his shoulder. He twisted. It caught the edge of his tunic and tore the cloth away in a long ragged strip, and the cold steel fingers grazed his skin, and he felt the thin hot line of a cut open across his collarbone.
He rolled. He came up against the glass wall, with the dark panes at his back and the island spread out beyond them, and the fourth steel arm came slicing toward his throat.
He did not reach for the bow.
He reached instead into the torn fold of his tunic, where the grey disc had been sewn into the lining since the hermitage at Siddhashrama, a small cold weight he had carried through the whole of the road, and never once taken out.
You will not use this yet, the old man had said, in the hermitage, with the sun coming down through the bars of light onto the packed earth floor. You are not ready. But you have now held it, and you know what it asks of you.
Ram drew the disc out.
It was grey and heavy and cold, and the spiral grooves on its underside pressed against his palm like the lines of a seed. He held it up in front of him, and the steel arm stopped a finger's width from his throat, its fingers flexing wildly, and Vashkar's copper eyes went wide as he saw what was in Ram's hand.
"No," said the Lord of the southern island, very softly.
"Yes," said Ram.
He did not look at the disc. He looked at the Lord. He looked at the narrow clever face, and the tired eyes, and the grey silk hanging loose across the shoulders of a man who had built a glass room to be the only thing in the three worlds that could see everything, and had never once in his life been able to see himself.
And he breathed.
Four.
Two.
Six.
It came. It came slowly, dragging at first, like a cart over broken stone. Then faster. Then steady. It rose up out of his chest like a tide coming in over the flats, and it filled him, and the racing of his pulse went out of him like water draining from a cracked basin, and in its place there was only a single slow, heavy beat, a stone settling into the bed of a river that had been running for a very long time.
The disc in his palm grew warm.
Then it began, very faintly, to glow.
The light came up through the grooves on its underside in a thin pale ring, and the ring pulsed once, and then again, in time with the beat of his heart. The four steel arms behind Vashkar had gone still. The fingers had stopped twitching. Every one of them was turned toward the grey disc in Ram's hand, and Ram could see, in the faint pale glow, the reflection of his own face in the polished steel of the Lord's fingertips: calm, grave, utterly still.
"Your heart," whispered Vashkar. His voice had gone thin and high. "It's steady."
"Yes."
"How? How can it be steady now?"
"Because I'm not waiting for anyone to tell me what it's for," said Ram quietly. "I already know. It's to put the sky down."
The ring of light on the disc went green.
Vashkar screamed.
It was not a scream of fear. It was a scream of pure, animal, astonished rage, torn out of him from somewhere deep in the belly, and his four steel arms snapped up like the legs of a dying spider and lunged. But the disc was already moving. Ram did not throw it. He only opened his hand, and the payload left his palm with a soft dull hum, and hung for a heartbeat in the air between them, turning slowly, glowing green.
Then the column fired.
It was not loud. It was a deep, hollow, shuddering thud, like a great door closing in a far distant room. A single column of compressed air and fine steel burst out of the disc in a shimmering silver wedge, and it went through the Lord of the southern island from the centre of his chest to the back of his skull, and out through the iron frame of his harness and the four steel arms, and the arms exploded outward in a shower of sparks and twisted carbon, and the glass behind him shattered into a thousand glittering fragments that rained down across the floor like hailstones.
Vashkar stood very still for a moment.
He looked down at his chest, where a neat round hole had opened in the grey silk, and his face was calm, and faintly puzzled, and then it went slack, and he fell.
He fell forward onto the flagstones, slowly, the way a tree falls when the axe has finally found the heart of it, and he lay there in the scattered glass with his copper eyes open and his steel arms twisted and smoking around him. A faint thread of smoke rose from the hole in his tunic. The light in the disc on the floor went out.
Ram stood with his back to the glass wall and the blood running warm down his collarbone, and he did not move.
For a long time there was only the sound of the wind through the broken dome, and the faint hiss of the sea far below, and somewhere in the next room, very quiet, a woman's voice humming a tune she had been humming for two months, without once stopping.
Then the tune stopped.
And a voice from the stair, breathless and frightened, called up through the iron door:
"Ram? Ram, are you—"
It was Lakshman.
Ram did not answer right away. He was looking at the Lord's body. He was looking at the face, which had been narrow and clever and tired, and was now only a face, pale and still and surprised, the face of a man who had been told the sky was his to hold and had only just found out it had never been his to drop.
"I'm here," said Ram, very quietly.
Footsteps came up the stair, fast and heavy, and the iron door flew open, and Lakshman came through it with his bow in his hands and his face white with terror. He saw the body, and the broken glass, and the cut across Ram's collarbone, and he stopped dead.
"You killed him," he said hoarsely.
"Yes."
"With the disc."
"Yes."
Lakshman looked at the grey disc lying on the floor by Vashkar's hand, its surface dark and cold now, its grooves catching the faint light of the lamp. He looked at his brother's face.
"Did you have to?" he whispered.
Ram was silent for a long time.
"No," he said at last. "But he'd have turned the wire on the south shore, and the coastal men would have died in their beds. And I'd have had to decide, again, which of them was worth more. And I'm not going to do that any more, Lakshman. Not once. Not for anyone."
Lakshman's face crumpled. He set the bow down very carefully against the wall, and came across the broken glass, and pulled his brother into his arms, and held on.
They stood like that in the wreckage of the glass room, with the wind coming through the broken dome and the sea roaring softly far below, and neither of them spoke.
Then, from the other side of the iron door, a woman's voice called, very clear and very quiet, through the stone:
"Ram?"
He pulled back from his brother's arms and turned his head toward the sound.
"I'm here," he said.
"I know you are." Sita's voice was steady, but there was a tremor under it, a thin fine crack like the one that ran through the iron of the grating before the fire was lit. "The sea went quiet. The lamp went out. And then the glass started to sing, and it sang your name, and I thought—" She stopped. Her breath caught. "Open the door. Please. I want to see your face."
Ram crossed the room, over the broken glass and the fallen chair and the dead man's body, and laid his palm against the cold iron of the door.
It did not wake for him. It did not need to.
He pushed.
It swung inward with a soft groan of old hinges, and beyond it, in the narrow dark of the inner passage, stood a woman in a grey cotton dress with her plait tied up under a cloth, and her feet bare on the stone, and a small pale scar along one side of her jaw.
She looked at him for a long time without speaking.
Then she looked past him, into the room, at the body and the glass and the grey disc lying dark on the floor. Her face did not change. But something in her eyes, very deep, very slow, let go.
"You put it down," she said.
"Yes."
"And it didn't fall."
"No," said Ram. "It was never mine to hold."
Sita stepped across the threshold, carefully, over the cracked iron, and came to stand in front of him in the broken light of the glass room. She lifted her hand, slowly, and laid two fingers against the inside of his wrist, where the pulse beat under the skin.
It was steady.
She closed her eyes, and held it there for a long moment, and when she opened them again, there were tears on her face, but she was smiling.
"Four, two, six," she said softly.
"Four, two, six," said Ram.
Behind them, the sun was beginning to come up over the eastern cape, pale and cold and clean, and its first light came in through the shattered dome of the glass room and fell across the three of them in long slanting bars, and touched the grey disc on the floor, and the dead man's face, and the broken panels of the house's glass, which had gone dark now, every one of them, and would not wake again.

Shadow III: The Blind South
> \*\*\[SHADOW ROAD]\*\* \*The crown, and the word not yet spoken.\*
The south went dark on the twentieth day, and the north did not say so aloud for another three hours.
The glass in the south did not die all at once. It flickered first. Ram saw it on the panel in the old control room beneath the north rampart, where he had come down alone in the grey hour before dawn with a lamp in one hand and his palm already out in front of him, as if the wall might reach back. The pale ring at the centre of the black glass pulsed, slow and steady, and then it stuttered, and for one long heartbeat the surface was not black at all. It was amber.
A face came up in it.
It was thin and pale, with grey eyes and a closed mouth, and a faint line of tiredness at the corners of the eyes. It was his face. It was the face on the spires, and the face in the feeds, and the face that had looked back at him from the wet glass of the north-tower stair on the morning he first laid his palm on the panel and felt the pulse answer.
It was not smiling.
It was looking at him with a clear, cool, unhurried attention, the look of a man standing in the centre of a room he has entered for the first time, and finding that the door he came through has already been closed behind him.
No, it said.
Only that. It did not raise its voice. It said the word as it might say a figure, or a name, or the time of day. And then the amber went out, and the ring on the glass pulsed once, twice, and was still, and the surface went black again, cold and blank and reflecting nothing but the lamp in Ram's hand.
He stood in the dark for a long time.
The lamp burned down. Somewhere above him, through a hundred thicknesses of stone, the engines on the north rampart began their morning test, a long low hum that rolled across the citadel and made the glass of the spire ring faintly in its frames.
He did not hear it. He was listening to a single word, very quiet, from the far side of the glass.
No.
---
Harit came down the stair an hour later with the signal-sheet in his hand, and his face was grey.
"The south is blind," he said. "Lanka station is silent. The island's feed is gone. The terrace engines report the pulse has not returned to the house since the glass went out." He laid the sheet on the bench beside the panel, very carefully, the way a man lays down a thing he would rather not have picked up. "They say the tower's lamp went out at the fourth bell. They say there was a sound in the glass room, like something very large breaking. And then the house-line went quiet, my lord, from the cape to the reef. As if the whole south had decided, all at once, that it no longer had anything to say to us."
"And the keys?"
Harit's mouth tightened. "Not in the house's hand," he said. "The coastal men have them. Or the Lord's son does. Or the brother, who was supposed to be shot on the sea-wall three days ago and wasn't, because the men on the terrace wouldn't pull the trigger when he begged them to. They told the terrace officers that the island had been asked what it wanted, and it had answered." He breathed out. "And somewhere in all that noise, my lord, I heard a name I didn't like."
"Which name?"
"Yours." Harit looked at him steadily. "The one on the spires. The coastal men say the prince walked out of the sea with a bow on his back, and asked the men on the wire to put down their guns. They say he carried a crowbar to the bridge himself. They say he sat on a stool in the square and asked what the wards wanted, and then he didn't tell them. He just listened." His voice was flat. "They say the face on the spires has been sitting in the mud of a salt-flat for three weeks, letting the men he's supposed to command make their own decisions, and that the island has started to believe him."
Ram did not move.
"He can't be doing that," he said quietly.
"No, my lord," said Harit. "That's what I told them. But they're not listening to me any more. They're listening to a man who won't put his hand on the glass."
---
The coronation was set for the Chamber of Oaths, at noon on the twenty-second day, and it was the first ceremony the north had seen in a year that did not have to be explained on the feeds.
They did not need the feeds. The square was full before the bell rang. Three thousand people stood packed on the grey stone below the bell-tower, and another two thousand pressed up the avenues and round the corners of the Board's old offices, and the faces on every spire had been restored to the glass, bright and gold and enormous, so that the whole city seemed to be looking up at one face and waiting for it to speak.
The face on the spires was his.
It had been painted and repainted over the last month, by the Board's engineers, from the old clip of the boy on the copper roof with the broken bow across his knees. The hands had been made stronger. The mouth had been made firmer. The grey eyes had been lightened to a steely blue, so that they caught the sun and seemed to glow. Beneath the face, in the Board's pale modern script, a single line:
THE KING OF THE TWO SHORES.
Ram walked up the steps of the Chamber alone.
He wore a plain black coat, buttoned to the throat, and no ring on his finger. The guards at the great doors stood aside for him without a word. Inside, the hall was lit by a dozen hanging lamps, and the light fell in long gold bars across the cold stone floor, and in the centre of the floor the black basalt slab lay as it had always lain, its carvings scrubbed clean by a clerk with a soft brush.
Vasishtha was not there.
Ram had sent the old priest to his rooms on the palace's west wing two days before, with a physician and a guard and a bowl of warm broth, because the old man had refused to come out of the Chamber when the Board's men came to clear it, and had sat on his black stone stool with his white beard combed flat and his eyes half-closed, saying nothing at all. He had been carried out at last, in a litter of folded cloth. He had not looked back.
On the high chair at the far end of the hall, where Dasharatha had sat with his hands shaking in his lap, there was now a new crown.
It was made of the glass from the spire-panels. The Board's engineers had cut it into thin curved sheets, polished them, and set them into a band of dark iron that had once been the rail of the east-spire lift. It caught the lamplight and threw it back in a thousand small sharp glints, like the surface of a lake at night. It was very beautiful. It was also, Ram knew, the only thing in the north that had ever been made to be looked at from every side.
He did not go to the chair.
He stood at the edge of the petitioners' square, where the shadow of the slab fell across the stone, and waited.
Harit came in through the side door with the decree-case under his arm and his black coat buttoned high. He did not look at Ram. He walked to the foot of the slab and unfolded a grey sheet of paper, and read it aloud in his flat clear voice, and the words rolled up the hall and into the vault of the ceiling and came back down again, very slowly, like something being lowered on a rope.
By the authority of the house of Ikshvaku, and the consent of the Board, and the sealed word of the Chamber of Oaths, the king of Kosala is confirmed in the north and the south, from the Sarayu to the sea-line, and all the engines of the realm are placed in his hand. The square is dissolved. The schools are closed. The lower wards are held for the restoration of order.
Harit lowered the paper.
"Will you take it, my lord?" he said quietly.
Ram did not answer.
"They're waiting," said Harit. "Below. In the square. They've been waiting since dawn. They don't want a speech, my lord. They want the glass lit, and the bell rung, and the face on the spires to turn round and look at them." His voice dropped. "And they want to know that the man who holds the keys is still the man they saw on the copper roof. Because if he isn't, my lord, they'll start asking who he is instead."
Ram looked at the crown.
He thought of a glass room at the top of a tower, with a grey disc in his palm and a dead man's face on the floor. He thought of a square full of people sitting on the stone with their bowls of rice, listening to a clerk read their names aloud. He thought of a woman's voice, through a grille in a vault, saying very clearly, You can't cut the square.
He had thought, for a while, that she was threatening him.
He understood now that she had been telling him the truth.
"Bring me the paper," he said.
Harit blinked. "My lord?"
"The decree. Bring it to me. I want to sign it myself."
---
He signed it at the foot of the high chair, with a Board pen that scratched on the old house paper and left a dark uneven line. The sound was very loud in the silent hall. Harit stood at his shoulder and watched. The guards at the doors watched. And the crowd, which could not hear anything inside the Chamber, watched the face on the spires, which had turned, very slowly, from its gilded glare toward the Chamber's great doors, as if it had been waiting for a sign.
When he straightened, the glass in the spires blazed gold.
The bell rang.
And from the square, very faint through the thick stone walls, came a sound that Ram had not expected, though he should have. It was not a cheer. It was not a chant. It was the murmur of three thousand people beginning to hum.
It was a short, low, wordless tune. It rose and fell without hurry, the kind of thing a woman might sing while kneading bread or washing a child's hair, and it had been travelling through the lower wards for eleven days, passed from mouth to mouth in the schoolyards and the kitchens and the lanes behind the Board's offices, since the morning a voice had come out of a grille on every spire in the city and told them the names of the dead.
The murmur grew.
Harit's face went white. "My lord," he said, very quietly. "They're not cheering."
"No."
"Shall I clear the square?"
Ram did not answer. He was listening. The tune was rising from the square now, out through the great doors and over the Chamber's threshold and into the cold bright air of noon. It was ragged and out of tune and getting louder. It did not stop when the guards at the foot of the bell-tower lifted their rifles. It did not stop when the first shot cracked across the stone, flat and hard and final, and a woman near the fountain went down with a cry.
It only faltered, for a moment, and then went on.
"Clear the square," said Ram.
He heard his own voice from a long way off, quiet and perfectly steady, the way he had once heard it in the Chamber of Oaths when the boons were called. He did not raise it. He did not need to. The guards at the doors turned and ran, and the sound of their boots went down the Chamber steps and across the stone like the clatter of falling dice, and then came the rifles, and then the screaming.
The tune did not stop until the engines fired.
---
Afterward, the square was very quiet.
It was not the silence of the hanging-day, or the held breath before a sentence. It was a flat, grey, empty silence, the silence of a space that has been swept clean, and there were not many people in it. The engines on the north rampart had fired three times, long and slow, over the lower wards. The lamps on the avenues had gone out. The faces on the spires had stayed bright, and their glass shone gold over the bodies on the stone, and nobody in the city could look up at them without seeing the dead.
Ram did not go down to the square.
He went down to the vault.
The guards at the top of the stair did not stop him. They only stood aside, with their faces white and their rifles grounded, and let him pass into the cold dim corridor where the iron doors stood in their rows, each one with its small dark grille at head height. He walked past them to the end, to the single larger door in its arch of black stone, and laid his palm on the steel panel.
It woke at once.
The green line rose along its base. The pulse came up through the steel, steady and clean, and the door hissed open on its old hinges, and the cold air of the vault moved past him in a long slow sigh.
Sita was sitting on the stone bench.
She had not moved since the morning he last came down. She sat with her back very straight and her hands folded in her lap, in the same grey cotton dress she had worn in the square, and the lamp in its iron bracket threw her shadow long across the polished walls. Her face was thin. Her eyes were dark and steady and absolutely clear.
She had been singing. He could tell. There was a faint flush along her throat, and her lips were still parted, as though the tune had only just stopped.
"They've broken the glass in the south," she said.
Ram did not answer.
"I heard it through the grille," said Sita quietly. "Anand told me. He came down with the bread this morning, and he was shaking so badly he dropped the jug. He said the spires went dark for a moment, all of them, and then came back on with your face, and then the square started to sing again. And then the guards came." She tilted her head. "He said the glass in the south showed a man who looked like you, once, very briefly. He said the man said no."
"Yes," said Ram.
"Who was he?"
Ram was silent for a long moment. The lamp hissed in its bracket. Somewhere far above them, a shot cracked, and then another, and then the square was quiet again.
"Someone who refused," he said at last.
"Refused what?"
"The keys."
Sita was very still.
"Someone refused the keys," she said slowly. "In the south. Someone who knew what the glass looked like from the inside. Someone who had already sat in the chair and felt the pulse rise through the stone, and understood what it was asking him to do, and then got up and walked away from it." She lifted her eyes to his face. "Who?"
"Someone who knew what it looked like," said Ram quietly.
He did not say the rest. He did not need to. She had already heard it in his voice, and her face changed, very slightly, the way the sea changes when the wind drops and the surface goes still.
"You took the crown," she said.
"Yes."
"And signed the decree. And cleared the square. And listened to the song all the way to the end."
"Yes."
"Why?"
He did not answer her.
He stood in the open doorway with the cold air moving past him, and looked at the woman he had married on a copper roof, sitting very straight on a stone bench in a vault dug out of living rock, singing to a city that was no longer allowed to listen. He thought of the south, and the face in the amber glass, saying no as though it were a name.
He thought: Someone else had to decide what the sky was for.
He thought: I was the only one who ever wanted to.
He said nothing.
Sita looked at him for a long time. Then, very gently, she shook her head.
"Lock it," she said. "Go on. You've been standing in the doorway for three minutes with your hand on the steel, and I can see the green light flickering. You're doing it again. Pretending the door is the thing that's holding you up."
Ram stepped back.
The door hissed shut. The lock sealed with a faint heavy click, and the green line along the panel went dark.
He stood in the dark corridor for a long time with his palm flat against the steel.
Above him, through the stone, very faint, the square had begun to hum again.
---
He did not sleep that night.
He sat alone in the glass room at the top of the east spire, with the lamp burning on the long table and the new crown lying beside it on a square of black cloth, and he listened to the city. The engines were silent on the north rampart. The lower wards were quiet, or quiet enough. And in the spaces between the quiet, rising out of the dark streets and the empty squares and the schoolyards with their sealed doors, the tune went on, low and uncertain and not quite in time, sung by people who had been told to stop and had not.
At midnight the panel on the wall beside him began to hum.
He did not reach for it. He sat where he was, with his hands loose on the table and the lamp throwing its warm gold light across the bare wood. The hum grew. The pale ring at the centre of the glass woke, slow and steady, and the surface went amber, and a line of script rose out of the dark, in the clean modern letters the Board had always used for its orders.
HOUSE CONFIRMED. KING OF THE TWO SHORES. CONFIRM PULSE.
Below the words, a blank line waited, with a cursor blinking at its end.
Ram looked at it for a long time.
He thought of a man in an amber glass, saying no and letting the light go out. He thought of a woman on a stone bench, humming a tune that had no words. He thought of the old priest carried out in a litter of folded cloth, not looking back.
He thought: It would be so easy.
He thought: It always was.
He laid his palm on the glass.
It was cold. The pulse came up through it, steady and clean and utterly his own, rising through the stone and the iron and the thin bones of his wrist, and for the first time in his life the beat did not quicken. It did not race. It did not stutter. It did not ask him anything at all. It only waited, the way a river waits at the edge of a fall, for a man to decide whether he was willing to go over.
AUTHORISE, said the glass.
Ram did not answer right away.
He sat with his palm pressed flat against the cold surface and his eyes on the blinking cursor, and he listened to the tune rising faintly through the walls of the spire, and he thought of the south, and a man in amber glass who had refused the keys, and a woman who had walked a grating in her bare feet while the fire lay under the ash.
Somewhere far away, a prince of Kosala had once said the word Yes in a room full of silence, and had meant it, and had been wrong.
Ram lifted his hand from the glass.
The pulse went on. It was still steady. It would always be steady now. He had learned that the steadiness was not the same as being right, and that a man could hold his breath for a very long time without ever once deciding whether he had any right to let it go.
AUTHORISE. CONFIRM PULSE.
He did not confirm.
He sat in the lamplight, with his hand open on the table, and listened to the city hum its one wrong song in the dark, and waited for morning, and for the first time in his life, did not know what he would say.

CHAPTER V: The Good Age
> \*\*\[THE ROAD TAKEN]\*\*
They dismantled the glass on the island in the first week of the dry season, and nobody asked the old men of the house for permission.
It began with a hammer. Kesh had brought it up from the boat-shed on the first morning, a heavy iron thing with a worn wooden handle, and he stood in the courtyard of the lower keep with it resting on his shoulder and his pipe clenched between his teeth, looking up at the nearest panel on the lowest tower. It was a black slab of glass set into the stone at the height of a man's chest. The pale ring at its centre had been dead for three weeks, since the tower went dark. The thin grey cable that fed it ran down through the stone into the lagoon.
"Well," said Kesh, to nobody in particular. "They can't say I didn't ask."
He swung the hammer.
The panel shattered. It did not crack. It burst outward in a shower of black fragments that rang off the courtyard flagstones like hail, and behind it the old house-line came away from the stone in a long grey coil, sparking faintly where the copper had been cut. Kesh laughed. It was a short hoarse bark, and then he swung again, and again, and the coastal men who had gathered round the courtyard with their children and their tools came forward slowly, one by one, and picked up whatever they could find, and began to work.
They tore out every panel on the island.
They did it in a week. They pried the black slabs from the lower towers and the keep and the lighthouse on the western cape, and they broke them on the stone, and they dragged the cables from their conduits and cut them into lengths and coiled them on the shore. Nalu supervised, with his clipboard under one arm and his grey hair plastered to his skull with sweat. He did not give orders. He only walked from panel to panel, very slowly, making notes in a neat small hand, and when a coastal man asked him whether a particular cable could be kept for the boats, he said yes and went on writing.
The wire came down the same week. Nobody argued about it. The men on the lower fence had been conscripts for ten years, and they took their pliers to the black filament strands and cut them one by one, and wound them round their hands, and carried them down to the beach to be melted in the big fire-pit that had once been the Lord's grating. The smoke rose in a long dark column over the lagoon. It was the first smoke the island had made in a decade that did not come from a machine.
On the seventh night, when the last of the glass had been carried to the shore, the lamps went out.
Not all at once. The coastal men had been told the tower's lights would stay on until the work was done, because the old men of the island had always kept them burning, and nobody had been sure what would happen in the dark. But when the last panel was broken, Tavish walked to the foot of the keep and put his hand on the switch-box in the wall, very slowly, and looked at Ram across the courtyard, and then pulled the lever down.
The island went black.
It was a silence so sudden and complete that Ram heard the sea breathe against the reef a full mile away. He stood in the courtyard with his bare feet on the warm stone and his face turned up, and for a long moment he could not see anything at all. Then, very slowly, his eyes began to adjust. The moon had come up over the eastern cape, pale and clean, and it laid a long bright road across the lagoon from the shore to the broken causeway, and the rails of the bridge glimmered on it like the spine of a sleeping animal.
Above the bridge, above the broken towers and the empty spires and the long low line of the coast, the sky was full of stars.
Ram had never seen so many. He had seen them on the road south, and on the roof of the east spire, and over the gully where the old vulture-pilot had died. But he had always seen them through a haze of lamplight, or the glow of a feed-screen, or the bright hard halo of a tower that had never once let the dark come all the way down. Now there was nothing between him and the sky but the sea and the moon.
Somewhere behind him, on the beach, a child began to cry, and then laugh, and then shout out a word he could not hear. He heard the coastal men's voices rise in answer, soft and startled and full of wonder, as they came out of their huts and stood on the sand with their heads tipped back, looking up.
"There's so many," said a woman's voice, very quietly, somewhere near the shore.
"There always were," said another. "We just couldn't see them."
Ram did not turn round. He stood in the courtyard with the moon on his face and his hands open at his sides, and he listened to the island learn how to look up.
---
Tavish did not want the throne of the south.
He said so on the first morning, sitting at the Lord's old desk in the courtyard, with the ledger open in front of him and the grey pencil in his fingers. He said it to the coastal men who had come to the table to argue about the sea-lanes. He said it to Megh, who sat beside him with a stack of papers in his lap and his face very pale. He said it to Ram, who had come to stand at the edge of the courtyard and had not been asked to sit.
"I'm not a king," Tavish said. "I never was. I was the one who kept the keys, and I wanted the keys because I thought they'd make me someone. They didn't. They made me the man who offered his brother's throne to a stranger on the sand." He put the pencil down. "The island doesn't need a king. It needs somebody to read the ledger out loud every morning until everyone's sick of hearing it. And then somebody else to take his turn."
"Who will that be?" asked Megh, quietly.
Tavish looked at his nephew for a long moment.
"You," he said.
Megh's face went white. "I'm the one who wrote the names."
"I know. That's why it has to be you." Tavish's voice was gentle. "You're the only one who ever counted the dead on this island without once pretending the number was smaller than it was. That's the job. Nobody else wants it."
So Megh sat at the desk every morning, in the cool hour after dawn, and read the names of the men who had been sent to the mainland for timber and never come home. He read the wire-men who had been whipped at the stair, and the kitchen women who had carried the Lord's food up to the top room for twenty years, and the child who had drowned in the lagoon during the last great storm, and whose mother had come to the table every morning since, standing at the back of the crowd, waiting to hear her daughter's name read out loud.
Ram came to listen once. He stood at the edge of the square with the sun on his neck, and he heard the names rise up one by one in Megh's clear, careful voice, and he watched the faces of the coastal men turn toward the desk, and then, slowly, turn back to their own children, and hold them a little tighter.
Nobody cheered when the reading was done. Nobody clapped.
But the next morning, when Megh sat down at the desk and opened the ledger, the square was already full.
---
The flyer went north on the morning of the fifteenth day.
Nalu had worked on it for eleven days. The black filigree was gone. The carbon arms had been stripped and melted down, and the rotor-housings had been rebuilt from the salvage of the broken towers, and the long white hull had been polished until it caught the sun like a bar of new silver. The coastal men had painted it. There had been some argument about the paint. In the end Kesh had said that the only colour nobody on the island would mistake for the Lord's was the colour of the sea-foam on the reef, and the coastal men had agreed, and painted it that way.
Hanu flew it. He had never flown anything bigger than a glider, he said, but he had a feel for the stick. He took the flyer up over the lagoon in a long unhurried arc, and then across the bridge of salvaged rails, and then out over the reef and the open sea, and when he brought it round to land on the beach, the coastal children came running out of the huts, shouting and waving their hats in the air.
Lakshman stood at the foot of the ramp with his arm round Urmila's shoulders. She had sent word from Mithila that she would meet them at the causeway in Ayodhya with a basket of sweets and a letter from her father, and that the letter would say only that the bow-breaker's brother was still a fool. Lakshman had laughed when he read it. He had folded it very carefully and put it in his pocket, and had not taken it out since.
Sita stood beside Ram. She wore a plain white cotton dress, and her plait was tied up under a cloth. She had a small brown bag over one shoulder that held a comb, and a clay lamp, and a grey iron ring on a cord, which she had worn every day since the glass room and had not once taken off.
"Are you going to say goodbye to him?" she asked quietly.
Ram looked up the beach.
Tavish stood at the top of the steps, very thin and very pale, with his hand resting on his nephew's shoulder. Between them stood the Lord's old desk, carried out into the sunlight and left there, because nobody on the island could bring themselves to take it back inside. On its surface lay the long grey ledger, open, its pages covered in close, careful, painstaking script.
The names. All of them.
"He's going to read them every morning," said Sita. "Hanu says so. He says it's the only penance the coastal men would accept from him, and the only one he can bear to give."
"Yes," said Ram.
"Do you think it'll help?"
"No," said Ram. "I think it'll be the first honest thing the island has done in ten years. And then it'll have to do another one tomorrow."
Sita was silent for a moment. Then she said, very softly, "That's what they'll tell the children, you know. Long after the wire's gone. That one man stood up and asked everybody to tell the truth about the island, and then they all did, and the sea didn't fall in."
"They'll tell it wrong."
"Of course they will." She smiled, faintly. "They always do. But they'll tell it."
---
They came home on the first dry morning of the new season, in the sea-foam flyer.
They flew over the reef, and the open sea, and the long grey line of the coast, and the fishing villages with their smoke rising in thin blue threads from the cook-fires and their nets drying on bamboo poles along the sand. Ram sat by the window in the back and watched the land go by. The Janasthan was a long black scar on the horizon, the western rise still grey with ash, the Ledger's dry bed a pale wound through the blackened scrub. Nobody spoke as they passed over it. Lakshman sat beside him with his bow across his knees and his eyes closed, and his lips were moving very slightly, in the rhythm of a count.
They stopped at Pampa lake.
Sumati was sitting on the shore beside her hut, mending a net in the sun. She did not look up at the flyer's roar, or at the white hull settling onto the cracked mud of the old shoreline, or at the young man who leapt out of the cabin with his hair flying and his face split by a grin. She only finished the knot she was tying, and bit off the end of the cord, and then looked up at Ram as he came down the slope toward her.
"You're alive," she said.
"Yes."
"Gidh would have liked the flyer. He always wanted one. He said a glider was only for people who couldn't afford an engine." She set the net aside. "Did he have a funeral?"
"Yes," said Ram. "On the island. The coastal men carried the stones."
Sumati nodded slowly.
"Good," she said. "Then the lake's still clean."
She put out one thin brown hand, and Ram took it, and held it for a long moment. When he let go, she was smiling.
"Go north, prince," she said. "There are people up there who've been waiting a long time to be told the truth. Don't be the one who makes them wait any longer."
---
They reached Siddhashrama on the third day.
The hermitage had been rebuilt. Not all of it. The western breach was still a heap of blackened stone, and the courtyard had not yet begun to green. But the central building had a new roof of thatch and bamboo, and its blue-washed doors had been painted again in fresh bright colours that caught the morning sun. There were children in the courtyard. There were women at the well, drawing water in clay pots. And under the neem tree, sitting in a half-circle on the ground with their hands full of dark purple berries, sat a small group of children sorting fruit into piles.
One of them looked up as the flyer came down in the field below the ridge.
It was the girl with the berries. She was older now, perhaps nine, and her hair had been cut short, and there was a scar across one of her knuckles that had not been there before. She stood up very slowly, with a purple berry still in her fingers, and stared at the white flyer as it settled into the dust, and then at the figure walking up the slope toward the gate.
She did not run. She lifted one hand, and waved.
Ram raised his own hand in return.
Nalini came out of the central building as he reached the gate. She was thinner than he remembered, and her grey braid was longer, and she carried a clay bowl in both hands. She stopped at the sight of him, and the bowl trembled, and a little water slopped over the rim onto the packed earth.
"You came back," she said.
"I said I would," said Ram.
"You said that three months ago." Her eyes moved past him, to Sita and Lakshman and Hanu at the edge of the courtyard, and then came back to Ram. They were measuring, as they had been the first time, but the measure had changed. "The sage is dead," she said quietly. "He went up the hill to the old Vamana tirtha in the spring, with his staff and his bag and his face like a dried riverbed, and he didn't come back. We found his staff at the bottom of the steps. The water was very still."
Ram was silent.
"He left you something," said Nalini. "On the table inside. He said you'd come for it, and that you'd know what it was."
The room was cool and dim, with its thick walls and its small high windows set with bars of pale light. The shrine on the far wall was lit, with a single clay lamp burning steadily in a bowl of water. On the low wooden table in the centre of the room, someone had set out a long bundle of cloth.
Ram unwrapped it slowly.
Beneath the cloth lay a row of smooth grey discs, each one the size of a palm, each with a thin silver ring round its edge. Beside them lay a single dark cylinder no longer than a finger, its end set with a small ring of pale lights, every one of them dark.
And at the very bottom, folded neatly under the cloth, lay a small wooden toy: a horse, painted once in bright blue and yellow, with a single wheel missing from its base.
Ram picked it up. It was light. It smelled of dust and old paint.
He stood in the cool dim room for a long time, holding the horse in his palm.
Behind him, very softly, Sita came to stand in the doorway.
"Whose is it?" she asked.
"A child's," said Ram. "One of the children in the courtyard. She lost it in the breach, the day the stripping rigs came through. I saw it on the ground. I thought someone would come back for it."
"Did they?"
"No," said Ram. "Not yet."
He went out into the courtyard, past the well and the neem tree and the children sorting berries, and he knelt in the dust beside the girl with the scarred knuckle.
"You lost something," he said.
She looked at him, wary and curious.
"A horse. With blue on it. And a wheel missing."
Her eyes widened. "That's mine," she whispered. "I thought the breach had taken it. I thought—" She stopped. Her face crumpled, the way children's faces do when they have been holding something in for a long time and have just been told it is allowed to come out. "Where is it?"
"Inside," said Ram. "I'll fetch it."
"Will it still be broken?"
"Yes," said Ram quietly. "But it's still yours. And I think somebody here knows how to fix a wheel."
She stared at him for a long moment. Then, very slowly, she nodded, and wiped her eyes on the back of her scarred hand. She did not look up again until he had gone inside and come back out with the horse held carefully in both hands.
---
They reached Ayodhya on the seventh day, at dusk, and the city was not the city Ram had left.
The grid of the ashtapada blocks was still there. The highways hummed with the freight pods, and the rails ran in their long bright processions toward the Sarayu. But the lamps on the corners were dark. The white panels on the raised platforms above the roads had been taken down and stacked in grey heaps beside the schoolhouse gates, and the children were climbing over them, shouting, using them as slides. The spires still caught the last of the sun. But their faces were gone. Every panel had been blanked, a flat grey sheet set into the frame like a closed eye, and when the light touched them, they did not burn gold. They only reflected the sky.
The square at the foot of the bell-tower was full of people. They were not chanting. They sat on the stone in small groups, with their children and their baskets and their bowls of rice, and in the centre of the square, on a low wooden platform, a clerk in a grey coat was reading aloud from a stack of papers. His voice carried across the whole square without any need for speakers.
It was Harit.
He did not look up as the flyer came over the spires. He only read on, steadily, while the crowd turned their heads to watch the white hull sink toward the palace roof, and then, one by one, turned back to listen.
Ram did not go to the palace.
He went to the river.
---
The river-steps at Ayodhya had been built by the first of the Ikshvaku kings, of pale stone worn to a soft gleam by ten thousand years of feet. They ran down from the palace gardens to the Sarayu in a long shallow sweep, and at the foot of them, where the water lapped the lowest step, there was a black patch on the stone where a pyre had been lit.
Bharat was sitting on the steps.
He was alone. He sat with his arms round his knees and his face turned toward the water, and he did not look up when Ram came down the stone stair. He only said, very quietly, in a voice that was hoarse and tired and not quite steady:
"They told me you'd come this way. They said the flyer had a white hull and a boy at the stick who laughed too much."
"Hanu," said Ram.
"He sounds like a monkey."
"He's better than most men," said Ram, and sat down beside his brother on the cold stone.
They did not speak for a long time. The river moved softly at their feet, grey and gold in the last of the light, and across it the far bank was dark with the shadows of the new schoolhouses. In the city behind them a small high child's bell was ringing, rung by someone who had been told it was time for supper.
"He kept asking for you," said Bharat at last. "At the end. Every morning. He'd wake up in the Chamber and look at the door and say, Has he come back yet? And I'd say no. And he'd close his eyes and say, Then he's still keeping his word."
"I know."
"You don't. You weren't here." Bharat's hands tightened round his knees. "I was. I sat with him for seven days. I held his hand while the physicians argued about his heart. And then I carried him down these steps, with Sumant and the old priest and four of the Board's guards who didn't know what they were carrying, and I put him on the pyre myself. And I didn't cry. I was too angry to cry."
Ram said nothing. He only looked at the black patch on the stone.
"I'm angry at you," said Bharat quietly. "I want you to know that. I'm angry at you for walking out of the Chamber with your head up, and taking a dying man's sandals into the dead country, and never once asking whether I wanted to be the one left behind." He drew a breath. "And I'm angry at you for coming back now, when the city's been half-blind for two years and the Board's men are reading their own crimes aloud. Because I've been trying to fix it alone, Ram. And I don't think I'm very good at it."
"No," said Ram. "Nobody is."
Bharat turned his head and looked at him, and his eyes were wet and furious and very young.
"Then what are you good at?"
Ram looked at the river, and the grey gold light on the water, and his own hands resting open on his knees.
"Listening," he said at last. "Badly. For a very long time. And then, when I finally understand what someone's trying to say, I usually do something stupid about it."
Bharat laughed. It came out of him all at once, a rough cracked bark that surprised them both, and when he took his hand away from his mouth his face was wet.
"That's the most honest thing I've ever heard you say," he said.
"It isn't the best."
"No." Bharat wiped his face on his sleeve. "But it'll do."
Ram reached into the pocket of his tunic and drew out a small bundle, carefully folded in oilcloth. He held it out.
Bharat stared at it.
"I told you I'd bring them back," said Ram quietly.
He unwrapped the cloth. The sandals were inside. They were pale ash-wood, worn grey and soft along the grain, their soles carved thin and smooth, their thongs of dark leather mended once at the heel with a tight neat stitch. They had walked over stone and dust and river-mud, through the salt flats and the forest and the ford and the ravine, and they had been carried for two years against a man's chest, in a bag that had been lost in a flyer and found again on a beach at dawn.
Bharat took them. His hands were shaking.
"They're his," he said.
"Yes."
"He walked barefoot to the source of the Sarayu. He only put these on at the end, so the first thing he'd feel at the holy place was the ground itself." Bharat's voice cracked. "He told me that once. I thought it was a joke. I never asked him whether he meant it."
"He meant it," said Ram.
Bharat looked down at the sandals in his lap for a long time.
Then he stood up, slowly, and walked to the edge of the lowest step, where the water lapped against the black mark on the stone. He knelt. He set the sandals down on the wet stone, side by side, very carefully, the way a man might set down a child who has fallen asleep in his arms. Then he stayed there, on his knees, with his head bowed.
He did not pray. He only stayed.
After a while, Ram came down the steps and knelt beside him. They stayed together on the wet stone, with the river moving softly at their feet and the bell ringing faintly in the city behind them, and neither of them spoke again.
---
Kaikeyi was in the palace gardens the next morning, on her knees in the dust beside the old well, with a basket of marigold seedlings at her side and a trowel in her hand.
She did not look up when Ram came through the gate. She finished pressing a seedling into the earth, and sat back on her heels, and only then turned her head. Her face was thin and grey and very still. She wore a plain brown cotton sari with no silk and no pins in her hair.
"The children asked for flowers," she said. "On the terrace. The schoolmistress came up with a list. She said they wanted to plant something that wouldn't be cut down by the Board's men, so they could see it grow." Her mouth twisted. "I thought I'd help. It seemed the least I could do."
Ram said nothing. He came to stand at the edge of the bed.
"I came out of the palace three years ago," said Kaikeyi quietly, "to say I was sorry. I got as far as the top step. And then I saw your face on a spire, looking down at the square, and I couldn't go any further. I thought, He's still punishing me. He's still making me wait for the thing I did to come back and knock on my door." She looked at her hands. "And then I realised it wasn't you who'd put the face there. It was the men in the towers. They'd taken your name and made it into a thing that could be sold."
"Yes," said Ram.
"Do you forgive me?"
He thought about it for a long time.
"I can't forgive you for him," he said at last. "He's the only one who can do that. And he's been trying for two years to decide whether he wants to."
Kaikeyi closed her eyes.
"But you can forgive yourself a little," said Ram, "if you stop asking anyone else to do it for you."
She was silent for a long time. Then, very slowly, she picked up the trowel and turned back to the bed, and began to press another seedling into the dark earth.
"Yes," she said quietly. "I suppose that's the only way it ever works. The stone was never going to give anybody back their father."
---
The Chamber of Oaths was open.
Its great doors had been folded back against the walls, and the light from within spilled across the threshold in a long pale bar, the same bar of light Ram had walked through on the morning the sage came to the gate. But the hall beyond was different. The pillars had been stripped of their Board banners. The white cloth had been taken from the high chair. There was no ministerial ring along the walls, no staff standing grave at their posts. There were only people.
They sat on the floor, and on the steps, and on the cold worn flagstones of the petitioners' square, in small quiet groups with their children beside them and their papers in their laps. Some were writing. Some were reading aloud from the sheets that Harit's clerks had been printing all week, on the old house paper, in plain black script. Some were simply waiting, with the patient, weary, expectant faces of people who had been told for a long time that somebody would come to hear them, and had not quite believed it.
At the foot of the basalt slab, on a low wooden stool, sat Vasishtha.
He was very old now. His white beard had yellowed at the edges, and his eyes were half-closed, and his lips were moving very slightly as he counted something under his breath. He did not look up as Ram came down the hall. He only raised one thin hand, very slowly, and laid it flat on the carvings of the stone, the boons that no one had been able to read for years.
"They've cleared it," he said, without looking up. "A clerk came with a brush this morning. He said the petitioners wanted to read it. He said it was time."
Ram looked down at the slab.
The carvings had been scrubbed clean. For the first time in a hundred years, the old script stood out sharp and dark against the black stone, the words of a king's oath and a queen's boon, cut into the rock by hands that had been dead since before the first tower was built.
Two boons, given freely, and not to be taken back.
"It's a promise," said the high priest quietly. "That's all the stone ever was. A promise carved so deep no one could say it had never been made. And every king who came after your father thought the carving was the same thing as the promise." He lifted his head, and his eyes were pale and clear and very tired. "It never was. The stone can hold a boon. It can't make anybody keep it."
"Then what does?" asked Ram.
Vasishtha smiled, faintly.
"The people who are still in the room when the next one is asked," he said. "Read the rest, prince. Then tell them what you've decided. They've been waiting a long time to hear you say it."
---
He did not take the throne.
He said so in the square that evening, standing on the wooden platform where Harit had read the permits for a week, with the lamps of the city burning gold on the spires above him and the crowd packed close around the base of the bell-tower. He said it plainly. He did not dress it in the old language of the stone, or the Board's pale modern script. He only said it, the way he had once said I'll go in the Chamber of Oaths.
"I won't be king," he said. "Not of Kosala, and not of the south, and not of the Janasthan or the sea. You don't need one. You need a council. Of the wards and the coast and the schoolhouses and the fields. Of people who can read the permits and the engine-loads and the Board's codes, and who will argue about them in public until they're sure the trees are safe. You need a clerk who'll read the names of the men who signed the permits aloud, every morning, until the square is tired of hearing them. And you need a child who can draw a face on a rope, and be told by someone who loves her that it wasn't a brave man."
The square was silent.
"I'll sit at the table," said Ram, "if you ask me to. I'll answer the questions I'm asked, and I'll tell you when I'm wrong. But I won't be the one who decides what the sky is for. That's yours. It always was. I only forgot, for a while, that I was allowed to put it down."
Somebody at the front of the crowd began to clap.
It was a single pair of hands, slow and hesitant. Then a second pair. Then a third. And then the whole square was clapping, not the rolling thunder of the hanging-day, but a steady, uneven, thoughtful sound, the sound of people who had been given something and were not yet sure what it was worth.
Ram did not move. He stood on the platform and let them clap. He did not feel the pulse in his wrist at all. He only felt the stone under his bare feet, and the warm evening air on his face, and the weight of his own body, which was tired and thin and bearing, for the first time in two years, nothing but itself.
---
The council sat for the first time three days later, at a long table made from the Board's old conference desk, carried down from the glass offices and set out in the courtyard of the Chamber of Oaths, under the open sky.
There were nineteen chairs. Nobody had argued about the number. Some were from the Board's old offices, and some were stools from the kitchens, and one was the high chair from the Chamber itself, which Dasharatha had sat in for thirty years. Bharat had carried it out himself. He had set it at the far end of the table, and sat down in it for a moment, very still, and then got up again and walked to the nearest stool. Nobody saw him sit in the high chair after that.
Ram sat at the table. He had been asked to, and had said yes.
The first question was about the forest. The second was about the wards. The third was about the schoolhouses on the Sarayu bank, which had no roofs. The fourth was about the men who had sat on the Board's council, and what should be done with them now that they had stopped confessing and started asking for pensions.
Harit was not at the table. He sat on the stone steps at the edge of the courtyard, with his leather case beside him and his black coat unbuttoned, and he read the answers as they were written, aloud, to the crowd beyond the gate. He read them slowly, in the same flat precise voice he had once used on a glass-room stair to offer the north its own kind of justice.
He did not look up once.
Near the end of the day, Sita came out of the vault-stair and stood at the edge of the courtyard, under the old arch, and listened. She did not come in. She only stood there, very straight, with her hands folded in front of her and her face calm and grave. When the council voted to open the palace gardens to the wards, she closed her eyes.
When it voted to open the vault, she opened them again.
The vault was dug out of living rock, and the men who had dug it were dead. The air moved now, for the first time in a thousand years, because the old grille had been taken off its hinges that morning, and the cold dry air had come rushing up the stone stair in a long slow sigh. The clerk Anand had walked down the steps with a basket of bread, and found the room empty, and sat down on the stone bench, and wept.
Sita came to stand beside Ram at the head of the table. She rested her hand lightly on his shoulder. She did not look at the chairs, or the stone, or the square beyond the gate. She only looked at the faces of the council, one by one, with the same grave, unhurried, measuring attention she had once given a prince at the foot of a dais at Mithila.
"You're going to be very tired," she said quietly, when the vote was done.
"Yes," said Ram.
"And very wrong, quite often."
"Yes."
"And there'll be a day, years from now, when someone stands in this courtyard and says the house was better when it had a single hand on the glass." She turned her head and looked at him, and her eyes were dark and steady and very kind. "Will you be able to bear it?"
Ram thought about it. He thought about a grey disc glowing green in his palm, and a copper-eyed man falling through glass. He thought about a pair of worn sandals set side by side on a wet stone. He thought about a square full of people sitting on the ground with their bowls of rice, listening to a clerk read their own names aloud.
"I'll bear it," he said. "I'll have to. But I won't be alone with it any more. That's the difference."
Sita smiled. It was small and tired and entirely genuine.
"Four, two, six," she said softly.
"Four, two, six," said Ram.
---
On the morning of the fortieth day, the engines on the north rampart ran their test.
Ram stood on the roof of the east spire in bare feet and let the cool of the tiles climb his shins. He had been awake since the fourth hour. He had not slept. He had sat at the window with a lamp burning on the sill and a stack of permits in his lap, reading the names of the men who had signed them, one by one, until the light came up grey over the Sarayu.
The hum came rolling across the citadel from the north wall, a long low sound that he felt in his chest before he heard it. The glass of the spire rang faintly in its frames.
It did not shake him. It only sounded like a bell someone had struck, once, and was letting ring out over the city in the early light.
The barrels on the rampart did not turn. They stayed exactly where they had been left, pointed at the empty fields beyond the moat, and the men who tended them stood at their posts with their hands loose at their sides, watching the sky. There was no panel on the wall. There was no black glass set into the stone, waiting for a pulse. The old control room beneath the rampart had been emptied, its iron ribs stripped and its floor of cracked tile lifted and laid in the garden of the Chamber, where the schoolchildren were planting marigolds between the stones.
Below him, the city was waking.
The first freight pods were sliding along the highways. The schoolhouses on the Sarayu bank were opening their doors, and the children were coming out in small chattering crowds, carrying their slates and their satchels and their bright new copies of the decrees. Somewhere near the fountain in the square, a clerk in a grey coat was setting out a folding table and a stack of permits, and a crowd was beginning, very slowly, to gather round it.
Ram did not look down at them. He could hear them. It was a low, uneven, growing sound, the murmur of people beginning to speak to one another in the early light.
He thought of the mortal choice, the old phrase from a book he had read as a boy in the palace schools. He had always thought it meant a single moment, the instant a man stood at the edge of a stair and decided whether to go up or down. He understood now that it had never been a single moment at all. It was a stair. It was a long one, and nobody reached the bottom in one step, and the choosing was not something you did once and then forgot. It was something you went on doing, in every room you entered, with every name you heard, every time someone looked at you and waited to see whether you would put your hand on the glass.
It was tiring.
It was also, he thought, the only thing a man could ever really do.
He closed his eyes. He breathed in, slow and cool and clean, and held it for a moment, and let it out.
Four. Two. Six.
The count came, as it always did now. It was not a trick or a cage. It was a thing he had learned long ago and was only beginning to understand. It was not a promise that the city would be safe, or that the next panel he touched would stay dark, or that the men who had once wanted the sky would never come up the stair again. It was only a way of listening to his own heart, in a room full of voices, and knowing when it was steady enough to speak.
Below him, in the square, a child's voice rose above the murmur of the crowd, clear and high and entirely unafraid.
"Is it true?" the child asked. "What the old man read? That there was a king who held the sky up for everybody, and it never fell?"
There was a pause. Then the clerk's voice answered, flat and tired and honest.
"It's true for the people who needed it," said Harit. "That's the only kind of true there is. The rest is just the men with their shovels."
Ram opened his eyes.
Far to the south, beyond the dead forest and the burned ledger and the sea-line, a white flyer was crossing the blue water toward an island where a bridge of salvaged rails ran over a lagoon, and a schoolhouse stood on the foundations of an old wire fence. In the sun, at a long wooden desk, a young man in a grey coverall was reading out a list of names.
And here, on the roof of the east spire, in the first grey light of the morning, a man in bare feet stood at the rail with his hands loose at his sides, and listened to the city wake, and did not once look away from it.
It did not fall.
It never had.
It only needed to be looked at, by someone willing to keep looking, every single morning, for as long as it took.
