Book One: The Shadow of Choice
Sargas follow the road taken. Passages marked SHADOW ROAD follow the other path: the same nights, under a different hand on the glass.
---
CHAPTER I: The Chamber of Oaths
> **[THE ROAD TAKEN]**
The glass of the east spire took the first light before anything else in Ayodhya did, and Ram had learned as a boy to read the day in it. A pale lozenge crept down the far bank of the Sarayu. The shadows of the hundred-killer batteries stretched across the ramparts like the legs of something sleeping and not yet decided whether to wake. Below the spire the grid of the city lay in its eight-by-eight blocks, each one lit from within by its own cold blue, and the highways between them were already humming with the first freight pods of the morning, sprinkled with water by the old municipal sprayers so the dust would not rise and cling to the windows of the Board's towers.
He stood on the roof garden in bare feet and let the cool of the tiles climb his shins. He was fifteen years old and had been awake since the fourth hour, because the engines on the north rampart ran their morning test at the fifth, and the test shook the glass in its frames, and he had never once slept through it without dreaming of falling.
Below him, on the practice range that the house had built out over the old well-court, his brother was shooting.
Lakshman did not look up when Ram came to stand at the rail. He drew, held, released. The arrow went into the hanging hide at the far end of the range with a sound like a door closing in an empty house, and then he drew again before the first arrow had finished its small shiver in the target.
"You are standing on the wrong side of the wind," Lakshman said.
Ram moved one step to the left. The air came off the river warm and smelling of silt and something sweeter underneath, the smell of the marigold barges that had already begun to slide upstream toward the flower markets.
"Better," Lakshman said, and loosed again.
They had been brothers for fifteen years and friends for most of them, which was a thing Ram did not think about, because thinking about it made it sound like an arrangement. Lakshman was built like a lever: short through the trunk, all force in the forearms and the set of the jaw. He was a year and a half older than Ram by the palace's records and had never once let Ram forget it, even on the days when the palace records were wrong and Lakshman knew they were wrong and said it anyway, because the wrongness was a thing they could share.
"You were up for the test," Lakshman said. He had a way of talking while he shot, as though the bowstring had a voice that needed to be spoken over.
"Yes."
"You're always up for the test."
"It shakes the glass."
"It shakes everything. It shook my tooth loose when I was nine." Lakshman drew, and this time he held for a long moment, the bow not quite still, the tendon of his arm trembling. Then he released. The arrow went wide of the hide and struck the stone lip of the well-court with a sharp crack. He lowered the bow and breathed out through his nose.
"Your hand," Ram said.
"My hand is fine."
It was not fine. Ram could see the knuckles, white. Lakshman had been drawing too hard for a week, since the news from the south had begun to arrive in the evening feeds: the brokers who bought forest acreage from the Dandaka townships, and the burned hermitages on the edge of the salt flats, and the people who had walked out of the ruined villages toward the city with nothing but their children and their dead.
"Sumant is coming up," Lakshman said, without looking toward the stair. "I can hear his boots."
Ram heard them too, a moment later: the heavy, measured tread of the king's minister on the iron steps, the particular rhythm of a man who was carrying bad news in both hands and did not want to spill it.
---
Sumant came out onto the roof with the morning on his shoulders and a tablet pressed against his chest. He was grey at the temples and had the sallow look of a man who had not slept in a bed since the last council session. He bowed to Ram, and then, more carefully, to Lakshman, because Lakshman was the one who did not forgive a bow given carelessly.
"Prince," Sumant said. "The King asks for you in the Chamber of Oaths. Both of you. Immediately, if you can."
"Is there trouble at the south gate?" Lakshman asked.
"There is a man at the south gate."
"A man."
Sumant's mouth tightened. "An ascetic. He walked across the causeway from the river road sometime before the fifth hour, without a vehicle, without a pass. The gate scanner reads him as an unregistered religious class. The guards did not know whether to lower the outer barrier or call the Board's security office. They called us. The Board's office is still asleep."
"Who is he?" Ram asked.
Sumant looked at him for a moment before he answered, and in the look Ram saw the thing the minister had been carrying since the night before. It was not fear. It was the careful blankness that came over the faces of the palace officials when a matter had moved beyond their power to shape it.
"He says he is the sage who stands at the Siddhashrama," Sumant said. "He says he has come for you."
---
The south gate was a mile from the palace by the inner roads, and they took the inner roads because the outer roads were full of freight, and because Ram had no wish to be seen running through the streets of the citadel like a boy who had been told something frightening. They ran anyway, down the long glass-walled arcade that connected the spire to the old citadel's southern ward, past the dormitory blocks where the palace's lower servants were beginning to stir behind their pale frosted windows, past the shuttered armoury where a single red light blinked over a door that had not been opened in three generations.
The sun was fully up when they reached the causeway. The outer moat lay on their right, black and wide and dotted with the grey rafts of the moat-engineers' drones, which rose and fell on their hidden lift like mechanical herons. The water smelled of rust and algae. On the far side of the moat the river road ran south through the scrubby fields toward the Sarayu crossing, and on the river road, in the full glare of the morning, a single figure stood.
He was not tall. He was thin in the way that old men are thin when they have stopped expecting their bodies to be useful, and his cotton was the colour of ash, and he leaned on a staff of dark wood with a steel ferrule at its foot. There was no bag. There was no escort. Behind him the fields were empty to the horizon, and the heat was already rising off them in long wavering sheets.
The gate guards stood on the causeway's inner end with their rifles held across their chests. They had the stiff, uncertain postures of men who have been told to hold a line and have not been told what they are holding it against. When Ram came up behind them, one of them turned, and the relief on his face was so naked that Ram almost looked away.
"Prince," the guard said. "He won't come through the scanner. He won't go back, either."
"Open the outer barrier."
The guard hesitated. "The Board's office—"
"The Board's office is asleep," Ram said. "Open the barrier."
The barrier rose with a hiss of hydraulics. The old man on the river road did not move. He watched Ram walk out across the causeway, past the drones, past the first of the moat lamps that had not yet been switched off for the day, and when Ram stopped at the edge of the paved road, the old man lifted his head and looked at him for a long time, as though he were reading a long document written in a hand he had once known well.
"You are the eldest," the sage said.
"I am Ram, son of the King."
"I know who you are. I asked whether you were the eldest."
"Yes."
"Good," the sage said. He shifted his weight on the staff. His voice was dry and light, the voice of someone who had spent many years speaking to the wind. "Then your father owes me a debt, and you owe your father an answer, and I do not have time to stand in this heat waiting for either of you to decide what to do with them."
---
The Chamber of Oaths was the oldest room in the citadel that still had a window. The rest of the palace's rooms had been sealed years ago and fitted with climate glass and the soft shadowless light of the Board's ceilings, but the Chamber had been left as it was: a high vaulted hall of pale stone, with a single arched window at the far end that looked out over the Sarayu, and in the centre of the floor a slab of black basalt the length of a man, its surface worn smooth by the palms of generations of petitioners. The house's boons had been carved into it in the old script before anyone had thought to record them anywhere else. Now the carvings were filled with dust, and a clerk came once a month with a soft brush to clear them, though no one could read them any more except the high priest and the king.
Dasharatha sat in the high chair at the far end of the hall with his hands in his lap. Ram had not seen him in daylight for six weeks, and the change in him was like the change in a riverbed after the monsoon has gone. The king's face had sunk in on itself. His hands were swollen at the knuckles, and he kept them still because they had started, lately, to shake when he held them out. He wore a plain grey tunic and no ring. Beside him, on a stool carved from the same black stone as the oath-slab, sat the high priest Vasishtha, his white beard combed flat, his eyes half-closed, his lips moving very slightly as though he were counting something under his breath.
Sumant stood at the king's left with his tablet clutched in both hands. Lakshman stood at Ram's shoulder. Behind them, at the back of the hall near the pillar where the petitioners were supposed to wait, a single guard held the great doors shut against the morning noise of the citadel.
The sage stood in the centre of the room, on the worn place where the petitioners stood. He did not bow. He had not bowed to Ram at the causeway, and he did not bow now. He had leaned his staff against the basalt slab and was looking up at the vault with the air of a man who had walked into a cathedral he had not expected to find so tall.
"You have a great deal of stone," he said.
"It was my grandfather's wish," Dasharatha said. His voice was hoarse, as though he had been speaking all night. "The stone does not lie. The Board's walls do."
"The stone is also very cold," the sage said. "Your guards would do well to bring blankets to the petitioners."
Sumant's jaw moved. The king lifted one swollen hand from his lap and let it fall again. "You have asked for my son," he said.
"I have not asked. I have come to tell you what I need, and you may decide whether to give it to me."
"Then tell me."
The sage turned his head slowly, and the light from the arched window fell across the hollows of his face and made them deep. "The Siddhashrama is my hermitage," he said. "It was built on the bank of the old Vamana tirtha, before the factories came up the river and turned the water to tea. For thirty years my people have kept the forest around it. We have kept it for the ascetics who come there to meditate, and for the animals who come to drink, and for the children of the villages who come to pick the berries that grow along the western wall. Three months ago two brokers from the salt-road towns sent men into the forest with stripping machines. They have been taking the trees. They have been burning the villages that will not sell. The people who lived in them are walking north to your gates. You will have seen them on the feeds."
"We have seen them," Vasishtha said. His eyes did not open.
"Then you know the men who are coming," the sage said. "The brokers are called Mari and Suka. They have paid the local militia to look away and they have bought the rogue syndicate that runs the salt pans. Their armament is clumsy but it is numerous. And there is a woman at the edge of the salt flats, whom the villagers call the Salt Widow, who came out of the dead country three years ago and has been eating the stripped men. She is not a broker. She is not a militia. She is not a machine. I cannot stop her with prayers, and I cannot stop her with sticks. I need a hand that can hold a bow and a mind that does not shake when it is afraid."
Dasharatha closed his eyes. When he opened them again, he was looking at Ram, and Ram saw in his father's face a thing he had seen only once before, on the night of his mother's funeral: a naked, unmanageable grief, held down by a lifetime of duty so tightly that it had begun to split the skin.
"He is a boy," the king said. "He is fifteen years old. You are asking me to send my son into the dead country with a man who walks across causeways without a pass."
"I am asking for one boy," the sage said. "Your towers hold ten thousand men. Your engines are the finest in the three worlds. Your Board sits in glass rooms and counts the dead by the hour. You have a city that could drown the Dandaka forest in a single night, and you ask me why I come to you with one boy. I come to you with one boy because one boy is what I can carry."
"And if he dies?"
"Then he will have died for something that needed doing," the sage said, "which is more than the brokers can say about their money."
Lakshman made a low sound in his throat. Ram put a hand on his arm without looking at him, and felt the tendons under the skin go tight and then, slowly, loosen.
Vasishtha opened his eyes. His gaze went from the king to the sage and back again, and it did not hurry. "The King's house has a duty to the forests of Kosala," the high priest said. "It is written on that stone. It is older than the Board and older than the towers. The duty of protecting the sacrifice-grounds was given to the house of Ikshvaku by the first of the Manus, and no minister and no corporation has ever had the right to revoke it. The sage has come to remind us of what the stone says."
"The stone says nothing about boys," Dasharatha said.
"The stone says a great deal about sons," Vasishtha said, very quietly. "Your father was a son. So were you."
The king's hands tightened in his lap. For a moment the hall was so silent that Ram could hear the faint shriek of the hundred-killers' hydraulics on the north rampart, a sound like a far-off bird that had been hurt, and then the high priest's stool scraped on the stone floor as he shifted his weight, and the sound was gone.
Ram stepped forward.
He did not decide to. His feet took him three paces across the cold floor, to the place where the stone's shadow lay across the edge of the worn petitioners' square, and he stopped there, because it seemed to him the place a son should stand when his father could not look at him.
"I'll go," he said.
Dasharatha's face changed. For a heartbeat it was relief, plain and enormous, washing across the sunken features like light across the Sarayu when a cloud breaks. Then it was shame, which came in behind the relief and displaced it, and Ram watched his father's eyes drop to the swollen hands in his lap and stay there.
"You need not," the king said.
"I know," Ram said.
"You have not been asked by anyone with the right to ask."
"I have been asked by a man who walked across a causeway in the heat to tell us what is burning." Ram did not look away from the king's hands. "I will go."
"Ram—" Lakshman began, from behind him.
"I'll go," Ram said again, and this time he turned his head, and his brother's eyes met his across the width of the hall, and whatever Lakshman saw there, he closed his mouth and did not say the rest.
The sage picked up his staff from where it leaned against the black stone. He tapped its steel foot once on the floor, a small flat sound that rang briefly under the vault.
"Good," he said. "The heat will be worse at noon. We should leave before the engines start."
---
The palace clerks had the boy's kit packed by the eighth hour. It was not much. Ram had never owned much, and the things he had were mostly books, and the books were on the palace feeds now and could be called up in any room that had a screen, so there was no point carrying them. He took two shirts of undyed cotton, a pair of trousers for the forest, a knife with a bone handle that had been his grandfather's, a cloth bag of dried fruit and the flat grain-cakes the kitchen made for the lower ward's laborers, and a small brass lamp that did not need a battery, because the oil in it burned clean and the clerks had said that a lamp of that kind might be useful in a country where the power grids had been cut.
The sage waited for him in the palace courtyard, beside the old well. He had been given a cup of water by the kitchen boy, and he was sitting on the lip of the well with his feet in the dust, looking up at the glass of the east spire as though it were a mountain he had once climbed and did not intend to climb again.
Ram came out across the courtyard with the bag on his back. Behind him, on the palace steps, his mother's maid stood watching with her hands clasped tight under her chin. He did not look at her long. He had already said his goodbyes in the women's wing, and they had not gone well, because his mother had asked him whether he wanted to go, and he had not been able to answer her truthfully, and she had turned away from him and begun to cry without making any noise.
"You're going to walk," Lakshman said. He had followed Ram out of the palace with his own pack slung over one shoulder and his face set hard as sun-baked brick.
"Yes."
"In this heat."
"He says the heat will be worse at noon."
"He says a great many things." Lakshman looked at the sage, who did not turn his head. "Are you going to tell me why I should not come with you?"
"No," Ram said. "You should come with me."
Lakshman let out a breath. The tension that had been holding his shoulders stiff all morning went out of them, a little, and then came back in a different form: a hard, watchful stillness, the look of a man who has decided to guard something he has not yet been asked to guard.
"Your father will not last the year," Lakshman said quietly.
"He might."
"He will not. I have seen the reports from the medical wing. You have not."
Ram said nothing. There was nothing he could say that Lakshman did not already know. He had been told the same things by the physicians in a language stripped of anything that could be called hope. Kidney function. Blood pressure. The fibrosis spreading through the hands. A year, perhaps two, if the king kept to the diet and sat in the cool of the Chamber each morning and did not argue with his ministers.
He had not told his father what the physicians said. His father had not asked.
"Come on," the sage said, rising from the well. He picked up his staff and set it against his shoulder, and without waiting to see whether they followed, he walked toward the palace gate.
They followed.
---
Behind them, as they crossed the courtyard, the hundred-killers on the north rampart began their morning test. The sound came rolling across the citadel in a long low hum that Ram felt in his chest before he heard it, and the glass of the east spire rang faintly in its frames, as it did every morning, like a bell somebody had struck once and forgotten to still.
He did not look back at the spire. He had learned, as a boy, that the glass would be there whether he looked at it or not.
---
CHAPTER : SARGA II: The Southern Road
> **[THE ROAD TAKEN]**
The road to the Sarayu crossing had once been a highway for pilgrims and merchants, and it still wore the bones of that old life along its shoulders: stone milestones with the names of forgotten villages worn to smudges, a dry stone well every few miles with a carved lintel that had cracked in half, a line of ancient banyan trees along the eastern verge whose roots had lifted the paving in slow, patient waves. The municipal sprayers had given up on this stretch years ago. The dust lay thick and pale on the asphalt, and where the morning breeze caught it, it rose in soft clouds that clung to the sweat on Ram's neck and arms and tasted of chalk.
The sage walked ahead of them. He did not hurry and he did not slow. His bare feet moved over the broken paving with the economy of long practice, and his staff struck the ground at a steady interval, a small dry knock that carried in the still air and seemed to set the rhythm of the morning.
Lakshman walked behind Ram. He had not spoken since the palace gate, except once, to curse the glare when it came off the fields in a sudden white sheet that made both of them squint. Now and then he looked back at the city, and each time he looked back the city was smaller. The spires had been the first thing to shrink. By the second hour they were no more than a series of pale teeth on the northern horizon, catching the sun. By the third they had sunk into the haze and become a rumour of glass.
The heat came up off the road in waves. Ram had been warned about it. He had read the figures on the feeds every summer since he was old enough to read: the temperature at the Dandaka boundary, the humidity at the Janasthana outposts, the percentage of rural wells that had gone dry since the brokers began pumping the aquifers for their salt-pan operations. He had read them the way he read the Board's quarterly reports, as numbers that described a country somewhere else. Now the country was under his feet. The heat pressed on his scalp and crawled down the back of his neck and settled in the hollows of his knees, and with every mile it grew heavier, until he felt that he was walking through something solid, a thick warm liquid that gave way reluctantly to each step and closed behind him as he passed.
By the fourth hour his legs had begun to burn. It started in the calves and climbed into the thighs, a dull ache that became a sharp ache and then a steady, grinding fire. He did not say anything. He watched the sage's feet and tried to match his stride to the knock of the staff, and found that the knock was going faster than his heart.
The sage stopped at the crest of a low rise where the road bent around a stand of tamarind trees. The wind moved in the branches above them, and for a moment the shade was cool and smelled of sour fruit. The sage set his staff down and turned to face them.
"You are walking as though the road has insulted you," he said.
"My legs hurt," Ram said.
"Yes."
"Is that a complaint?"
The sage smiled. It was not an unkind smile, but it was not warm either. It was the smile of a man who has spent a long time being asked foolish questions and has decided, at last, to stop taking offence at them. "No," he said. "It is a fact. Facts are not complaints. Sit down."
Ram sat. The bark of the tamarind was rough against his back and the ground under him was packed and dry. Lakshman sat beside him without being asked, and the sage sat across from them with his legs folded under him and his hands resting on his knees.
"Close your eyes," the sage said.
"Why?"
"Because you cannot see what your legs are doing while you are looking at me."
Ram closed his eyes.
"Now breathe in. Count four. Hold for two. Breathe out for six. Do not hurry the count. The count is not a race."
Ram breathed. The air came into his chest cool and dusty. He held it. He let it go, slowly, feeling it leave his lungs in a long unwinding thread. He breathed again.
"Again," the sage said. "Do not think about the legs. Think about the count. Four, two, six."
They did it for a long time. Ram lost track of how many breaths he had taken. The wind came and went in the tamarind leaves, and somewhere in the scrub a bird was calling in a soft, repetitive whistle, like someone trying to remember a tune. Lakshman's breathing beside him slowed and steadied. After a while Ram realised that the fire in his calves had dropped to a low hum, the way the hundred-killers' hydraulics dropped to a hum after the morning test, and he was not sure at what moment it had happened.
"Open your eyes," the sage said.
Ram opened them. The sage had not moved. The light through the leaves made a shifting pattern across his face.
"Stand up," the sage said.
Ram stood. His legs held. They ached, and they were heavy, but the sharp grinding fire had gone, and when he took a step forward, the step did not hurt.
"How?" Lakshman asked. He was staring at Ram's feet as though they had done something impossible.
"It is not magic," the sage said. He picked up his staff. "It is breath. Breath tells the body what the mind is afraid of. If the mind is afraid, the body tenses, and the tensing burns. If the body is calm, it burns less. It is a simple arrangement. It is also very hard to keep."
"Is that what you taught the kings of Ayodhya?" Lakshman asked, a little sharply.
"I have never taught a king anything," the sage said mildly. "Kings do not like to be taught. They like to be obeyed, and they like to be told that what they do is right. I have found that a man who does not know how to breathe is not much use to a king, and a king who does not know how to breathe is worse than none at all."
Lakshman opened his mouth, then closed it again. Something in the old man's voice had shut the argument before it could begin.
They walked on.
---
The afternoon came on with the slow, bruised heaviness of the dry season. The road ran south through fields that had been ploughed and abandoned, and then through scrubland where the grass had gone the colour of old bone. The few villages they passed were quiet. Women looked up from their courtyards as the three of them went by, their faces wary, their children half-hidden behind the pillars of the house-gates. The men were mostly gone, to the salt flats or to the brokers' camps or to the city, and the old men who remained sat in the shade of the wells and watched the strangers with the flat patience of people who had long ago stopped expecting anything from the road.
Near the third milestone a cart had been abandoned in the ditch on the western verge. Its wheels had been taken and its bed had been split open, and the grain that had been in it lay in a pale spilled heap in the dust, untouched by the birds. Nearby, under a banyan, a child's sandal had been left on the ground. Ram looked at it for a long time as they passed. It was a small sandal, red leather, the strap mended with thread that had been pulled tight and knotted in a neat double loop, the kind of knot a mother ties when she is in a hurry and wants the job done before her child notices it has come loose.
He did not stop. The sage did not look at it. Lakshman, behind him, made a small noise, and then said nothing for a mile.
The Sarayu appeared an hour before sunset, a wide brown band between dark banks of tamarisk and reed. The road ran down toward it through a long gentle slope, and at the bottom of the slope, where the old stone ghat had once been built for the bathing of pilgrims, the water lay still and milky in the failing light.
It did not look like a river. It looked like a sheet of tea poured out across the land and left to cool. The surface had a faint oily sheen, broken here and there by bright shards of something that caught the last of the sun and flashed, and the smell that came up from it was sharp and metallic, a smell that caught at the back of the throat and stayed there.
Ram stopped at the top of the ghat steps. Below him the stone stairs went down into the water, the lowest of them slick with a greenish film. A few dead fish floated near the edge, white-bellied, their mouths open. A tin basin lay on its side on the second step, half-full of brown water, and a line of washing hung from a rope strung between two posts at the head of the ghat. The clothes were grey with a fine dust that had settled on them and never been washed away.
Three people were bathing in the shallows. An old man with his back to the shore, scrubbing his shoulders with a fistful of ash. A woman in a faded sari, up to her waist in the water, with a baby held against her chest and her face turned down toward it as she whispered something Ram could not hear. And a younger man, standing thigh-deep and pouring water over his head from a brass pot, his eyes closed, his lips moving in a steady, half-audible chant.
They were bathing in it. They had been bathing in it for years, Ram thought, and would bathe in it tomorrow, because the river was the only water that came to the ghat, and the people who lived here had no other place to go.
"They know it's poisoned," Lakshman said quietly beside him.
"Yes."
"Then why—"
"Because they are pilgrims," the sage said. He had come up the steps behind them and stood now with his staff across his shoulders, looking down at the water. "Because the river is where their dead were carried. Because the gods are still in the water, even if the water has been cut off from them. Because there is nowhere else to wash the ashes of a grandmother, and because the factories upriver have their pumps on day and night, and because no one with a pump has ever come down to this ghat to ask what the pilgrims are doing here."
He turned and started down the steps. He walked into the shallows without hesitating and stood there, the water rising to his knees, his ash-grey cotton darkening to a dull brown at the hem. He bent and scooped up a handful of the water, and looked at it for a long moment in the fading light.
Then he let it fall through his fingers.
"Come down," he said. "Wash your feet. We will camp on the high bank tonight."
---
They camped on the high bank above the ghat, in the lee of a broken temple wall that had been built of red brick and had lost most of its carvings to weather and to the hands of people who had needed something to sell. The sage built a small fire of dry tamarisk wood without asking for help. The flames rose blue and then yellow, and the smoke went up in a thin straight line into the still air, and when the last light went out of the sky, the stars came up above them in a thick dusty band that Ram had never seen from the city.
They ate the grain-cakes from Ram's bag, and some dried fruit that the sage produced from somewhere in his cotton folds, and a handful of salted nuts that Lakshman had bought from a vendor at the village three miles back and had been carrying in his shirt ever since. Nobody spoke for a while. The fire popped. Out on the river a fish broke the surface with a sound like a stone dropped into a bucket, and the rings spread out slowly across the milky water and were gone.
"What did the king want you to say?" the sage asked.
Ram looked up. The sage was not looking at him. He was looking at the fire, his face lit gold on one side and dark on the other.
"Nothing," Ram said.
"Then you have already learned something."
"What?"
"That a man can be asked to say a thing without being told what it is," the sage said. "That is a skill. Most people learn it late, if they learn it at all."
Lakshman set down the nuts he had been eating. "My father is dying," he said abruptly. He did not say it to the sage. He said it to the fire, in a flat tone that was not quite anger.
"The King is dying," the sage said. "The man who taught you to pull a bow is something else."
"What's the difference?"
"One is a title. The other is a debt."
Lakshman was silent for a long time. He picked up a stick from the pile beside him and began to break it into smaller and smaller pieces, dropping each piece carefully into the fire. The sage watched him do it without comment.
"You are angry," the sage said at last.
"Yes," Lakshman said.
"At whom?"
Lakshman's hand stopped. He looked at Ram across the fire. There was a question in his face that he did not ask, and Ram understood it, because it was the same question he had been carrying for six weeks in the palace and on the feeds and in the quiet hours when the hundred-killers were silent.
Who was going to pay for this? Who would be made to answer for the king's hands, and the broken oath, and the boy sent south with a stranger to walk into the dead country? And who, in the end, would have the power to decide?
"I am not angry at anyone," Lakshman said. "I am tired of being told that the world is a place where everyone has to pay for everything."
The sage smiled very faintly. "You will find that the world agrees with you about the tiredness," he said. "It is not so sure about the rest."
He leaned forward and placed a single stick of tamarisk on the fire. The flames took it, and for a moment they burned brighter, and in that brighter light Ram saw the sage's face very clearly: lined and dry and weathered by the sun, but not unkind, and not tired in the way that Lakshman was tired. It was the tiredness of a man who had long since stopped expecting the world to change its mind.
"Sleep," the sage said. "Tomorrow we reach the edge of the forest. The forest does not care whether you have slept. You will need to have slept anyway."
Ram lay down on the packed earth with his bag beneath his head and the stars wheeling slowly above him. The fire crackled and went low. Somewhere out on the river the fish were still breaking the surface, one after another, in long slow rhythms, the sound of something that had been living in the water for a long time and did not intend to stop.
Before he slept, he counted his breaths. Four, two, six. The count came easily. It did not feel like a trick. It felt like something he had always known and had only now remembered, the way the body remembers a road it has walked as a child.
He did not know, lying there, that the count would be the thing he would need most in the years to come. He knew only that his legs no longer hurt, and that the water had been very still, and that somewhere to the north, beyond the haze and the glass, the city he had left behind was humming in its sleep, its engines testing, its towers turning their shining faces toward the south.
---
CHAPTER : SARGA III: Siddhashrama
> **[THE ROAD TAKEN]**
The forest began as a rumour of shade and became, over the course of a morning, a wall.
It did not look like the forests in the old feeds. The feeds had shown canopies, tangled and green, with monkeys leaping through the branches and light falling down in long shafts through the leaves. What Ram saw from the top of the last rise was a ridge of broken teak and dead sal, their trunks stripped to the grey wood, their crowns sheared off at the height of a man's reach. Between the stumps the ground had been raked into long furrows, and the furrows had filled with a fine white dust that caught the morning light and threw it back in a glare that made his eyes water.
"Salt," Lakshman said. He crouched and drew a finger through the dust, and held it up to the light. The grains glittered. "They've been boiling the ground."
"Not the ground," the sage said. He had not stopped walking. "The roots. The roots hold water. The brokers want the water. They take the trees to get at the roots, and the roots they boil in pans, and what is left is this."
He went down the slope into the dead forest without looking back. The dust rose around his feet in small, soft puffs. Ram followed him. Lakshman came after, brushing the salt from his fingers on his trousers, his mouth set.
They walked for an hour through the stripped ground. Nothing moved. There were no birds. Once, far to the east, a machine started up with a rising whine, and then stopped, and the silence afterward was so complete that Ram could hear his own pulse in his ears. Once they passed a fire-pit, cold and grey, with a row of tin cups set neatly along its rim. Once they passed a stake driven into the ground with a strip of red cloth knotted to its top, flapping in the hot wind, and beneath it a small hollow in the dust where something had been dug up and removed.
The sage stopped beside the stake. He looked at the cloth for a long time. Then he lifted his staff and touched the top of the stake with the ferrule, very gently, the way a man might touch the forehead of a sleeping child.
"A marker," he said. "For the next line of stripping. They put them out in the morning, so the men know where to cut before the sun is too high."
"Who taught them that?" Lakshman asked.
"The brokers. Who else?"
They went on.
---
Siddhashrama stood on the far bank of a narrow channel that had once been a stream and was now a bed of cracked mud, strewn with the bleached ribs of dead catfish. The hermitage had been built in the old style: a rough circle of stone walls, each one shoulder-high and capped with flat slabs, enclosing a courtyard of packed earth. Inside the walls stood a dozen low huts thatched with dried grass, and a single larger structure at the centre, with a shrine on its roof and a pair of carved doors that had been painted, once, with blue and yellow, and had now faded to the colour of old bruises. A well stood in the courtyard, its rim worn smooth by ropes, and beside it a row of clay pots had been set out to dry in the sun.
The walls had been breached on the western side. The stones had been pulled down and piled in a rough heap, and the heap had been scorched black, as though a fire had been lit against it and allowed to burn for a long time. Ash lay in drifts along the inner face of the wall. Someone had swept some of it into a neat pile and left it there, beside a child's wooden toy, a horse with one wheel missing.
Ram saw the children before he saw the adults. They were on the far side of the courtyard, in a patch of shade under a neem tree, sitting in a half-circle on the ground. There were perhaps nine of them, ranging from a girl of about seven to a boy who could not have been more than three. They were sorting berries into piles, dark purple ones into one heap and pale green ones into another, and they did not look up when the three strangers came through the breach in the wall. One of the older girls glanced at them, and then looked away very quickly, and went on sorting.
A woman came out of the central building as the sage approached. She was tall and thin, with grey hair braided tightly down her back and the same ash-coloured cotton as the sage, though hers was torn at the hem and mended in several places with a thread that did not match. She carried a clay bowl in both hands, and when she saw the sage, she stopped, and the bowl trembled very slightly, and a little of the water inside slopped over the rim onto the packed earth.
"You came back," she said.
"I said I would," the sage said.
"You said you would come back in a month. It has been three."
"Then I am late. Nalini, this is Ram, son of Dasharatha. And his brother Lakshman."
The woman called Nalini looked at Ram. Her gaze was not unfriendly, but it was measuring, the way a shopkeeper looks at a coin that has been offered to him in the wrong currency. Then she looked at Lakshman, and her eyes narrowed a little.
"The King's sons," she said. "They sent you two?"
"They sent me one," the sage said. "The brother came on his own."
"Did he?" Nalini's mouth moved in something that was not quite a smile. "Then he is a fool, or he is a friend. We will see which."
She turned and went back into the central building without waiting for an answer, and the sage followed her, and Ram stood in the courtyard with the sun on his neck and the children sorting berries in the shade, and for a moment he did not know where to put his hands.
Lakshman came up beside him and let out a long breath.
"She doesn't like us," he said.
"No."
"Good. I don't like her either."
"She is afraid," Ram said.
Lakshman looked at him sideways. "How do you know?"
Ram did not answer. He was watching the girl with the berries, who had stopped sorting and was holding a single dark berry between her thumb and forefinger, turning it slowly in the light, looking at it as though she had forgotten what it was for.
---
The sage called them into the central building after the midday meal. It was cool inside, with thick walls and small windows set high up so that only a little light came through, falling in pale bars across the packed floor. The shrine on the far wall was empty except for a single lamp, unlit, and a bowl of water with a few white petals floating in it. The air smelled of smoke and dried grass and something sharp and green, a herb Ram did not recognise, bundled in a cloth on the windowsill.
On a low wooden table in the centre of the room, the sage had laid out a long bundle of cloth. He unwrapped it slowly. Beneath the cloth lay a row of objects that looked, at first glance, like nothing at all: a set of smooth grey discs, each about the size of a palm, each with a thin silver ring around its rim. Beside them lay a coil of something that might have been cord and might have been wire, and a single cylinder of dark metal, no longer than a finger, with a small ring of pale lights set into one end.
"You have seen the feeds," the sage said. "You know what the Board's armouries look like. You know what the hundred-killers do. You know what a corporation means by a defensive system, and what it means by a weapon that is not a defensive system."
"Yes," Ram said.
"Then you know that most of what the Board owns is made to kill from a distance, by people who will never see what it kills. The engines decide. The men only press the stud."
"Yes."
"These are not like that." The sage picked up one of the grey discs and held it out on his flat palm. "They are made to be carried. They are made to be used by a single person, standing in front of a single enemy, who can see his face. Each one of them can end a life. Each one of them can end many lives. A single one of them, in the wrong hands, can burn the sap out of a forest for ten miles around. I have carried them for thirty years. I have not used them for twenty-two. Take it."
Ram took the disc. It was heavier than it looked, and cold, and the silver ring around its edge was etched with fine lines that caught the light from the window. He turned it over. On the underside, pressed into the grey metal, a series of small indentations ran in a spiral toward the centre. He could feel them with his thumb, like the grooves on a seed.
"What does it do?" Lakshman asked from the doorway. He had not come in. He had leaned against the frame with his arms folded, watching with the flat attentive stillness he used when he was trying not to show that he was interested.
"It is a payload," the sage said. "Kinetic. It holds a charge of compressed air and fine steel in a shell that cannot be opened once it has been armed. When it is released, the charge goes forward in a single column, and the column does what columns do when they meet resistance. It does not care what it hits. It does not forgive. It is one shot. You cannot recall it, and you cannot stop it once it has begun."
"And the arming?" Ram asked.
The sage looked at him with something like approval. "That is the important part," he said.
He reached out and touched the ring of pale lights on the dark cylinder, and they came up one by one, faintly, a pale amber glow that spread across the cylinder's end like breath on a cold window. He picked up the cylinder and held it out.
"This reads the pulse," the sage said. "When you hold it, it takes the beat of your heart through the skin of your palm. If the beat is steady, the lights turn green, and the payload will arm. If the beat is racing, the lights stay red, and the payload will not. It does not matter how strong you are, or how well you aim, or how much you want the thing to happen. It only matters whether your heart is steady when you ask it to be."
"And if I arm it angry?" Ram asked.
"Then it will not arm," the sage said. "It will sit in your hand, and it will be very heavy, and you will understand for the first time that there are things you cannot make happen by force of will alone. Hold it."
Ram took the cylinder. It was cold, and then, slowly, it was warm. The pale lights glowed red. He could see them in the grey light from the window, a line of four small dots, very bright, and below them a faint hum that he felt in the bones of his wrist.
He looked up. The sage was watching him. Lakshman in the doorway had gone still.
The image came to Ram without his asking for it. The stripped forest outside the walls, the dust on the furrows, the marker with its strip of red cloth. The fire-pit with the cups in a neat row. The breach in the hermitage wall, the scorched stones, the child's horse with one wheel missing. The boy of three under the neem tree, and the girl with the dark berry turned between her fingers, and the three men with the cutting machines who had come up the road the week before, who had laughed at the children and called them rats and had not, Ram was sure, been punished for it.
The hum in his wrist grew louder. The red dots flared. Under his palm the cylinder seemed to shiver, very faintly, like something alive and trying to get away.
"You are angry," the sage said quietly.
"Yes," said Ram.
"Good. Now do not be."
"I don't know how."
"You know how. You learned it on the road yesterday, under the tamarind. You learned it in the palace before you were born. You have simply forgotten where you put it."
Ram closed his eyes. Four, two, six. The count came slowly, dragging, like a cart over broken road. The hum did not drop. He counted again. Four. Two. Six. He thought of the sandal with its double-looped knot. He thought of the tooth-marks on a mango he had found once in the kitchen garden, bitten by somebody who had been hungry. He thought of his father's hands in his lap, shaking. He thought of Lakshman's knuckles, white on the bowstring.
Four. Two. Six.
He opened his eyes. The red lights were still red.
"Again," the sage said.
He did it again. And again. The sun moved across the bars of light on the floor. Somewhere in the courtyard a child began to laugh, and then stopped abruptly, as though someone had put a hand over her mouth. Lakshman shifted in the doorway, and his boot scraped the frame, and the sound made the red lights flicker. Ram's palm was sweating. The cylinder was slick against his skin. His heart was loud in his ears, and every beat seemed to pulse through the metal and back into his bones, and every beat was too fast.
Four. Two. Six.
He thought of nothing. He tried to think of nothing. It was harder than the road had been, harder than the bow in his brother's hands, harder than anything he had done in the palace schools with their glass walls and their watching feeds. It was like trying to still a river by standing in it.
Then, without his noticing exactly when it happened, the count stopped being a count. It became a kind of listening. He could hear his own heart, not as a drum, but as a slow intermittent knock against the inside of his chest, the sound of someone at a far gate who had not yet decided whether to come in. He let it knock. He did not answer it. He breathed out, long and slow, and felt the breath leave him, and felt something leave with it.
He opened his eyes.
Under the amber ring of lights, the four red dots had dimmed. One went out. The second flickered to orange. The third went green, and then the fourth, and the cylinder under his palm went still, not cold and not warm, but simply present, as a stone is present in a riverbed, as a tree is present on a hill.
The lights were green.
Ram let out a breath he had not known he was holding. His arm was trembling. His hand ached as though he had been gripping a bow for hours.
"Good," the sage said softly.
"It's armed?" Lakshman asked from the doorway, his voice tight.
"It is armed." The sage reached out and took the cylinder gently from Ram's palm. The moment it left his hand, the lights went out, one after another, down to a single pale spark, which faded and vanished. "It will hold its arming for a little less than an hour. After that, it must be read again. That is the other part of the design. It is not a weapon for a man who has decided on something once and will carry the decision forever. It is a weapon for the moment you are sure."
He set the cylinder down on the table beside the discs. He looked at Ram across the table, and his face was unreadable.
"You will not use this yet," he said. "You are not ready. But you have now held it, and you know what it asks of you. That is the beginning of the lesson. Everything else is learning how to keep the answer."
---
They ate that evening in the courtyard, sitting on the low wall beside the well while the sun went down behind the stripped trees and the dust of the dead forest turned from white to rose to a deep bruised violet. The children had been called in. Nalini brought out a pot of lentils and a basket of flat bread, and she set them down in front of the three visitors without a word, and went back inside.
Lakshman ate in silence, his eyes on the breach in the wall. When he had finished, he wiped his fingers on the grass beside him and said, without looking up: "The sage. What did he do to you in there?"
"He taught me how to count."
"That's not what I asked."
"It's what he did."
Lakshman was quiet for a while. A bat came out of the neem tree and flitted across the courtyard, its wings a soft whisper, and vanished over the wall.
"I tried it," Lakshman said at last. "After you went back in, I tried the counting. I sat by the well and I did it, four and two and six. My hands wouldn't stop shaking."
"They will," Ram said. "It takes time."
"Did you ever stop shaking?"
Ram thought about it. "Only once," he said.
"When?"
"In the chamber. When I said I would go."
Lakshman looked at him then, sharply, and Ram saw in his brother's face the thing he had been trying not to see since the morning: that he had watched Ram stand up in the Chamber of Oaths and say the words, and had seen their father's face change, and had understood, in the same instant, that the thing Ram had just done was not the kind of thing that could be undone.
"You were calm," Lakshman said slowly. "When you said it. I thought you'd lost your mind."
"No."
"What was it, then?"
Ram did not answer. The count had come to him in the chamber, at the moment he stepped forward. He had not planned it. It had arrived in the space between one breath and the next, a single steady beat that had taken the place of the fear, and in that beat he had seen clearly, for the first time in his life, the exact shape of the choice in front of him: a long road south, a stranger's staff tapping on broken paving, a king's hands shaking in his lap. And a city, glass and steel and humming in its sleep, that would go on turning its towers toward whatever it was told to face.
He had not known, until that moment, how much of him had been waiting for someone to give him a reason to be frightened.
"It was quiet," he said. "That's all."
The sage came out of the central building then, and stood for a moment at the edge of the lamplight, looking at the two of them sitting on the wall. He did not come closer. He nodded once, as though something had been confirmed, and went back inside.
---
CHAPTER : SARGA IV: The Salt Widow
> **[THE ROAD TAKEN]**
The brokers came at dusk, on the fourth day, in a line of three machines that threw up dust in long white plumes along the dead ridge. Ram heard them before he saw them: a low grinding roar, rising and falling as the machines crested the furrows and dropped into the hollows between. Then a fourth sound, higher and thinner, like a dragonfly with a bad engine, and above the ridge a small grey shape appeared against the violet sky and began to circle.
"Flyer," Lakshman said. He had already reached for the bow.
"Don't," said the sage, from the shadow of the well.
"They're coming to the wall."
"They are coming to the marker. The marker is the boundary. They want us to see them coming. They want us to run."
"And if we don't?"
"Then they will come closer, and they will look for the reason we are not running," the sage said. "That is their whole method. They are very good at it."
The machines stopped at the foot of the ridge, a hundred paces from the breach. The flyer circled once more, dipped low over the channel, and then settled on the mud beside a rusted tin shed that had been built by the stripping crews as a store. A door opened in the flyer's side, and three men climbed out. They wore grey coveralls and heavy boots and each carried a rifle slung across his chest. The one at the front was tall and lean, with a close-cropped skull and a face that seemed to have been sanded flat by the sun. He walked forward a few paces and stopped, and raised one hand.
"Hermitage," he called. His voice carried easily across the mud. "We have a broker's notice. You have three days to vacate the perimeter. The timber rights to this land have been transferred. Any persons remaining inside the marked boundary will be considered trespassers under the salt-road ordinance."
"Trespassers," Lakshman said, under his breath. "Where's he getting his law from?"
"From whoever is paying him," the sage said.
The man in grey heard that. He could not have heard it at a hundred paces, but his head turned as though he had. He looked toward the well, and then past the well to the blue-washed doors of the central building, and then his gaze came to rest on the breach in the wall, where Ram stood now with the lamp-glow behind him.
"Ah," the man said. "The boy from the city. The King's son." He lowered his raised hand and smiled. It was not a pleasant smile. "We heard you were coming. We thought you'd be taller."
"What do you want?" Ram asked.
"The same thing I want from everyone. For you to leave."
"And if I don't?"
The man shrugged. "Then we'll have to make you."
He turned and said something over his shoulder in a language Ram did not know, a short clipped string of syllables, and one of the other men in grey lifted his rifle and fired.
The shot went high. It struck the top of the breach wall with a flat crack and sent a spray of grit into the air. Ram felt it sting his cheek. He did not move. Beside him Lakshman had the bow up and an arrow nocked before Ram had finished breathing in, and the arrow was humming against the string, and Ram could feel his brother's heartbeat through the air like a second pulse.
"Wait," the sage said. He had come to the edge of the courtyard. He stood in the open, bare-footed, his ash-coloured cotton lifting a little in the evening breeze.
The man in grey laughed. "Old man," he said. "Go back to your berries."
"I will," said the sage. "After you have gone."
He lifted his right hand. It was empty. He held it up, palm outward, toward the flyer and the three men and the three grinding machines, and he said something in a voice so quiet that Ram could not hear it.
The flyer's engine stuttered.
It was a small sound. A cough, a catch in the rhythm. For a moment the silence was so complete that Ram heard the bat in the neem tree shift its wings. Then the flyer's rotors began to slow. They did not stop. They slowed, the blades dragging through the air as though through thick water, and the grey shape of the flyer sank a foot, and then another, and settled back onto the mud beside the shed with a thin, despairing whine.
The man in grey stared at it. His two companions stared at it. The door in the flyer's side opened, very slowly, and a fourth man climbed out, coughing, his face white.
"What have you done?" the lean man asked. His voice had lost its amusement.
"Nothing," said the sage. "The engine was tired. It has been running for three days in the salt. It needed to stop."
He lowered his hand. The rotors, very slowly, began to turn again. The flyer lifted, and tilted, and rose unsteadily into the dusk, and the man with the sanded face watched it go with his mouth open. Then he looked back at the sage, and something in his posture changed, and he took a step backward.
"We'll be back," he said.
"I know," said the sage.
The three machines turned on the ridge with a shriek of tortured metal. The men in grey climbed aboard. The flyer, labouring, turned in a wide circle over the dead trees and then headed north, toward the highway and the salt-road towns, its engine coughing faintly like a dog with a thorn in its paw. The machines followed. The dust rose behind them in long white plumes that hung in the still air long after the engines had faded into silence.
Lakshman lowered his bow. His arms were shaking.
"That was not an engine," he said hoarsely. "I know what an engine sounds like when it's dying. That was—"
"Be quiet," said the sage. He was still looking at the ridge. His face in the failing light was grave.
"Why?"
"Because she is coming."
---
The Salt Widow came out of the white flats at full dark, when the moon had not yet risen over the dead trees and the only light came from the lamp at the breach and the cold pale stars.
Ram did not see her come. He was watching the ridge, and then the channel, and then the long shallow basin beyond the channel where the salt pans had been dug and then abandoned, a vast grey plain of crusted white that stretched to the horizon and gave back the starlight like a sheet of frozen water. He saw nothing move on it. Then he heard a sound, faint and dry, like someone walking very slowly through a field of brittle reeds.
He turned.
She was standing on the far bank of the channel, twenty paces from the breach. He had not seen her step onto the bank. She was simply there, the way a stone is there in a stream, its shape suddenly obvious against the water. She was tall and thin, and her skin was grey-white and cracked across the back of her hands and up the length of her arms, as though she had been dipped in lime and left to dry. Her hair hung in long matted rope-strands to her waist, pale with salt. She wore a sari that had once been red and was now the colour of old blood, torn at the hem and tied at the waist with a length of rough cord. Her feet were bare. They left no mark on the crusted mud.
She did not look at Ram. She did not look at Lakshman, or the sage, or the blue-washed doors. She was looking at the courtyard beyond the breach, where the children were sleeping in the central building, and her head moved very slightly from side to side, as a dog's head moves when it smells something it has been hunting for a long time.
"Hold," the sage said softly.
Lakshman had the bow up again. The arrow was steady on the string. Ram could hear his brother's breath, short and sharp, and could feel the tension in the bowstring through the air like a plucked wire.
The woman took a step forward. The salt crackled under her foot.
Ram's hand went to his belt, where the grey disc hung in its leather sheath. He did not draw it. He thought about the cylinder with its ring of lights, the amber spreading across its end, the red dots flaring and dimming under his palm. He thought about his heart, which was pounding now so hard he could feel it in his throat, and he thought about how it would look to the lights, and whether they would turn red or green, and whether it mattered, because the woman was taking another step, and another, and the children were behind the walls, and the breach was open, and there was no one standing between the grey shape on the bank and the sleeping heads under the neem tree but himself and his brother and an old man with empty hands.
Four, two, six.
The count would not come. It was too loud. The woman was too close. He could smell her now, a thin bitter reek of salt and sweat and something that might have been smoke.
She stopped at the edge of the channel and lifted her head. Her eyes caught the lamplight. They were not the eyes of a monster. They were red-rimmed and bloodshot, sunk deep in the grey-white face, and they moved over the breach and the courtyard and the three of them with a slow, terrible, enormous patience, as though she had all the time in the world and knew exactly how little of it the rest of them had.
"Let her come," the sage said. "Do not loose until she is in the breach."
"She'll kill the children," Lakshman whispered.
"She will not kill the children. She has not come for the children. Watch her hands."
Ram watched her hands. They hung at her sides, loose and open. Across the right wrist, tied with a double-looped knot, a strip of red leather had been wound three times around the cracked skin, faded now and stiff with salt, the strap mended once with thread that had been pulled tight.
He knew the knot. He had seen it on the road under the banyan tree, a child's sandal left in the dust beside a spilled cart of grain.
The woman stepped down into the channel.
She came up the far bank through the mud and the broken reeds and into the breach, one slow step after another, her bare feet leaving no mark on the blackened stones. The lamp caught her face. Her mouth was open a little, and her breath came in soft wet pulls, and as she came into the courtyard and the light fell fully across her, Ram saw that her lips were moving, very faintly, around a single word. It was repeated over and over, like the chant of the young man in the milky water at the ghat, the same syllables in the same rhythm, and it took him three steps to understand it, and when he did, it stopped his heart.
"Asha," she was whispering. "Asha. Asha."
A name. The name of a child.
Lakshman's arrow began to drop. Ram felt the tension in the air change, the bowstring loosening by a fraction, his brother's breath hitching in a half-sob that he would deny later. And in that moment the woman lifted her head toward the blue-washed doors of the central building, where a small voice had just begun to call out in the dark, a thin high question, Ma? Ma, is that you?, and the woman's face broke open.
It did not break into anger. It broke into something worse, a naked, unbearable hope, and she took a stumbling step toward the sound, her arms lifting, her cracked hands reaching for the door.
Ram did not decide. He had been trying for four days to decide, and the decision had not come, and then there was no time left for it to come in. The count dropped out of him. The fear dropped out. What was left was the bow in his hands, the stretch of the string against his cheek, and the woman's back in the lamplight, forty paces off and closing, and the small voice in the dark behind the door still calling for a mother who had been dead, or lost, or sold, for longer than the child could possibly remember.
He loosed.
The arrow was not fast enough to be seen. It went into the woman's left shoulder, high, below the collarbone, with a sound like a knife going into dry wood. She jerked. She did not fall. She turned her head, slowly, and looked at him with an expression of simple surprise, as though someone had called her name in a crowded street and she had not expected to be recognised.
She took another step toward the door.
Ram drew again. His hands were steady. He noticed that with a kind of distant wonder. His heartbeat was steady, too, slow and heavy, and it was the slowest and steadiest thing he had felt since the cylinder had turned green under his palm in the hermitage.
The second arrow went through the side of her neck, below the jaw, and came out on the other side with a fine spray of dark liquid that hung in the lamplight for a moment before it fell.
The Salt Widow stopped.
She stood with her back to the blue-washed door and her arms half-raised, and she looked at Ram across the courtyard with her red-rimmed eyes, and her mouth moved once more around the word that had been her whole world for three years. No sound came out. Then her knees folded, slowly, as a tree folds when the axe has finally found the heartwood, and she sank onto the packed earth in front of the door, and lay on her side in the dust, one cracked hand still reaching for the wood.
The strip of red leather lay across her wrist.
Behind the door the child had stopped calling.
---
Nobody moved for a long time.
Ram stood where he was, with the bow still raised. He did not lower it. His arms were not shaking now. They were perfectly still, the way a stone is still, and he understood with a cold clarity that the stillness was worse than shaking would have been.
Lakshman came up beside him. He did not touch Ram. He stood close enough that Ram could feel the heat of his brother's body through the cotton of his shirt, and he said nothing, and after a while he took the bow out of Ram's hands with a gentleness that Ram had never seen him show to anything, and laid it on the ground.
"She had no weapon," Lakshman said quietly.
"No."
"She didn't carry a single thing."
"No."
"She came for the child."
Ram looked at the woman lying in the dust. The lamplight had softened her face. It was not young. There were lines around the mouth, and a long white scar across the forehead, and the cracked skin had a dull shine in places, as though the salt had worked into it and set. She looked tired, Ram thought. She looked as though she had been walking for a very long time and had finally, after years of walking, arrived at the place she had been looking for.
"Yes," Ram said.
From the shadow of the well, the sage came forward. He walked slowly across the courtyard, his bare feet soundless on the packed earth, and when he reached the woman's side he knelt down beside her, very carefully, so that his knees did not touch her skin. He looked at her for a long moment. Then he reached out and laid two fingers on the red leather strip on her wrist, and he held them there, and his lips moved in a silent count.
When he took his hand away, his face was wet.
"Her name was Mai," he said quietly. "She was from the village at the salt-road turning. Her daughter was taken in the second stripping, three years ago, by the men in grey coveralls, to work the pans. The child was seven. The mother walked after them. She walked for a week, through the furrows, without water. When they found her, she was half-dead, and they put her in the pans to drown the fever. She came out of the water in the morning, and she was not the same."
Ram said nothing. The lamp hissed softly in its bracket.
"The villagers call her the Salt Widow," the sage said. "They tell stories about her. They say she eats the men who cut the trees. They say she can walk through the salt without leaving a track. They say she takes children from their beds and carries them off into the dead country where no one can follow." He lifted his hand from the woman's wrist and let it fall gently into his lap. "She did none of those things. She walked here because she heard a child's voice in the night, and she thought it was hers."
"Is the child—"
"The child is in the central building," said the sage. "Asleep, I think. Or awake, and hiding, and trying not to cry. Go and see."
Ram did not move. He stood with the bow lying on the ground at his feet and the woman's body in front of him, and he could not make his legs carry him toward the blue doors.
"I killed her," he said.
"You did."
"She wasn't—"
"No," said the sage. "She was not what they said she was. She was a mother who had lost her child, and who came for the sound of a voice. And if you had let her through that door, she would have found the children asleep, and she would have sat with them until the morning, and then she would have wept, and then she would have walked away into the salt again, and no one would have ever known what she had come for." He stood up slowly, his knees cracking. "I know what you are thinking. You are thinking that you could have done something else. Perhaps you could. Perhaps there was another arrow, another road, another way to stop her that did not end with a grey woman on her side in the dust. But you did not find it in the moment. You found the one you had. That is all anyone ever does."
Lakshman's voice came out rough. "That's not an answer."
"No," the sage agreed. "It is only what happened."
The blue-washed doors opened very slowly. A small face appeared in the gap, dark-eyed and tear-streaked, a girl of perhaps five with her hair tied up in a cloth, clutching the edge of the door with both hands. She looked at the grey woman lying on the packed earth. She did not scream. She looked for a long time, with the careful, unnatural stillness of a child who has learned that the world can change without warning and has decided not to be surprised by it again.
Then she looked at Ram, and her eyes moved from his face to the bow on the ground, and then back again.
"Is she sleeping?" the girl asked.
Ram opened his mouth. Nothing came out.
The sage went to the door and crouched down in front of the child, slowly, so that their eyes were level. He did not touch her. He said something too quiet for Ram to hear, and the girl's lip trembled, and her grip on the door tightened, and then she nodded once, as though she had been given a reason to believe something she had not wanted to believe.
She let go of the door and came out into the courtyard.
She did not look at Ram again. She walked past him, past the bow on the ground, past the woman lying with one hand still reaching for the wood, and she knelt down in the dust beside the body, and very carefully, with the tenderness of someone who has been taught how to handle a thing that might break, she took the cracked hand in both of hers and laid it gently across the grey chest, and smoothed the matted hair back from the face.
Then she stood up, and wiped her eyes on the back of her wrist, and walked back into the central building without a word.
The moon rose over the dead trees, pale and enormous, and laid its cold light across the salt flats and the channel and the blackened stones of the breach. In the courtyard the lamp burned on. Lakshman picked up his bow from the ground and held it against his chest, the way a man holds something he is afraid he will drop.
Ram stood very still in the moonlight and looked at his own hands. They were steady. They were perfectly steady. He understood, in a way he would not be able to explain to anyone for many years, that they would always be steady from now on, and that this was not a comfort, and that he would have to learn to live inside the steadiness the way he had learned to live inside the count.
Far to the north, beyond the dead forest and the highway and the salt-road towns, the glass towers of the city would be catching the moonlight too, their faces turned to the south, waiting to hear what had happened in the hermitage.
They would hear soon enough.
---
CHAPTER : SARGA V: Goutama's Ruin
> **[THE ROAD TAKEN]**
They left Siddhashrama on the morning of the fifth day, before the heat came up off the salt, and they did not look back at the breach. Nalini walked them to the edge of the channel. She carried the clay bowl again, and she did not offer it to anyone, and when the sage thanked her with his palms pressed together, she looked at his hands for a long moment and then turned and walked away without speaking.
The girl was not in the courtyard. Ram had asked for her at the door of the central building the night before, and Nalini had said only that the child had gone to sleep beside the grey woman's body and would not be moved, and that the old women of the hermitage had wrapped the body in a clean white cloth and laid it under the neem tree, and that the child had sat beside it through the rest of the night with her knees drawn up under her chin. In the morning she was still there. Ram had seen her from the breach, a small hunched shape in the dust, and had not gone to her, because he could not think of anything he could say that would not be a lie.
The sage walked ahead, his staff striking the broken road at its steady interval. He had said almost nothing since the night of the Salt Widow. When he spoke at all, it was to name the next village, or to tell them to drink, or to ask whether they had slept. He did not ask about the arrows. He did not ask what Ram had felt when the second shaft went home. Ram had waited for the question all through the first morning, and when it did not come, he found that the silence was harder to walk through than any question would have been.
Lakshman walked behind him. He carried the bow across his back, and his right hand kept drifting up to touch the wood of it, the way a man's hand goes to the place where a wound has been stitched.
"You should let me carry that," Ram said, somewhere past the second milestone.
"I can carry my own bow."
"I know."
"Then why are you asking?"
"Because you keep touching it."
Lakshman's hand dropped to his side. He walked for a while without speaking. Then he said, very quietly, so that the sage, ahead of them, would not hear: "I've never killed anyone."
"I know."
"You're not going to say it was the only thing you could have done?"
"No."
"Good." Lakshman's voice cracked slightly on the word. "Because it wasn't, and I don't want to hear someone tell me it was, and I don't want to be the one who believes them."
---
The road bent east in the second week, away from the dead forest and into the country of the river plains. The land changed as they walked. The scrub gave way to fields of stubble, bright gold in the early light and grey by noon, and between the fields ran canals lined with rusted pumps and broken sluice gates, and the water in the canals was the same tea-coloured sludge that had lain in the Sarayu's shallows, but here it moved sluggishly, carrying scraps of plastic and the bloated bodies of small animals, and the air above it hung thick with a smell that made Lakshman retch twice before they reached the first village.
The villages were larger here, and older. Their houses were built of baked brick, the walls painted in faded colours that had been bright once: ochre, blue, a rust-red like the inside of a pomegranate. Women sat in the doorways grinding grain on flat stones. Old men sat in the shade of the banyan trees at the village wells and played a game with pebbles on a board scratched into the earth. Children ran in packs through the lanes, shrieking, and stopped when they saw the three strangers, and stared with their mouths open, and then ran on again.
Nobody spoke to them. Nobody refused them water, either. In the third village an old woman brought them a clay jug and set it on the step of a well without a word, and then went back into her house and closed the door.
At the edge of the fourth village, where the road ran out into a flat expanse of wet fields and the river showed itself again beyond them, grey and wide and slow, the sage stopped and turned to face the east.
"Goutama's ruin is three miles from here," he said. "On the bend of the river. Below the old ferry landing."
"Who is Goutama?" Lakshman asked.
"A man who had a wife," said the sage, "and who was ashamed of her."
He said nothing else. He walked on, and after a moment they followed.
---
The hermitage stood on a raised bank above a bend in the river, where the water ran fast and dark between two long spits of white sand. The buildings had been large once. Their walls still stood, in places, to the height of a man's shoulder, but the roofs had collapsed long ago, and the timber beams had rotted and fallen in on themselves, and the courtyard was overgrown with knee-high grass and clumps of wild thorn. A pair of stone steps ran down from the bank to the water. At the foot of the steps, where the river lapped at a flat grey slab, something stood up out of the shallows.
It took Ram a moment to understand what he was looking at.
It was a figure. A woman, standing waist-deep in the river with her back to the bank, perfectly still. The water ran around her legs in a steady swirling current, and the light off the river caught her in a wavering silver net, and she did not move. Her shoulders were bare. Her hair hung down her back in a long dark plait, tied with a thread. Her arms hung at her sides, and her head was bent a little forward, as though she were listening to something very far away.
She was not alive. Or she was alive, and had been standing in the water for so long that the water had made her part of itself.
The grey crust began at her feet. It spread upward in a thin shining layer, like the film that forms on the surface of a cooling pan of milk, then thickened as it rose, climbing her calves and knees and thighs in long ridged folds, until at her waist it had become a hard pale shell, rough and porous, veined with fine white lines like the rings of an old tree. Her belly and her ribs and her shoulders were encased in it. Her arms were encased in it. Only her neck and her head and a few inches of her throat were still her own, and even her face was dusted with the white crust, so that the skin looked like old paper that had been left out in the rain.
The current went around her. The water was clear here, for a little way, and Ram could see the crust going down under the surface: thick grey stalactites of mineral dangling from the underside of her body, reaching toward the riverbed like roots.
"Limestone," Lakshman said. His voice was quiet. "From the factories upriver. It comes down in the runoff. The old pilgrims used to say the river would turn to stone if you bathed in it too long."
"It doesn't turn people to stone," the sage said. "It only makes them very still."
He went down the steps. He did not hurry. At the foot of the steps he stopped at the edge of the grey slab and looked up at the woman in the river for a long time, and then he did something Ram did not expect. He took off his sandals, which he had not worn since the causeway, and set them neatly side by side on the slab. Then he stepped into the water.
It came to his knees. It came to his thighs. He waded out until he stood a pace from the woman's back, and then he put out one hand and laid it flat against the crust of her shoulder, very lightly, the way a man might rest his palm on the flank of a horse he did not yet know.
"Come down," he said. "The boy will take you."
Ram had not been told that. He went down the steps. The water was cold, colder than he had expected, and it pulled at his legs with a quiet insistent force that made him stagger. Lakshman came behind him, muttering curses under his breath. At the foot of the slab they stopped, and the sage looked over his shoulder at them.
"You will go to her front," he said. "Not her back. Stand where she can see you."
Ram waded around the shell of the woman's body, the current pushing at his hips, until he stood facing her. Up close the crust was rough and cold under the water, and the white dust on her face had settled into the fine lines around her eyes and mouth like the powder of an old woman's cosmetics. Her eyes were open. They were dark brown, and clear, and they looked past Ram at the far bank of the river with an expression of such terrible, patient waiting that he felt his throat close.
"What do I do?" he asked.
"Use your hands," said the sage. "Not the bow. Not the disc. Your hands."
Ram lifted his right hand and laid it against the crust over the woman's ribs. The surface was dry and gritty. He pressed. Nothing happened. He pressed harder, and the grit crumbled a little under his palm, and a fine white dust sifted into the water and was carried away by the current.
"Harder," said the sage.
Ram pressed. He put his other hand flat against the crust beside the first and leaned his weight into it. The crust gave a little. A thin crack appeared, running down from under his fingers, black at the edges where the water had got into it. He pulled at the edge of the crack with his fingernails. Flakes of grey mineral came away in his hand, sharp as broken shell, and cut into his skin, and the blood ran pale pink in the cold water.
He did not stop.
It took the better part of an hour. He worked in silence, his hands raw and bleeding, his breath coming hard, and the current tugging at his legs, and the sun climbing slowly over the river until it struck the back of his neck like the flat of a blade. Lakshman stood at the edge of the slab and watched. Once he came forward and tried to take over, and Ram shook his head without looking up, and Lakshman stepped back and went on watching.
The sage did not move from his place at the woman's back. His palm stayed on her shoulder the whole time, very still. Ram did not look at him again, but he could feel him there, the way you feel the weight of a stone in a stream.
At the end of the hour the crack ran all the way around the woman's ribs. Ram sat back in the water and looked at it. The crust had split along the line of his fingers, a clean ragged seam from her left side around to her right, and the two halves of the shell had begun, very slightly, to pull apart, the way bark pulls away from a tree after a lightning strike.
"Now," the sage said quietly. "The rest is hers."
Ram stood up in the water. His legs were trembling. He reached out and took the woman's hands, where they hung inside the crust at her sides, and he could feel the shape of her fingers through the stone, long and thin and very cold.
He looked into her face.
"Ahalya," he said.
The name came out of him very softly. He did not know where he had got it. It was not a name he had heard in the palace or on the feeds. It was not a name he had read in any of the old books the palace schools had made him learn. He thought perhaps it had been in the air around the hermitage, under the grass and the thorn and the drifting white dust, a word the villagers had stopped saying because it had become too heavy to carry.
The woman's eyes moved. They came slowly to his face. Something shifted in them, very deep, like a stone moving at the bottom of a pool.
"Ahalya," Ram said again. "You can come out now."
She did not speak. Her lips parted. A breath went in, and it came out as a thin cracked sound, like wind through a dry reed, and the crust around her chest gave a long low groan and split another inch along the seam. Her head tilted back. Her throat worked. And then she made a sound that was not a word but was, nevertheless, the first thing she had said in what might have been twenty years, and it was a sob, raw and enormous, torn out of her from somewhere below the stone.
The crust broke.
It came apart in a single long shudder, the two halves sliding away from her body and sinking slowly into the current, carried off in a cloud of white grit and tiny splinters of mineral that glittered in the sun before they vanished downstream. The woman swayed. Ram caught her before she could fall, his arms locking around her narrow back, and felt her weight come onto him all at once, shaking, cold, impossibly light.
She was crying. She was crying with her face pressed into his shoulder and her arms wrapped around his neck, and her body heaved against his chest with great dry racking sobs, as though she were trying to expel twenty years of breath in a single breath. Ram held her. He did not say anything. He did not know what to say. The water swirled around them both, and the sun beat down on the river, and somewhere behind him, on the steps of the ruined ghat, Lakshman let out a sound that might have been a laugh or a sob or both.
---
The villagers had come down to the river while Ram was working. He did not notice them until he had carried the woman out of the water and laid her on the grey slab, wrapping her in the cloth the sage had taken from his own bag. He straightened up and saw them on the bank above, perhaps thirty of them, men and women and children, standing among the thorn bushes and the broken walls with their faces turned toward the river.
They were not cheering. They were not weeping. They stood in silence, watching, with the careful blankness of people who have been told a story for so long that they no longer know how to hear anything else.
At the front of the crowd stood a man in a white dhoti and a grey shawl, with a shaved head and a thick gold chain around his neck. He was old, and heavy-set, and his eyes had the bright hard shine of a man who counts coins for a living. When Ram looked up at him, he stepped forward and raised one hand.
"You have broken the curse," the man said.
His voice was loud and smooth. It carried across the water with the ease of long practice. Ram knew the tone. It was the tone of the Board's officers on the feeds, addressing a crowd they expected to agree with them.
"I have helped her out of the river," Ram said.
"The river is holy." The man's eyes went to the slab and the woman lying on it, wrapped in white. "The stone was given to her by her husband, the sage Goutama, in the old time. It was his judgement, and his gift. It was a mark of the gods' justice, and it has been kept for the sake of the village for twenty years. Every pilgrim who comes to the ferry pays a coin to see it. The coins feed the temple. The temple feeds the village. You have taken away the thing that keeps the village alive."
Ram said nothing. He looked at the woman on the slab. Her eyes were open. She was watching the man on the bank with an expression Ram could not read.
"Who will pay for the temple now?" the man asked. His voice had dropped, become almost gentle, the voice of a man reasoning with a child.
"Nobody," said Ram.
The crowd shifted. A murmur went through it, low and uncertain. The man on the bank smiled, very slightly, and folded his hands across his stomach.
"Then you have made a poor exchange, young prince," he said. "The river gave us a thing we could sell. You have given us a woman who will want to eat."
The sage came up out of the water. He had been standing at the edge of the current all this time, his sandals beside him on the slab, and now he walked up the steps with the water streaming from his cotton, and stopped beside Ram at the top. He did not look at the man on the bank. He looked at the crowd, slowly, one face at a time, until the murmuring stopped.
"A curse is only as strong as the people who keep it," he said. His voice was quiet, but it carried. "You have been keeping this one for twenty years, and you have been paid to keep it, and you have grown fat on it. Now the woman is out of the river. She has eaten nothing for many days, and she will want to eat, and she will want to sleep in a house with a door that closes. She will want to see her own face in water that is not made of stone. You may pay for her food, or you may watch her go hungry in front of you. The choice is yours. It has always been yours."
The man in the gold chain opened his mouth. Then he closed it. He looked at the sage, and at Ram, and at the grey woman on the slab, and his face did something complicated and unpleasant, a shifting of muscles that might have been fear, or calculation, or simply the ordinary disappointment of a man who has lost a reliable income.
"Goutama will hear of this," he said.
"Goutama is in the city," the sage replied calmly. "He has been in the city for twenty years. He sits in a glass office on the twentieth floor of the Board's audit tower, and every month he sends a stipend to this village through a clerk who has never visited it. He has never come back. He does not care whether his wife is in the river or on the steps. He has not thought of her once in all that time, except when the clerk reminds him to sign the forms."
Silence fell over the bank. Nobody moved.
Ram looked at the sage. The old man's face was hard, and grey, and wet with river water, and there was a tightness around his mouth that Ram had not seen before, a kind of contained, bitter fury, very old and very deep.
Then the sage turned away and walked down the steps to the slab, and knelt beside the woman, and began to speak to her in a low murmur too soft for anyone else to hear.
---
They carried her to the nearest village that evening, on a litter of woven reeds that the women had made in an hour, without being asked. Ram and Lakshman took the poles. The sage walked beside them with one hand resting on the litter's edge, as though to make sure it did not tip. The woman lay on her back under the white cloth, her eyes half-closed, her breath coming in slow shallow draughts. When they came to the first lane of the village, a girl of about twelve came running out of a doorway and stopped dead at the sight of the litter, and her face went white, and then she threw her arms around the woman's neck with a cry that echoed off the mud walls.
The woman's hands came up slowly from under the cloth. They touched the girl's hair. They did not stop moving until the girl pulled back and wiped her eyes on her sleeve, and then the woman's hands dropped, very softly, into her lap.
"Who is she?" Lakshman asked, very quietly.
"Her daughter," said the sage. "The one who was born after the curse. Goutama never saw her. The clerk sent her to school in the city, and then she came back, and she has been looking after her mother ever since, in the way the village allows."
They laid the litter down in a house with a door that closed. The girl brought water. Ram watched her hold the clay cup to her mother's lips, and the woman drink, and the water run down her chin in clean pale streaks that washed the last of the white dust from her skin. When she had finished, she lay back against the pillow and closed her eyes, and her face, for the first time since Ram had seen it, looked like the face of someone who was tired rather than someone who was waiting.
The sage left them then. He went out into the lane without a word, and Ram followed him to the edge of the village, where the road ran out across the fields toward the east and the glow of the setting sun lay across the flat land like spilled gold.
"You know what you did," the sage said. He was not looking at Ram. He was looking at the road.
"I broke a curse."
"You broke a silence. The curse was only the cover on it."
"And what's under it?"
The sage turned his head and looked at him, and for the first time since the causeway Ram saw something soften in his face. It was not a smile. It was closer to grief.
"Under it," he said, "is a village that has been paying a stonecutter for twenty years to tell it that its suffering was given by heaven, so it would never ask who was taking the money. And a husband in a glass tower who has never once been asked to explain himself. You are going to find that it is the same everywhere, boy. Every hard truth is a cover on a softer lie, and every softer lie has a man in a glass office on the far side of it, signing forms."
"Then what do I do about it?"
"Nothing, yet," said the sage. "You have only just learned to see it. That is more than most manage in a lifetime."
They went back to the house with the closed door. Lakshman was sitting on the step, his bow across his knees, watching the lane. He looked up as they came, and his face in the dusk was strange, open, almost frightened.
"She asked for you," he said to Ram.
"Who did?"
"The woman. Ahalya." He shook his head slowly. "She opened her eyes and asked whether the boy who broke the stone was still in the village. The girl said yes. And then the woman said—" He stopped. His mouth worked. "She said, He has the hands of someone who knows how to hold on to a thing without crushing it."
Ram did not answer. He looked past his brother into the dim interior of the house, where the lamp had been lit and the light fell in a warm soft pool across the earthen floor. Ahalya lay on the pallet with her daughter's head on her shoulder. Her eyes were open. They were looking at the doorway, and when they found Ram standing in it, they did not look away.
He went in. He sat down on the floor beside the pallet, and she reached out one hand, slowly, and laid it over his, and her fingers were warm now, and light, and very steady.
She did not say anything. Neither did he.
They sat like that until the lamp burned low.

CHAPTER : SARGA VI: The Bow at Mithila
> **[THE ROAD TAKEN]**
Mithila was a city of canals and bridges and old brick walls painted white, and it had the look of a place that had been rich for a very long time and had spent most of that time deciding whether to admit it. The bridges were wide and stone-paved, lined with carved balustrades and small stalls selling sweets and cloth and clay lamps, and the canals beneath them ran green and slow, their banks bordered with lotus flowers that had been planted deliberately, in rows, by somebody who wanted visitors to see them. Above the canals the white walls rose in long unbroken curves. Above the walls, far off on the western rise, stood the palace of the King of Mithila, and its roof was not glass. It was copper, green with age, and it caught the afternoon sun like the flank of some enormous animal lying down to sleep.
They came into the city on the fourteenth day, through a gate that had no guard, and walked up the long avenue toward the palace with the dust of the road still on their clothes. The sage had sent them ahead of him from the last village. He had said he would follow when he had finished a matter of his own, and he had not said what the matter was. Ram had thought at first that he would not come at all. Then, on the third morning, he had caught the old man's eye across the breakfast fire and seen there the same flat, careful look that had been on his face in the Chamber of Oaths, and he had understood that the sage had simply decided to make the journey at his own pace, the way he did everything.
The avenue was lined with stone benches, and on the benches old men sat with their feet up, arguing over games of dice. Women with baskets of flowers sold garlands to the pilgrims who streamed up the hill toward the palace gate. Ram saw a row of lamps hung on a rope between two trees, each one a small glass bowl with a wick floating in oil, and beneath the lamps a boy sat cross-legged on a mat, stringing jasmine into long fragrant chains. The air smelled of smoke and sweetness and the sharp green tang of the canal water. It was a city of people who had nothing to do but be seen, and who had been seen for centuries, and who had grown quite comfortable with it.
"They're watching us," Lakshman said.
"They're watching everyone."
"No." Lakshman's voice was low. "They're watching us. Look at the men on the benches. They know who we are."
Ram looked. The men on the benches had stopped playing their dice. They were looking up the avenue at the three of them with a sort of idle, speculative interest, the kind of attention people give to something they have already decided to talk about later.
"Word travels," Ram said.
"Word from where?"
"From the city," said Ram. "Probably from the Board."
Lakshman was quiet for a moment. Then he said, very quietly, "Harit."
"Maybe."
"He'd have sent a runner. To the towers, and then to every house with a line into the Board's feeds. He'd want it known that the King's sons had gone to Mithila and that the eldest had stopped being a boy."
Ram did not answer. The idea was not comforting, and he did not want to think about it on the avenue, in the sun, with the lotus flowers opening on the canal and the smell of jasmine in the air.
---
The palace gate was guarded by four men in white tunics and grey sashes, and their faces were carefully empty. They did not stop Ram and Lakshman at the threshold. They stepped aside, and one of them lifted a hand toward the inner courtyard and said, in a flat voice that did not match the courtesy of the gesture, "The King is expecting the suitors."
"We are not suitors," said Lakshman.
"Everyone who comes through the gate is a suitor," said the guard. "That is the custom."
They went through.
The inner courtyard was large and paved in white marble, and in its centre stood a fountain shaped like a lotus with a dozen stone petals, and the water sprayed up from the centre of the lotus in a fine silver column that caught the sun and broke it into rainbows. Around the fountain, on a raised dais of red stone, a crowd had gathered. Perhaps two hundred men, dressed in every style from the palace's finest silks to the plain cotton of village farmers, stood in ranks on the marble, murmuring to one another in low voices. Some carried weapons. Most did not. All of them were watching the far end of the courtyard, where a wide arched doorway opened into a hall of dark wood and gold leaf.
Through the doorway, Ram could see a long low room, lit by hanging lamps and lined with carved pillars. At the far end of the room, on a dais covered with white cloth, stood a glass case the size of a bed. Inside the case, lying on a bed of black velvet, was a bow.
It was not a beautiful bow. It was long and dark and very plain, made of some grey-brown material that caught the light without shining. Its limbs were thick, nearly as thick as a man's wrist, and along the length of each limb ran a series of fine silver lines, like the veins on the back of a hand. Its string was not string at all. It was a single strand of something pale and fibrous, drawn taut between the tips of the limbs, and it hummed faintly in the still air of the hall, a sound so low that Ram felt it in his teeth rather than his ears.
Around the glass case stood six sentinels. They were tall and slender, made of dark metal, with smooth rounded faces that had no eyes, and they stood perfectly still, their hands folded behind their backs, their heads turned toward the crowd at the doorway. Each one had a ring of pale green lights set into the crown of its skull. The lights were glowing steadily, like the lamps on the avenue.
"That's the bow," Lakshman said under his breath.
"Yes."
"They say no one has ever lifted it. Not in five hundred years. They say the house of Janak keeps it in the glass because if anyone outside the blood ever touched it, the sentinels would take their hand off."
"Does that sound like a story someone would tell?"
"Yes," said Lakshman, very dryly. "That's exactly the kind of story someone would tell."
A man came through the crowd toward them. He was tall and lean, dressed in a long grey coat with a high collar and a sash of dark red silk, and his beard was clipped close to his jaw and streaked with white. He had a long intelligent face and eyes that moved quickly from Ram to Lakshman and back again, taking them in and putting them away, like a clerk sorting documents.
"You are the sons of the King of Kosala," he said. "I am Vishala, the King's chamberlain. You are welcome in Mithila. You have come for the contest."
"We have come because we were asked to," said Ram.
"By whom?"
"By a sage."
The chamberlain's mouth thinned. "The sage has sent letters," he said. "He is a difficult man. He has a habit of arriving when he is least expected and then making it impossible for anyone to refuse his requests. The King has been persuaded to allow one of you to attempt the bow, on the sage's word, as a courtesy. It is not a duty. It is not a claim. The King does not wish his daughter's hand to be decided by a contest that the house of Kosala has no part in."
"And if the bow lifts?" Lakshman asked.
The chamberlain looked at him with a flicker of something almost like amusement. "Then the bow will have been lifted," he said, "and the King will have to decide what that means."
---
The King of Mithila was waiting for them in a small room off the main hall, and the room was not what Ram had expected.
It was bare. There was no gold on the walls, no carved pillars, no cushions on the floor. Only a plain wooden table, two stools, and a window with its shutters half-closed against the afternoon heat. The King sat behind the table, very upright, a small thin man in a cotton tunic the colour of unbleached linen. His hair was white, cropped close to his skull. His face was lined and brown and very still, and his eyes were the palest grey Ram had ever seen, like river stones after rain.
He did not stand up. He looked at Ram for a long time without speaking, and then he looked at Lakshman, and then back at Ram.
"Dasharatha's sons," he said at last. His voice was dry and quiet. "I have heard that the eldest is a boy who does not weep at funerals."
"I weep when there is cause," said Ram.
"Is there cause today?"
"I don't know yet."
Janak made a small sound in his throat that might have been a laugh. "The sage told me you would say something like that," he said. "He told me you would be honest in a way that would annoy everyone who met you. He was right, I see." He leaned back in his chair and laced his fingers together over his stomach. "I have one daughter. You will have heard of her."
"Yes."
"Then you will know that she was not born in this house."
"I have heard that she was found," said Ram carefully. "In a furrow. In the sacrifice-field."
"That is what the stories say. It is true, as far as it goes." The King's eyes narrowed slightly. "I was ploughing the field myself, at the start of the rains, as my fathers did before me. I had been ploughing for three days and had not found a single seed, and my hands had blistered until they bled. On the third day the plough struck something soft in the earth, and I knelt down and dug with my fingers until I found a small wooden box, and inside the box was a girl. She was perhaps a year old. She was not crying. She was looking at me with the same expression you have now."
Ram said nothing.
"I took her home," the King said. "I raised her in this house. I taught her to read, and to count, and to speak to the men who came to the palace with their petitions. I did not teach her to be meek, and she did not learn it. When she was twelve she asked me why the bow in the glass case could not be lifted by anyone who did not have the blood of my house in his hands. I told her it was the custom. She asked me what the custom was for. I could not answer. That was the first time I realised that a thing could be kept for five hundred years without anyone knowing why."
"What did you do?" Ram asked.
"Nothing," said Janak. "I wrote a law to keep the bow in the glass case, and I hired the sentinels to guard it, and I announced a contest for my daughter's hand that no one would ever win. Then I waited to see whether someone would have the courage to ask the question I could not." He smiled, very faintly, without warmth. "Your sage has taken his time, I see. He told me he would bring someone who would not wait."
He stood up. He was shorter than Ram by a head, and his shoulders were narrow, but he moved with the economy of a man who had spent his life being the least physically imposing person in any room and had learned to make that count for something.
"Come," he said. "The hall is waiting."
---
The hall was full when they came back into it. The men on the red dais had fallen silent, and their faces were turned toward the doorway, and as Janak walked through the crowd, the ranks parted before him without a word, and closed again behind him like water.
Ram walked behind the King. Lakshman followed. As they came into the hall, Ram saw a girl standing at the foot of the dais, beside a pillar of carved white stone. She was not dressed like the others. She wore a plain red cotton sari with a narrow gold border, and her hair was tied back in a thick black plait that hung to the middle of her back. She was perhaps seventeen. She was not beautiful in the way the palace girls of Ayodhya had been beautiful, with their painted lips and their careful silks. Her face was sharp, and grave, and intensely alert, and her eyes were dark and steady. She was watching Ram.
She did not look away when he met her gaze. She did not smile. She simply looked, with an expression of such concentrated and unhurried attention that Ram felt, for one strange disorienting moment, as though he had been picked up and turned over in her hands and examined from every side.
Then she looked at the bow in the glass case, and her face changed. The gravity went out of it and something cooler came in, something almost like contempt.
"Father," she said, not loudly, but clearly, so that the sound carried across the silent hall. "You are going to let a boy from another kingdom break your bow for a husband."
"I am going to let him try," said the King.
"Why?"
"Because the sage asked."
The girl's mouth tightened. "The sage asks for a great many things," she said. "He has never once asked for anything that did not come with a price attached."
"Sita," said the King, very quietly. "Be silent."
She was silent. But she did not look away from Ram.
---
The sentinels turned their smooth faces toward him as he approached the glass case. The green lights in their skulls brightened slightly. A low hum rose in the hall, and the pale fibrous string of the bow trembled in its frame, and Ram felt the air go tight around him, thin and cold, as though a window had been opened in winter.
He stopped at the edge of the dais. The sentinels did not move. The hum grew stronger.
The case was sealed. Its front panel was made of thick clear glass, and along the lower edge of the glass a row of small black studs had been set into the wood. Ram could see a thin pale line of light running around the rim of the case, very faint, the colour of the sentinels' lights. The line was pulsing, slow and steady, like a heartbeat.
It was reading him.
He understood it at once, the way he had understood the payload in the hermitage: the thing was not a lock in the old sense, a bar of iron and a key. It was a lock that listened. It read the pulse of the hand that touched the glass, and the rhythm of the breath behind the hand, and the set of the shoulders and the tension in the arm, and it decided, from all of that, whether the one who stood there was fit to lift what was inside.
Behind him he could hear the crowd breathing. Lakshman was a silent presence at his shoulder. The King stood a little apart, watching with his pale river-stone eyes. And at the foot of the dais, in the red sari, the girl called Sita was watching him with her hands clasped in front of her, very still.
Four, two, six.
He did not reach for the count. He had been using it for weeks now, and it had become a thing he did not have to think about, the way the body does not think about walking. It came to him as he stood there, slow and steady, in the cool heavy air of the hall. He breathed in. He held. He breathed out. The pale line of light around the case pulsed, once, twice, and then began to slow.
He put his hand on the glass.
It was cold. It was smooth. He felt the faint vibration of the bow through the panel, a steady thrumming like a far-off engine, and he felt the line of light beneath his palm go very still, as though it were holding its breath.
Then it began, very slowly, to glow green.
A murmur went through the crowd. Ram did not look up. He pressed his palm flat against the glass and waited, breathing, while the sentinels' heads turned in unison toward him and the green lights in their skulls flickered and steadied, and then, one by one, went out.
The sentinels stepped back.
The case did not open. There was no mechanism to open it. Instead, the front panel simply dissolved, the way ice dissolves in the first warm hour of spring. The glass ran down in silent sheets and pooled at the foot of the dais and was gone, and the bow lay exposed on its bed of velvet, humming softly in the sudden open air.
Ram reached in and took it.
It was heavier than he had expected. Much heavier. The grey-brown wood was cool and dense, and the silver lines along its limbs were faintly warm under his fingers, like the veins of a living thing. The string hummed against his skin. He lifted the bow out of its case and held it up in front of him, and the whole hall gasped at once, a sudden rushing intake of breath that rolled from one side of the room to the other like wind through standing corn.
He had not drawn it. He had only lifted it.
He could feel the pull of the string. It was not a pull on his arm. It was a pull on something deeper, under the ribs, somewhere below the place where the count lived. It wanted him to draw. It wanted him to bend the limbs back and set the string in the notch and let the thing sing, because that was what it was for, that was what the house of Janak had kept it for, five hundred years in a glass box waiting for someone with the blood and the nerve to answer its call.
The sentinels were standing very still. Their lights were out. They were waiting.
He did not draw.
He set the bow across his knees, and took hold of the two ends, one in each hand, and began to bend.
It was not a draw. It was not a test of strength in the way the palace schools would have taught it. It was a pressure applied slowly, steadily, from both ends at once, the kind of pressure a man applies to a green branch he wants to snap for firewood. The limbs resisted. He felt them resist, the faint silver lines beneath his palms tightening and warming, and he kept the pressure steady, and the count kept his breath slow, and the bow kept humming, and then, with a sound like a single dry crack from a frozen lake, the grey-brown wood split across the middle.
The string went slack.
The humming stopped.
The hall was so quiet that Ram could hear the water of the fountain spraying in the courtyard beyond the arched doorway, a faint and endless hissing, like a breath drawn in and never let out.
He sat there on the dais with the broken bow across his knees, both halves still in his hands, the split edges pale and fibrous where the wood had torn. The sentinels had not moved. Their smooth faces were turned toward him still, and their lights were dark. Slowly, very slowly, one of them lifted its folded hands from behind its back and reached toward him, and stopped, and lowered them again, as though it had lost the instruction it had been given.
Behind him, the King of Mithila said nothing at all.
Ram looked up. The girl at the foot of the dais had come forward a few paces. Her face was pale, and her hands were no longer clasped. They hung loose at her sides, and she was staring at the broken bow with an expression Ram could not read at all: not anger, not triumph, not grief, but something sharper and more intent than any of them, as though she were trying to see what was going to happen next, and had not yet decided whether she wanted to know.
"Did you break it?" she asked.
Her voice was very clear in the silence.
Ram looked down at the two halves of the bow. The wood was still warm. The silver lines had gone dark, like the veins of a leaf after frost.
"I don't know," he said.
Sita's eyes narrowed. For a moment she said nothing. Then she took two steps forward, until she stood at the very edge of the dais, close enough that Ram could see the fine dust of the road still lying in the creases of her red sari, and the small gold ring in her left ear, and the faint shadow of tiredness beneath her eyes that she had not bothered to hide.
"Or did it break you?" she asked.
Ram looked at her. She was waiting. She was not smiling. She was watching him the way she had watched him from the pillar, with that unhurried, concentrated attention, as though his answer mattered more to her than anything else in the room.
"I don't think it broke me," he said slowly. "It only wanted to be pulled."
"By whom?"
"By the one who wanted to draw it."
"And you didn't want to draw it."
"No."
"Why not?"
Ram hesitated. The answer was there, in the back of his mind, the count and the cylinder and the sandal with its double-looped knot, but it was not an answer he knew how to say aloud in a crowded room in a palace he had never seen before.
"Because it wasn't mine to draw," he said at last.
Sita was silent for a long moment. Then something moved in her face, very slightly, at the corner of her mouth. It was not a smile. It was closer to recognition, the look of someone who has been told a thing she already knew, and has been surprised to hear it said aloud.
"That," she said, "is the first honest answer anyone has given me about that bow in ten years."
She turned away. She walked back to her pillar, and stood beside it, and folded her hands again in front of her. The red sari caught the light as she moved.
From the far side of the dais, the King of Mithila let out a long slow breath, and when Ram turned to look at him, the old man's pale grey eyes were wet, and his mouth was pressed into a thin hard line that might, in another man, have been the beginnings of a smile.
"You have broken the house's bow," said Janak quietly. "The sentinels will be repaired by morning. The glass will be replaced by the end of the month. The custom will be recorded, and then forgotten, and then recorded again, and the men who keep the record will argue for a generation about what it meant." He looked at the broken halves in Ram's hands. "But you have not broken the house. I wonder whether you know the difference."
"I'm not sure there is one," said Ram.
"Then you will learn," said the King. "Give me the pieces. And then come to the table, prince of Kosala. There is a great deal we have to discuss, and my daughter has already decided how she intends to answer you."

CHAPTER : SARGA VII: The Furrow
> **[THE ROAD TAKEN]**
The wedding was held on the copper roof, because the King of Mithila said that a marriage belonged under the sky and not under the painted beams of a hall, and because, Sita said dryly, it was the only place in the palace where the sentinels could not hear anybody speak.
They had built a fire in a clay pit at the centre of the roof, and around the pit they had laid out a square of white sand, marked at each corner with a brass pot of water and a sprig of mango leaves. There were no priests from the great temples. The King had sent for an old woman from the riverside, who had been married four times and buried three husbands and had, she said, nothing left to fear from any god who wanted to argue with her about the terms. She wore a faded green sari and carried a basket of dried rice and a bundle of kusha grass, and she moved around the fire with the economy of someone who had done this many times and found it tiresome.
"You're thinner than the groom," she said to Ram, looking him up and down. "That's a bad start. Stand up straight."
He stood up straight.
"Better. Now don't look at the fire. The fire doesn't care. Look at her."
He looked at her.
Sita stood across the sand from him in a deep red silk sari, the gold border heavy with thread, the black plait pinned up with small white flowers that smelled sweet and faintly sharp in the heat of the fire. She did not wear the veil the palace women had worn at the feasts of Ayodhya, and she did not lower her eyes. She looked back at him with the same unhurried, measuring attention she had given him at the foot of the dais, and when the old woman began to chant, she did not look away once.
Lakshman stood behind Ram with a face like a man at a funeral who has been asked to laugh. Beside him, to Ram's surprise, stood a girl of about his own age, small and round-cheeked and bright-eyed, in a yellow dress with a gold thread at the hem. Her name was Urmila. She was the King's other daughter, the one who had been raised in the women's quarters and had never been allowed past the gate without an escort, and she had been introduced to Lakshman that morning in the courtyard by the fountain, and she had looked him up and down with a single quick glance and then said, to nobody in particular, that she supposed the bow-breaker's brother would do.
Lakshman had not stopped smiling since.
The fire popped. The old woman threw a handful of rice into it, and the flames flared gold and blue, and the smell of burning grain rose sweet and thick into the warm night. She began to speak in a quick low rhythm, too fast for Ram to follow, and Sita answered her in the same rhythm, and then the old woman turned and looked at Ram, and waited.
He did not know the words. He said what he could, and the old woman nodded, and then, when the sentence was finished, she took his right hand in her dry brown fingers and laid it against Sita's palm, and wound a length of kusha grass around their wrists, three times, and pulled the knot tight.
Sita's hand was cool and very steady.
"You'll have to walk round the fire," said the old woman. "Seven times. The first three you go slow, because you're nervous. The next three you go slow, because you're thinking about what you'll say to her when you finally stop. The seventh time you go fast, because you're glad it's over." She cackled. "Everyone does it the same way. Men are predictable. Walk."
They walked.
The first circuit was slow. Ram could feel the grass binding their wrists, could feel the warmth of her hand through it, could hear the faint click of her gold bangles at every step. The fire was hot on his face. The copper roof was hot under his bare feet, and the sand between the brass pots was cool, and above them the sky was enormous and black and crowded with stars, the same thick dusty band that had hung over the Sarayu on the first night of the road. He thought of the tamarind tree and the count. He did not reach for it. It came anyway, under the rhythm of their steps: four, two, six.
The second circuit was slower. Sita did not speak. Neither did he.
The third circuit was the one the old woman had warned about. Halfway round, Sita stopped suddenly, so that their joined hands tugged sharply and Ram had to stop too, and she turned her head and looked at him across the fire with an expression he had not seen on her face before. It was not anger. It was not amusement. It was the look of someone who has been carrying a question for a long time and has just now realised she can ask it.
"Why did you come?" she said.
"To the wedding?"
"To Mithila. To the bow. To me." She did not raise her voice, but the words were very precise. "You came because a sage asked for you. You didn't know him. You didn't know what he wanted. You didn't know whether you'd come back. And you came."
"Yes."
"Why?"
Ram looked at the fire. The old woman was muttering something under her breath and throwing rice into the flames with small irritable flicks of her wrist.
"Because my father couldn't say no," he said slowly. "And somebody had to be the one who didn't need to."
Sita was quiet for a long moment. Then she began to walk again, and he had to walk with her, because the grass was still tied at their wrists and neither of them had any intention of untying it.
"That's an answer you've been practising," she said.
"No."
"It sounds practised."
"It's true."
"I didn't say it wasn't." She glanced sideways at him as they went round the pit. The firelight caught the gold in her earrings and the sharp line of her cheek. "I said it sounded practised. There's a difference. Practised answers are the ones people give when they've already decided what the truth should be."
"And what was it?" Ram asked. "The truth, I mean. That you think I should have said."
She laughed. It was a short, low sound, startled out of her, and it surprised them both. "You're not going to get off that easily," she said. "Keep walking."
---
They were allowed to sleep alone that night, which the old woman had said was a rare kindness from the gods and a sign that the marriage would be a difficult one. The bedchamber was a small room at the top of the eastern stair, with a single window that looked out over the city and a bed of carved sandalwood piled with white cotton. Someone had left a brass lamp burning on the sill, and it cast a warm wavering light across the floor and up the walls, and in its light the room looked older than it was, as though the stones had been waiting for two people to come and sit in them.
Ram did not sit. He stood at the window with his hands on the stone sill and looked out at the lamps of the city, scattered across the dark plain below the palace like sparks blown off a fire.
Behind him, he heard Sita come in and close the door. He heard her walk across the floor. He heard the rustle of silk as she sat down on the bed, and then a pause, and then the rustle again as she lay back, and he understood, without turning, that she had lain down on the cotton with her arms folded behind her head and her eyes on the ceiling, the way a person lies when they have been carrying something heavy all day and have finally put it down.
"You can sit," she said. "I won't bite. Not tonight."
He turned. She was lying on her back, exactly as he had imagined, with her red sari pooled around her on the white cotton and the gold border glinting in the lamplight. The pins had come out of her hair. It spilled across the pillow in a thick black tangle, and she had not bothered to smooth it.
He sat on the edge of the bed. The cotton was soft under his hands.
"My father told you about the furrow," she said.
"Yes."
"Did he tell you that I'm not sure I believe him?"
Ram looked at her. "No."
"He's told the story so many times he's forgotten it isn't true." She turned her head on the pillow and looked at him. "I don't mean the furrow. I mean the box. He says he found me in a wooden box in the earth. Maybe he did. Maybe he didn't. Maybe the box was made for a dead man's grain and someone threw a baby into it because they couldn't afford to feed her, and my father was the one who found the box and decided it would be a better story if the gods had put her there."
"Does it matter?" Ram asked.
"It matters to me." Her eyes were steady. "It matters because everything in this city has been built on stories like that. The bow. The contest. The sentinels. My father keeps the custom because he was told it was ancient, and the men who guard the glass keep it because they were told it was ancient, and nobody in the whole kingdom has ever once asked what it was for." She paused. "You asked. You broke it. That's why they're going to talk about you for a hundred years."
"They'll talk about the bow."
"They'll talk about whoever broke it. That's what people do with a story. They keep the shape and forget the inside, and then they argue about what the shape means." She turned onto her side, facing him, and propped her head on one hand. "Tell me something true."
He thought for a long moment. The lamp hissed softly on the sill. Out in the city a dog barked, once, and fell silent.
"There's a child in a hermitage," he said at last, "who thinks a grey woman is her mother. Or she did. Her mother's dead. The woman came for her, and I killed the woman before she could reach the door, and the child came out and looked at the body, and then went back inside and didn't cry. Her name is Asha. I don't know her surname. I didn't ask."
Sita did not move. Her face in the lamplight was very still.
"Is that true?" she asked quietly.
"Yes."
"Did you want to kill her?"
"No."
"Did you want to stop her?"
"Yes."
"Then you did what you wanted," said Sita. "It was just a hard thing to want."
He looked down at his hands. They were resting on the white cotton, very steady, the way they had been in the courtyard with the arrows still in the grey woman's flesh.
"What do you do," Sita said slowly, "when the word your father gave costs someone else?"
Ram said nothing.
"I'm asking," she said. "Not testing you. Not trying to catch you out. I'm asking because I want to know whether the man I've married is going to keep a promise that somebody else has to pay for."
"Which promise?"
"Any promise." Her eyes did not leave his face. "A father's oath. A king's word. A vow made in a room with a stone table and a priest counting on his fingers. A boon that someone asked for, and was given, and can't be taken back. Would you keep it? If keeping it meant breaking something else?"
The silence stretched. The lamp burned. Ram thought of the Chamber of Oaths, and the black slab with its boons carved in dust, and his father's swollen hands, shaking in his lap. He thought of the sage's face when he had said one boy is what I can carry. He thought of the crack running round the grey woman's ribs, and the sound of the bow splitting in his hands.
"I'd keep my word," he said. "Mine. Not anyone else's."
"And if your word was the thing causing the harm?"
"Then I'd pay for it."
"With your life?"
"With whatever I had."
"And if you had nothing?" Her voice was quiet, almost gentle. "If you had only other people's lives?"
Ram looked up.
She was watching him very carefully, her head still propped on her hand, her black hair tangled across the white pillow. There was no judgement in her face. There was only a kind of tired patience, the patience of someone who had been asked this question by a great many people and had never once received an answer she believed.
"Then I'd pay with mine first," Ram said. "And I'd hope it was enough."
Sita let out a long slow breath. Something in her face eased, a little, though not all the way.
"That," she said, "is what a man says when he hasn't yet been asked to pay with someone else's."
"Maybe."
"Maybe." She sat up slowly. The silk slid off one shoulder, and she did not bother to pull it back. She looked at the window, at the scattered lamps of the city beyond the palace walls, and her face in the lamplight was grave and far away. "My father kept the bow in the glass because he was told to. The sentinels guard it because they were paid to. The priests say the contest is a blessing. But I've been thinking, since the morning, that the bow was never the thing that mattered. What mattered was the men who got to decide what it meant. They're the ones who ran the house, and the city, and every story that was told about the bow for five hundred years. And none of them ever had to stand in front of it and be the one who lifted it."
"Until today," said Ram.
"Until today." She turned back to him. "And now you have to decide what it means. For yourself. For your father's house. For the men who saw you break it and are already deciding what to say about you at dinner tomorrow. Whoever keeps the custom gets to decide what it's for. You understand that, don't you?"
"Yes."
"Good." She lay back down, slowly, and looked at the ceiling. "Because I think that's the question that's going to matter. Not whether you can lift the bow. Whether you'll be the one who decides what happens when you put it down."
Ram did not answer. He sat on the edge of the bed with his hands on the cotton, and listened to her breathing, and watched the lamp flicker on the sill. After a while her breathing slowed and evened out, and her eyes closed, and her face relaxed into the soft unguarded lines of sleep, and he realised she had fallen asleep in the middle of the conversation, as though she had said everything she had come to say and did not need to wait for his reply.
He lay down beside her, on top of the covers, in his clothes. He did not sleep for a long time. He lay with his eyes open and his hands at his sides, and thought about the word keep, and the word pay, and the word decide, and the difference between them, which seemed to him very small and very large at once.
Somewhere below, in the palace courtyard, the fountain went on hissing in its basin, spraying up its silver column of water into the dark, and falling, and rising again, and falling, without ever once asking what it was for.
---
Lakshman came to their door before dawn, in a state of such uncharacteristic agitation that Ram thought at first something had happened to Urmila.
"It's not her," Lakshman said, reading his face. He was barefoot and dishevelled, his shirt half-buttoned, and he had clearly been running. "It's Sumant. He's come from Ayodhya. He's in the audience hall. He says the King wants us home."
"Why?"
Lakshman's mouth worked. He glanced over his shoulder, down the stairs, as though he expected someone to be listening.
"Because Kaikeyi has called her boons," he said quietly.

CHAPTER : SARGA VIII: The Boons
> **[THE ROAD TAKEN]**
Sumant had ridden three days without stopping, and he looked it. His tunic was stained with road-dust and sweat, his face was grey and hollow, and he held a sealed document in both hands as though he were afraid it might try to escape. He bowed to Ram without rising from the stool where he had been sitting, and when he stood, his legs shook, and he had to steady himself on the back of a chair.
"You need to read this," he said. "The King signed it himself, this morning, before I left. He had me swear I would deliver it into your hand and no one else's."
Ram broke the seal. The document was short. It was written in the King's own cramped, unsteady script, the letters sprawling and uneven as though he had been forced to make them one stroke at a time.
My son. Come home. Your mother has come to the Chamber and has asked for the two boons I gave her, long ago, on the night of the fourth year of her marriage. I could not refuse her. I do not believe I should have refused her. Come quickly, before the Board decides what the boons are worth. Come before they decide what you are worth. Your father, Dasharatha, who has no right to ask you for anything.
Ram read it twice. Then he folded it carefully and put it in the inner pocket of his tunic.
"What are the boons?" Sita asked from the doorway.
She had dressed quickly, in a plain white cotton that made her look very young and very severe. Her hair was tied back again. She had not asked Ram whether she could come. She had simply come.
Sumant looked at her. Something in his face shifted, a faint wariness, as though he had been told in the city that the bow-breaker's wife was a woman who could not be spoken to lightly.
"Two wishes," he said. "My lady. The King granted them to his second queen on the night she saved his life in a chariot accident in the old days. He promised, in front of the stone, that he would grant any two requests she made of him, whenever she made them, however long after. The stone holds the oath. The priest holds the record. The King is bound by it."
"And what does she ask?" Sita said.
Sumant swallowed.
"That her son Bharat be crowned in the eldest's place," he said, "and that the eldest be sent into the forest for fourteen years."
---
They rode back to Ayodhya in a Board freight pod that Sumant had requisitioned from the Mithila rail-depot, a long grey shell with padded benches and smoked-glass windows and an air-conditioning system that made a low continuous rattle like teeth chattering. It took two days. For most of them Ram did not speak. He sat by the window and watched the country go past: the flat green fields of the Mithila plain giving way to dry scrub, the canals grey and sluggish under a sky the colour of pewter, the villages small and silent in the heat. Once they passed a stone milestone with a name carved on it, worn almost to nothing. Once they passed a line of women walking along the verge with bundles on their heads, moving north, and one of them looked up as the pod slid by, and her eyes met his through the smoked glass, and then she looked away.
Lakshman sat across from him, his bow across his knees. He had not let it go since the broken bow in Mithila. Urmila had given him a second one as a wedding gift, plainer and lighter than the first, of black wood with a string of braided silk. He held it now as he had held the old one on the road, as though it were something that might be taken from him at any moment.
"Harit will be at the gate," he said, somewhere past the second day.
"Probably."
"He'll be waiting for us with the towers' guard. Four hundred men, at least. Maybe more."
"Why would he need four hundred men to meet two princes and a bride?"
Lakshman turned the bow over in his hands. "Because by now he'll have decided what we are," he said. "And he'll want to be sure we know it before we walk through his gates."
They came into Ayodhya at dusk, on the second day, and the city was not the city Ram had left.
The grid of the ashtapada blocks was still there, the highways still humming, the freight pods still sliding along their rails in long glittering processions. The glass of the spires still caught the last of the sun and burned gold against the darkening sky. But the lamps had changed. On every corner, every junction, every raised platform above the highways, a new light had been mounted: a flat white panel, bright and steady, angled down toward the road. And on the panels, slowly rotating, a single image played over and over, too far away for Ram to make out clearly at first, until the pod turned onto the great avenue that led to the south gate, and the image came up in front of him, huge and luminous, the size of a building.
It was a face. It was his face. Not a painting, not a sketch, but a shot taken from the feeds, a grainy image of a boy standing on a copper roof with a broken bow across his knees, his hands spread wide, his mouth open as though he were about to say something. The text beneath it was in the Board's pale modern script, and it said, in three words that burned through the smoked glass and into the back of his skull:
THE RETURNING PRINCE.
Lakshman let out a long breath. "They've been running that since noon," he said quietly. "Sumant said they started on the feeds the day after the messenger left."
"Who wrote the text?"
"Who do you think?"
Ram looked at the face in the light. The boy in it was not him. He knew that as clearly as he knew his own hands. The boy had been cut from a longer shot, edited to fit the Board's frame, the expression chosen from a dozen others to make him seem purposeful and grave. Somebody had taken a moment of stillness and turned it into a promise.
"Let's go in," he said.
---
The palace was lit from within when they reached it, every window glowing, every corridor full of people moving quickly and speaking in low voices. Servants stood in clusters on the stairs and fell silent as the three of them passed. Councillors in grey robes hurried past without looking up. At the foot of the great stair a line of guards in the Board's black uniform stood at attention, their rifles grounded, their faces blank, and as Ram came up the steps, one of them lifted a hand, very slowly, and touched the brim of his helmet in a salute that was not quite a salute at all.
The Chamber of Oaths was open.
Its great doors had been folded back against the walls, and the light from within spilled across the threshold in a long pale bar. Ram walked through it.
The hall was crowded. Ministers stood in ranks along the pillars, their faces grave and watchful. Vasishtha sat on his black stone stool beside the slab, his white beard combed flat, his eyes half-closed, his lips moving soundlessly. Sumant had come in ahead of them and taken his place at the king's left. Harit stood at the back of the hall, near the pillar where the petitioners were supposed to wait, a tall man in a black tunic with silver epaulettes on his shoulders, and when he saw Ram come in, he did not move. He only looked, with an expression of cool, deliberate appraisal, and then he looked away.
The king sat in his high chair at the far end of the hall. He had not moved since Ram had last seen him. His hands were in his lap. His face had sunk further, the skin grey and loose across the cheekbones, and his eyes, when they found Ram across the length of the hall, were wet and enormous and utterly without hope.
Kaikeyi stood beside the slab.
Ram had not seen his stepmother in four years. She had grown thinner, or perhaps only older, and the silk of her sari was a deep unrelieved green that made her skin look pale as parchment. Her hair was pinned back severely. Her face was calm. There was no triumph in it, and no regret. She stood with one hand resting lightly on the black basalt, her fingers tracing the dust-filled carvings, and when she saw Ram, she smiled, very faintly, the way a woman smiles at a child who has come home late from a long journey.
"There he is," she said. "The Returning Prince."
Somebody in the ranks of ministers laughed, a short ugly sound that stopped as soon as it had begun.
Ram walked the length of the hall. The floor was cold under his feet. The petitioners' square was empty now, the worn stone bare, and he walked across it and stopped at its edge, where the shadow of the basalt slab lay across the floor like a dark ribbon, and he stood there and looked at his father.
"You came," said Dasharatha.
"Yes."
"I asked you not to."
"You asked me to come home," said Ram. "I did."
The king's face crumpled. It was not a dramatic movement. It was small and quick and terrible, like a paper fan folding in a hot wind, and then it was smoothed away, and the king's hands were shaking in his lap again, and he looked down at them, and did not look up.
"Ram," said Vasishtha quietly from the stool.
Ram did not turn. He was looking at Kaikeyi, who had not taken her hand from the stone.
"Tell me the boons," he said.
Kaikeyi's smile widened a fraction. "You know them," she said. "Sumant will have told you on the road. He's a conscientious man. He gets very nervous when he has to deliver bad news."
"I want to hear them from you."
"Very well." She took her hand from the slab. She did not move closer. She stood with her back very straight, and her voice when it came was calm and clear and pitched to carry across the whole length of the hall, so that every minister along the pillars could hear it. "My son Bharat is the heir. He was promised the throne by his father, in the fourth year of our marriage, when I saved the King's life. The King swore before this stone that he would grant me two requests, whenever I chose to make them. I have chosen. My first request is that Bharat be crowned king of Kosala in the eldest's place. My second is that the eldest be sent into the forest of Dandaka for fourteen years, and that he not return before that time has passed."
The hall was perfectly still.
"That is all," said Kaikeyi. "I am a simple woman. I ask only what the stone allows."
Ram looked at her for a long moment. He could see the calculation behind her calm, the careful precision of a woman who had spent a lifetime knowing exactly where the edges of her power lay, and who had come to this room with a single question and was waiting now, very patiently, to see how the men around her would answer it.
He could also see that she was afraid.
It was not much. It was a faint tremor at the corner of her mouth, a tightness at the base of her throat, the breath held a fraction too long. But it was there. And Ram understood, with a clarity that felt almost painful, that she had not asked for these boons for herself. She had asked because she had been told, long ago, that one day the house would need a reason to fall, and she had wanted to make sure that when it did, the reason would be hers.
"Your son is not here," Ram said.
"He is on the road from Kekaya. He will arrive by the end of the week." Her eyes did not leave his face. "He will not refuse what his father has promised."
"No," said Ram. "I don't suppose he will."
He turned back to the king.
Dasharatha had raised his head at last. His eyes were red-rimmed and blind, and he was looking at Ram as though he were looking at something very far away, the way a man looks at a shore he has long since given up hope of reaching.
"Refuse her," the king whispered. "Ram. Please. Refuse her."
The silence in the hall deepened. Somewhere far above, in the vaulted roof, a pigeon shifted on a ledge and cooed once, softly, and was still.
Ram knelt.
He did it slowly, deliberately, the way he had done everything since the morning he had stood in this room and told the sage he would go. He went down onto one knee on the cold stone, at the edge of the petitioners' square, and bowed his head, and held it there for a long moment. When he lifted his eyes again, his father was staring at him with an expression of such naked, helpless horror that Ram felt something in his chest give way, like a rope that has been pulled tight for too long and finally parts.
"You asked me to refuse," he said quietly. "And I won't. Because you didn't ask me to break the stone. You asked me to keep it. I was raised to keep it. So were you."
"Ram—"
"I'll go."
The king made a sound. It was not a word. It was the sound of a man who has been holding his breath for a very long time and has finally been forced to let it out, all at once, and it broke in the middle, and dissolved into something raw and wet and undignified that he could not stop.
Kaikeyi did not move. But her hand, where it rested against the black stone, had curled into a fist.
Behind Ram, in the open doors of the hall, Ram heard someone come running up the steps. Heavy boots on marble, quick and urgent. Then a voice, young and cracked, calling out from the threshold:
"Mother! What have you done?"
Ram turned his head.
A young man stood in the doorway, dressed in the dusty travelling clothes of the Kekaya roads, his face flushed and damp with sweat, his chest heaving from the run. He was perhaps twenty. He had his mother's clean narrow features and a clear, unguarded expression that the palace had never managed to train out of him, and his eyes went from Kaikeyi to the king weeping in the high chair, and then to Ram, kneeling on the stone.
"Bharat," said Kaikeyi, very softly.
"You've called the boons." Bharat's voice shook. He came forward, slowly, into the hall, past the ministers and the pillars, until he stood at the edge of the petitioners' square beside his brother. He did not look at his mother again. He looked only at Ram, and his face was white. "You've sent him into the forest. For this? For a crown?"
"It is my right," said Kaikeyi.
"It is my father's oath," said Bharat, and his voice rose suddenly, sharp and clear, so that it rang off the vaulted stone. "It is not your right. It was never yours to ask for."
He turned back to Ram. For a long moment the two brothers looked at each other across the space between them, and Ram saw in Bharat's face the same thing he had seen in Lakshman's face on the road, the same raw helpless anger that had no place to go.
"Get up," said Bharat. "Get up, Ram. You're not going to kneel in front of her."
Ram stood. He did it slowly, without taking his eyes from his brother's face.
"You don't have to refuse the throne for me," he said.
"I'm not refusing it for you." Bharat's hands had clenched at his sides. "I'm refusing it for the stone. I'm refusing it because if I take a crown this way, I'll be sitting in a chair that a brother walked out of for a woman's grudge. I'll be the one who had the boons granted. I won't live that way. I won't live in a palace built on someone else's exile."
He turned to the king.
"Father," he said. "Let him stay. Let me go into the forest in his place, if the stone needs a body in it. I'll go. I'll take the boons and walk into the dead country, and no one in Kosala will ever have to say that Ram was sent away for my mother's sake."
"No," said Ram.
Bharat looked at him, startled.
"No," Ram said again, and his voice was quiet and clear. "You're not going into the forest for me, Bharat. And you're not refusing the throne for me, either. Father swore an oath before the stone. He's bound by it. So am I. If you break it on my behalf, you'll be breaking his word and mine, and then there won't be anyone left in this room who can say what the house of Ikshvaku was for."
Bharat's face crumpled, suddenly, into something young and helpless and furious. He looked at the king, and at Vasishtha on his black stool, and at the ministers along the pillars, who had gone very still and were watching the two sons with an expression of bright, unwilling hunger, like men watching a dispute they already knew would be worth a great deal of money.
"Then I won't take it," said Bharat hoarsely. "Not the throne. Not a single day of it. You can tell the Board that. You can tell the whole city that Dasharatha's second son refused the crown in front of the stone, and that his mother was the one who asked for it."
Kaikeyi said nothing. Her face had gone very white.
At the back of the hall, by the pillar where the petitioners waited, Harit stood with his arms folded, watching the scene with an expression of deep, interested calm, the look of a man who has just seen something he had been waiting a long time to see, and has begun to calculate what it is worth.
---
They gave Ram until the next morning to say his goodbyes.
He spent the evening in his father's room, sitting on the edge of the bed while the king lay propped against the pillows with his eyes closed and his hands folded on the coverlet. The room was dark. A single lamp burned on the table by the window, and in its light Dasharatha's face looked very old, the skin fine and papery and marked with the fine brown spots of the medicines he had been taking for two years. He did not speak for a long time. Ram did not speak either. He had nothing he could say that would make the room any less dark.
At last the king opened his eyes.
"You were always going to do it," he said, very softly. "Weren't you?"
"Yes."
"Even when you were small. You used to sit in the gardens with your books and wait for the bees to land on your hands. You never once tried to brush them off. The gardeners used to say you'd be stung one day. You never were."
"I didn't want to frighten them."
"No." The king's hand moved on the coverlet, a faint shiver of the fingers. "No. I know you didn't."
He reached out. Ram took the hand. It was light and dry and very cold, and the tremor in it was steady, a long, slow vibration that seemed to come from somewhere deep within the bone.
"Keep your word," said Dasharatha. "Ram. Whatever it costs. Keep it. Don't let them make you break it for a crown. Don't let them tell you it was for the good of the house. A broken word is the only thing a man can never get back."
"I know."
"Do you?" The king's eyes were wet. "I didn't. Not when I was your age. I thought a word was a thing you gave away. I didn't know it could be taken back out of your hands and used to hurt you, by people who had never once in their lives kept a promise of their own."
Ram held the cold hand a little tighter.
"I'll keep it," he said.
Dasharatha closed his eyes again, and let out a long slow breath, and after a while his grip on Ram's fingers slackened, and his breathing settled into the soft shallow rhythm of sleep. Ram sat beside him in the dark for a long time, listening to the lamp hiss on the table and the water of the fountain hiss faintly in the courtyard below, and when at last he got up to leave, the king's lips moved, very faintly, in the shape of a name that Ram did not hear.
He did not look back. He had learned, from the glass of the east spire, that the things you could not see would still be there when you turned around.
---
CHAPTER : SARGA IX: The Panel
> **[THE ROAD TAKEN]**
Harit came after midnight.
Ram had not been asleep. He had been sitting on the roof of the east spire in the dark, on the low stone wall at the rim of the garden, where he could see the whole city laid out below him like a circuit board turned face-up. The grid of the ashtapada blocks, each one lit from within by its cold blue. The highways, humming. The Board's towers, rising from the centre of the citadel in their clusters of glass, their upper floors dark, their lower floors pulsing with a faint restless light, as though something inside them were breathing too quickly. And along the outer ramparts, the hundred-killers, their long black barrels catching the moon.
They had not moved since the morning test. The engines on the north wall had been silent all evening. Ram had listened for them for an hour, the way he had listened for them every morning of his life, and had heard nothing, and the silence had felt like the held breath before a blow.
He heard the stair door open behind him. He did not turn.
"Prince," said Harit.
His voice was pleasant and even, with the faint rasp of a man who had spent his life giving orders across parade grounds. Ram turned then. The commander of the towers' guard stood at the top of the stair with his hands clasped behind his back. Two men stood a step below him, in black tunics with silver at the shoulder, their rifles slung across their chests and their faces carefully blank. Behind them, in the stairwell, the light was dim and yellow, and Ram could see the shape of another figure hovering at the bottom of the steps, half-hidden in the shadow of the wall.
"You've come a long way to sit on a roof," said Harit.
"It's quiet up here."
"It's quieter below, if you know where to listen." Harit took a step forward, onto the roof, and his men followed him. "May I speak with you?"
"Yes."
"Alone, I thought."
"No," said Ram. "My brother stays."
The figure in the stairwell shifted, and Lakshman came up the last few steps into the moonlight, the new black bow across his back and his face set like a closed door. He did not look at Harit. He looked at Ram, and in his eyes was a question that he would not ask in front of strangers.
Harit smiled. It was not an unfriendly smile. It was the smile of a man who has been told he will have to work harder than he expected, and has decided to enjoy it.
"Very well," he said. "Your brother can hear what the city needs."
---
They sat in the glass room at the top of the stair, the one with the long low table and the window that looked out over the whole eastern half of the citadel. The room had been used for council meetings in the old days, before the Board moved its offices into the towers and left the spire to the royal family and their servants. The table was bare. There were no chairs except the three that Lakshman dragged across the floor, and Harit took the one nearest the window and sat with his back to the glass and his hands folded on his knee.
Sita had not come up. Ram had seen her briefly at the foot of the stair, before Harit's men had arrived. She had been standing beside the doorway to the eastern wing, very still, with her arms folded and her face pale in the lamplight, and when she saw him look at her she had nodded once, very slightly, and gone back into the darkness of the corridor. He had not known whether she would stay there. He had not known whether he wanted her to.
"The Board is finished," Harit said.
He said it without preamble, in the same even voice he had used on the roof. He did not look at the window. He looked at Ram.
"Not dead yet," he went on. "Not yet. But finished. They have been sitting in their glass offices for thirty years, signing forms, counting money, sending clerks into the forest with cutting machines and calling it progress. They have never once had to explain themselves to anyone who could make them stop. And now the King is dying in his chair. The second queen has called the boons. The heir has refused the crown in front of the stone. And the eldest has been sent into the forest like a bad debt." He spread his hands, very slightly, the gesture of a man laying out a set of cards. "There is no one left in this city with the authority to decide what happens next. Except the man who holds the towers."
"And who holds the towers?" asked Lakshman.
Harit's gaze moved to him, and then back to Ram, without any change in his expression.
"You know who," he said. "The engines answer to the house. They always have. The old men built them that way, so that no clerk could ever turn them against the throne. You need a hand on the panel, and a pulse, and a single word. The house's pulse, the house's hand, the house's word. Nobody else can do it. Nobody else has ever been able to."
"You want me to kill the Board," said Ram.
"I want you to take the city back." Harit's voice did not rise. "I want you to put your hand on the panel tonight and tell the engines to face the towers instead of the south road. My men will walk through the gates of the Board's offices in twenty minutes. They will arrest the council. They will close the freight rails and the brokers' accounts. They will stop the stripping in the forest by morning, Prince, and they will hang every man who sold a village to a salt pan. Not one of those things is a crime against the stone. Every one of them is what the stone was written to allow."
"And the King?"
"The King will sign whatever the house commands him to sign." Harit's mouth moved in something close to pity. "He is an old man, and he is grieving, and he has no will left to resist anything. He will be glad to sign. He will be glad it was you."
"And the boons?" Lakshman said. His voice was low and tight. "The second queen's, and the stone's? What do they become?"
"Nothing," said Harit simply. "They are a family quarrel. A new authority supersedes an old oath, and the King, who swore it, will be the first to say so. The stone does not care who holds the hand that signs it, boy. It only cares that the house keeps the custom."
Lakshman was on his feet. The chair scraped back across the floor.
Harit did not move. He sat with his hands folded on his knee and looked up at Lakshman with the same level calm, and when he spoke again, his voice was softer.
"Sit down," he said. "You're the one who will have to live with it. Think about that before you start shouting at me in your brother's house."
Lakshman stood for a long moment, his fists clenched at his sides, his breath coming hard through his nose. Then he sat down again, slowly, his eyes never leaving Harit's face, and said nothing.
Ram had not moved.
He sat on the stool with his hands resting on the bare table, and he looked at the window. Beyond it the city lay in its grid of blue light, and the towers rose from its heart with their breathing lights, and along the northern ramparts the hundred-killers stood with their long barrels turned toward the empty dark, waiting. He could see the shape of the panel in his mind's eye. He had seen it on the day he came back from the south, when Sumant had shown him the old control room beneath the north rampart, the one the palace had kept sealed since his grandfather's time: a long slab of black glass set into the stone wall, with a single pale ring of light at its centre and a row of amber indicators beneath it. A hand's width of cold smooth surface. He had touched it once, out of curiosity, and the ring had flickered, and then gone dark again, and Sumant had said quietly that it would not wake for anyone who had not been born to the house.
He thought about the pulse. He thought about the bow in the glass case at Mithila, the thin pale line of light pulsing around its rim, reading him, deciding whether he was fit to lift what was inside. He thought about the payload in the hermitage, and the four red dots going green under his palm, and the long slow count that had turned the hum in his wrist into something almost like silence.
And he understood, with a clarity so sharp it was almost physical, that Harit was not lying.
It would work. Tonight. The engines would turn. The towers would go dark, one by one, as the Board's officers were pulled from their chairs. The brokers' accounts would freeze. The salt pans would go quiet. The sandal with its double-looped knot would be found, perhaps, in some village house, and the child who had worn it would be returned to her mother's arms, or to a grave, and the city would hear about it on the feeds in the morning and would not know what to think. A clean, quick, almost bloodless seizure, done in a single night by a single man with a steady heartbeat and his hand on a sheet of cold black glass.
It would fix everything.
It would fix everything except the one thing that could not be fixed by fixing anything else.
He lifted his hand from the table. He held it in the air between himself and the window, palm out, and looked at it in the blue light from the city. It was very steady. He could see the faint pale lines across the palm, the lines of the grooves on the payload, the marks of the grey mineral from the river. He thought of the grey woman's hands, cold and long-fingered, reaching for the door.
"Show me," he said.
---
The panel was in the old control room beneath the north rampart, at the bottom of a long stair that smelled of damp stone and machine oil. Harit led the way with a lamp in one hand. His men walked behind, and Lakshman walked behind them, and Ram came last, his footsteps echoing off the walls.
The room was round and low, with a ceiling of black iron ribs and a floor of pale tile that had cracked in long lines, as though something heavy had been dropped on it many years ago and never removed. In the centre of the room stood a single chair, of dark wood, its seat worn smooth. Facing the chair, set into the curve of the far wall, was the panel.
It was larger than Ram remembered. Its surface was black and glossy, and when Harit lifted the lamp, it caught the light and gave it back in a faint shimmer, so that for a moment the glass seemed to be full of moving shapes. A row of amber indicators ran along its base, dark. Above them, at the height of a man's chest, the pale ring of light sat in the glass like a single open eye.
Harit set the lamp on the floor. He stepped back.
"It's yours," he said.
Ram walked to the panel. The tiles were cold under his bare feet. He did not look back at the others. He stood in front of the glass and looked at the ring of light, which had begun, very faintly, to pulse.
The pulse was slow. It matched his heart. He could feel it, in the air, in the cold stone, the way he had felt the bow's humming in his teeth at Mithila, a steady rhythmic presence that had been waiting for him in the dark for two hundred years.
He lifted his right hand and laid it flat against the glass.
It was cold. Colder than the river had been. The surface gave very slightly under his palm, the way a skin of water gives under the weight of a leaf, and then it held him, and he felt the pulse come up through the glass and into the bones of his wrist, and into his arm, and into the centre of his chest, and it was matching him, beat for beat, as if the panel had been waiting all these years for the exact rhythm of his heart.
The amber indicators came on, one by one, along the base of the glass.
Behind him, he heard Harit exhale, very softly, as though someone had finally handed him something he had been holding out for a long time.
The panel did not speak. It did not need to. The words appeared in the glass itself, rising slowly out of the black surface in pale script, the same clean modern script as the Board's feeds, as though the old machine had learned to speak the new language in order to ask its question in terms the new men would understand:
AUTHORISE. CONFIRM HOUSE PULSE. DIRECT THE ENGINES.
Below the words, a single blank line waited, with a cursor blinking at its end.
Say it, Harit said quietly, from the dark.
Ram did not turn. He stood with his palm against the glass and felt the pulse move through him, steady and strong and entirely his own, and he saw his face faintly reflected in the black surface, pale and very still, and behind it the shapes of Harit's men and the bowed head of his brother, and far back in the shadows the lamp on the floor throwing long wavering shadows up the curved iron ribs of the ceiling.
Four, two, six.
It came easily. It came as easily as breathing.
He thought of the engines turning on the north wall, their long barrels swinging south, then east, then west, then back to the towers, where the lights would go out one by one. He thought of the men in grey walking up the great stair of the Board's glass office, and the clerks sitting at their desks with their hands in their laps, and the council members being led out in their grey robes, blinking, bewildered, into the bright new lamps on the corners of the avenue. He thought of the lamps themselves, flat and white and turning, showing his face over and over to the whole city until it stopped being his face and became something that the city had agreed to need.
He thought of the first decree. It would be a good one. He was sure of that. It would say that the salt-road ordinance was void, that the stripping was forbidden, that the forest would be given back to the villages that had lived in it. It would be signed by the King, who would weep when he read it. It would be read out on the feeds by a man in a black tunic, and it would be obeyed, because every engine on the rampart would be pointed at the men who did not obey it.
And the man who had written it would be the one who had taken the towers with a single steady word and a single pulse. Not a king. Not a sage. Not a boy who had walked out of a hall because his father could not look at him.
A man who would never again have to ask anyone's permission to be right.
Ram took his hand from the glass.
The moment the skin left the surface, the pulse stopped. The ring of light went dark. The amber indicators along the base of the panel dimmed, one by one, and went out, and the black glass was just black glass again, dull and cold, with his own reflection faintly visible in it, pale and tired and entirely still.
The silence in the room was enormous.
"What did you do?" Harit asked. His voice was very quiet.
"I didn't answer it," said Ram.
"You had the panel. You had the pulse. You had the whole city in your hand, boy, and you took it off the glass like a child taking his fingers off a hot plate."
"Yes."
"Why?"
Ram turned around. Harit stood a few paces from the chair, with the lamp at his feet and his men in the shadows behind him, and his face in the wavering light was no longer calm. It was something else entirely: a hard, bright, startled anger, the anger of a man who has just watched a door he had built with his own hands swing shut in front of him.
"Because it asked me to decide what it was for," said Ram. "And I already know what it would become if I did."
"It would become order."
"It would become my order," said Ram quietly. "The city would forget the engines were ever anyone else's. The Board would be gone, and the council, and the brokers, and the stripping. And then the next time someone needed the towers, they'd come to the panel, and they'd put their hand on the glass, and they'd find the man who had taken it first was still there, waiting for them to ask. And they would ask. And he would answer."
"You think you're better than that."
"I don't know if I am. I know I'd have to live every day knowing I could do it again, and that the only thing stopping me was whether I felt like it." Ram looked at the dark panel. "I won't put my hand on that glass, Harit. Not tonight. Not for any reason you can give me."
Harit's jaw worked. For a long moment he did not speak. Then he breathed out through his nose, slowly, and his shoulders dropped, very slightly, and when he spoke again his voice had gone flat and cold.
"Then the towers will answer someone else," he said.
"I know."
"You don't know. You don't know what it's like out there, in the wards, when the Board's clerks start disappearing and the freight rails go quiet. You don't know what the old men will say when the feeds run their clip again tomorrow, and the people start asking who the Returning Prince was really coming home to save." He took a step toward Ram. His men shifted in the dark behind him. "They'll say you walked out on the city. They'll say you let them take it. And in a year, when they need someone to stand on the north wall and tell the engines what to do, they'll come looking for you, and they'll find you on the road, and you'll have to explain to them why you didn't take the chance you were given."
"Then I'll explain it," said Ram.
"To whom?"
"To them."
Harit laughed. It was a short, harsh sound, without any humour in it. "You'll come back," he said. "They always come back, the ones who walk away from a throne. They can't stop themselves."
"Then they'll find I'm the same man who walked away," said Ram.
Harit stared at him for a long time. Then he turned, very slowly, and looked at Lakshman, who had not moved from the foot of the chair, and whose face in the lamplight had gone very pale.
"And you?" Harit said. "Are you going to walk away too?"
Lakshman did not answer at once. His hand had moved to the black bow on his back, and his fingers were resting on the wood, very lightly, the way a man's fingers rest on the edge of a cliff.
"He's my brother," he said at last, in a rough low voice.
"I know that."
"Then you know I'm going with him."
Harit's mouth twisted. "You'd let the city go for a man who won't even put his hand on the glass?"
"I'd let the city go," said Lakshman, "rather than watch him put his hand on it and become something I can't follow."
It was the closest Ram had ever heard his brother come to saying something true about himself without first making it into a joke. Lakshman's eyes were wet. He did not wipe them.
Harit looked at them both for a long moment, and then, very slowly, he picked up the lamp from the floor.
"The towers will answer someone," he said. "They always do."
He turned and walked toward the stair. His men fell in behind him without a word. At the foot of the stone steps, Harit paused, and looked back over his shoulder, and his face in the lamplight was no longer angry. It was simply tired, and older than Ram had thought it was, and very sad.
"You had the chance," he said quietly. "You should know that. Nobody else in this city will ever be handed the same chance again."
Then he climbed the stair, and his footsteps went up and up through the dark until they were gone.
---
Lakshman sat down on the cracked tile floor with his back against the chair and his bow across his knees, and put his face in his hands.
He did not weep. He sat like that for a long time, breathing hard, his shoulders rising and falling, and Ram stood in front of the dark panel and did not go to him, because he did not know yet what his brother needed, and because he was afraid that if he moved, the thing in his chest that had been holding still for the last hour would come loose all at once.
At last Lakshman lifted his head.
"You could have done it," he said. His voice was hoarse. "You know that. You could have ended all of it tonight."
"Yes."
"And you didn't."
"No."
"Why?" Lakshman looked up at him with raw bewildered eyes. "I'm not angry. I just need to understand. Tell me the truth, Ram. Tell me why."
Ram looked at the panel. The black glass gave him back his own face, very faint, very pale.
"Because the first thing I'd have done," he said slowly, "is decide that I knew what everyone else needed. And I'd have been right, mostly. And then I'd have decided that being right was enough to keep doing it. And after that there wouldn't have been anything left to stop me."
Lakshman was silent for a long moment.
"That's not a reason," he said at last. "That's a fear."
"Yes," said Ram. "It's both."
His brother let out a long breath, and then, slowly, he began to laugh. It was a broken, exhausted sound, half sob and half something else, and it went on for a while, echoing off the iron ribs of the ceiling, and when it stopped, Lakshman wiped his face with the back of his arm and stood up, stiffly, and picked up his bow.
"Fine," he said. "Then I'll be the one who doesn't need to be right."
---
Sita was waiting at the top of the stair.
She had not come down. She had stayed in the dark corridor of the eastern wing, where she could see the stairwell and the roof door and the faint blue glow of the city beyond the glass, and she had listened to everything. Ram could see it in her face. It was pale and very still, and her eyes were fixed on him as he came up the last steps, with an expression that was neither relief nor reproach but something quieter and more thorough, as though she were reading a document she had been given and was determined to understand every line of it before she spoke.
"You didn't take it," she said.
"No."
"I heard the panel. I could hear the pulse through the wall. It was slow. Then it stopped." She tilted her head slightly. "What did it say?"
"It asked me to answer."
"And you didn't."
"No."
Sita was quiet for a moment. Then she stepped forward, out of the shadow, and lifted her hand, very slowly, and laid two fingers against the inside of his wrist, where the pulse beat under the skin. She held them there, without pressing, and her eyes did not leave his face.
"It's steady," she said softly.
"Yes."
"It always is, with you. I don't think you know how much that frightens people." She let her hand fall. "They'll say you were afraid to act. They'll say you let the city burn for your conscience."
"Maybe they'll be right."
"Maybe." She looked past him, at the dark stair, and the blue light of the city beyond the spire's glass. Her face was tired, and very grave, and something in it had hardened, though not against him. "It isn't a comfort, is it? Refusing a thing you could have done. It doesn't feel like anything at all, at first. It only feels like a door you've closed, and then you find yourself standing in front of it for the rest of your life."
"Yes," said Ram.
Sita nodded slowly. "Then we'll stand in front of it together," she said. "And we'll see who comes to knock."
---
Ram stood on the roof garden for a long time after the others had gone below. The night was very still. The moon had set behind the western towers, and the sky above the citadel was a deep dark blue, pricked with a few thin stars. Far off, beyond the ramparts and the moat and the grey fields, the faint outline of the forest rose against the horizon, a band of deeper darkness under the stars.
He looked at his right hand. It was lying open at his side. In the faint light from the city he could see the place where it had pressed against the glass: a small, dull, reddish mark across the palm, where the cold had raised the skin in a faint rim, like the mark of a seal pressed into warm wax. It would fade by morning. He knew that. It would not fade from the rest of him.
Below the rampart, in the old control room, the panel was still dark. He did not need to see it to know. He could feel its absence, somewhere beneath the stone, the way you feel a tooth that has been pulled, a space that will remain long after the ache has gone.
The towers will answer someone, Harit had said.
He wondered who.
He wondered whether the man who would one day put his hand on that glass would be a stranger, or a friend, or a man he had once been able to recognise in his own face. He wondered whether that man would know the count, or whether he had never had any reason to learn it. He wondered whether, somewhere in the city, Harit was standing at a window of his own, looking up at the spire, waiting to see what the boy from the forest would do next.
He did not know. He only knew that the glass had asked him a question, and he had not answered it, and that the question would come back. It would come back in a year or in ten. It would come back in a different voice, with a different face, and it would ask him the same thing, in the same clean modern script, and somewhere inside himself he would hear the pulse begin to quicken.
He would have to count.
He began now, quietly, in the dark, on the roof of the east spire, with the city asleep below him and the forest waiting on the horizon.
Four. Two. Six.

CHAPTER : SARGA X: The Gate
> **[THE ROAD TAKEN]**
They left Ayodhya in the first grey light of the morning, as the hundred-killers began their test.
The sound came rolling across the citadel from the north rampart, the long low hum that Ram had woken to every day of his life, and tonight, as the engines turned on their mounts and their long barrels swung slowly through the dawn, it seemed to him that the hum had changed. It was not louder. It was more deliberate. It had the sound of something that had been told where to look, and was looking.
He stood on the palace steps with his bag on his shoulder and watched the engines turn.
Sumant was waiting at the foot of the stair with a face like paper. He had not slept. He had spent the night, Lakshman said, in the Board's office in the lower tower, trying to keep the council from tearing itself apart, and had come back at dawn with ink on his fingers and an expression of such raw, exhausted bewilderment that he could hardly speak. He bowed to Ram now, very low, and held the bow for a long time, and when he straightened, his eyes were full.
"They'll remember you for this," he said hoarsely.
"For what?"
"For not taking it." Sumant wiped his face with the back of his hand. "I've been in government for thirty-one years, prince. I have never once seen a man walk away from a throne he could have had for the asking. I don't know whether to call you a fool or a saint."
"Neither," said Ram. "Call me a man who was asked."
Sumant almost smiled. Then he looked past Ram, up the steps, and his face changed.
Vasishtha was coming down from the palace door.
The high priest moved slowly, leaning on a staff of white wood, his robes the colour of bone in the grey light. His eyes were half-closed, as they always were, and his lips moved faintly as he walked, the same silent counting Ram had seen in the Chamber of Oaths. He did not hurry. When he reached the foot of the stair, he stopped beside Sumant and looked up at Ram for a long moment, and his face was calm and grave and utterly unreadable.
"You have chosen the forest," he said.
"Yes."
"And the boons?"
"I've kept them."
"Yes," said Vasishtha. "You have. The stone will remember that. It remembers very few things, and it forgets most of what it remembers. But it will remember this." He lifted his staff and touched the top step with its white tip, very gently, as the sage had touched the marker in the dead forest. "Your father is dying, prince. He will not see the year out. When he goes, the stone will want a new voice to speak for the house, and the towers will want a new hand to speak for the stone. You will hear both of them calling your name. Remember that you were asked, and that you said no."
He turned and began to climb the steps, slowly, toward the palace door.
"Wait," said Ram.
The high priest paused, half-turned.
"Did you ever think I should have refused the boons?"
Vasishtha looked at him for a long time. Then he smiled, very faintly, and the lines around his eyes deepened into something almost tender.
"I thought," he said, "that it was not my place to think it. And I thought that you already knew."
He went on up the steps and disappeared into the dark mouth of the palace door.
---
Bharat was waiting at the gate.
He stood alone on the causeway, in the dust-coloured travelling clothes he had worn the day before, his hair uncombed and his face drawn with the sleeplessness of a man who had spent the night arguing with his own mother. He had a bundle under one arm. When he saw Ram come through the outer barrier, he stepped forward, and his face was white, and he did not smile.
"I'm not going to stop you," he said.
"I know."
"I'm not going to beg you to stay, either. You'd only say no."
"Yes."
Bharat's mouth twisted. He looked down at the bundle in his arms, and then, with a sudden jerky movement, he held it out.
It was a pair of sandals. They were made of pale ash-wood, the soles carved smooth and thin, the thongs woven from strips of dark leather, worn at the edges and mended once at the heel with a tight neat stitch. They were old. They were the kind of sandals a man wears for walking long distances, over stone and dust and river-mud, and they had been used until the wood had gone soft and grey along the grain.
"They were Father's," said Bharat. "He wore them on the pilgrimage to the Sarayu source, when he was young. He told me once that he walked the whole way barefoot and only put them on at the end, so the first thing he'd feel at the holy place would be the ground itself. I think he meant it as a joke. I'm not sure."
Ram did not take them.
"Keep them," he said.
"No." Bharat's voice cracked. "Take them. I can't carry them around the palace any more. Every time I see them I think about the way you knelt in front of the stone, and I think about how I should have done it instead, and I think about how I didn't have the nerve." He pushed the sandals into Ram's hands, roughly, as though he could not bear to hold them any longer. "Take them. Walk in them. Let the forest feel what it's like to be walked on by a man who was asked to give up everything and didn't."
Ram closed his fingers around the worn wood. It was light, and dry, and smelled faintly of old oil and dust.
"I'll bring them back," he said.
"You'd better." Bharat's eyes were bright with tears he would not let fall. "I'll keep the house warm for you. I don't know how. I don't know whether I can. But I'll try, and I'll sit in the Chamber every morning, and I'll tell the ministers they can't have my brother's name for their feeds, and I'll go on telling them until one of us is dead."
He turned abruptly and walked back up the causeway toward the gate, his shoulders stiff, his head bowed, and he did not look back.
---
Lakshman and Sita were waiting at the edge of the causeway, where the road ran down toward the south.
Lakshman had his bag slung over one shoulder and the black bow across his back. He had not spoken since dawn. His face was tight and pale, and his hand kept drifting up to touch the bow, the way it had on the road from Siddhashrama, and each time it did, he dropped it again.
Sita stood a little apart from him. She wore the plain white cotton she had worn in the Chamber, and her hair was tied back severely, and she carried nothing at all. Her hands were loose at her sides. She was looking past Ram, up at the city, at the spires catching the first pale light of the morning, and her face was calm and far away.
"Do you want to look back?" she asked, without turning her head.
"No."
"Good. Neither do I." She glanced at him, very briefly. "But somebody will."
They started down the road.
It was not a road for carts. It ran south from the outer barrier through the market-gardens on the city's edge, where the cabbages were still wet with dew and the women in the fields looked up from their hoes and watched the three of them pass without a word. Past the gardens the land turned to dust. The asphalt gave way to packed red earth, and the earth was cracked and hard and dotted with the bones of old wells. In the distance, far ahead, a line of dark green trees marked the edge of the forest, and beyond them the sky was heavy and yellow with the first banks of cloud building on the horizon.
Ram had not looked back. He did not need to. He could hear the city behind him, the hum of the freight rails, the far-off clatter of the market carts, the thin high chatter of the crowds that had begun to gather along the causeway, drawn by the sight of the three figures walking out through the gate.
Then he heard the engines.
They were not testing. They were turning. The long low hum that had rolled across the citadel every morning of his life rose suddenly into a deeper note, a grinding shudder of hydraulics and iron, and Ram felt it through the soles of his feet, in the packed red earth, in the bones of his legs.
He stopped. He did not turn around.
Lakshman stopped beside him. Sita stopped beside Lakshman.
"Look," Lakshman said quietly.
Ram looked.
He saw the north rampart first. The line of hundred-killers stood along the top of the wall, their long black barrels catching the morning light, and they were moving. Slowly, deliberately, with the heavy patience of machines that have been built to wait and have finally been given something to wait for. They were turning. Each barrel swung round on its mount, one after another, in a long unhurried arc, until they had all come to rest facing the same direction.
South.
Toward the red earth and the dust and the three figures on the road.
Ram watched the first barrel settle. He watched the second, and the third. He counted them without meaning to, the way he counted the breaths on the road, and when he reached twenty he stopped counting, because there were too many, and because the sight of them turning toward him had begun to make his chest tighten in a way the count could not quite reach.
Above the rampart, along the faces of the spires, the great glass panels began to light.
They lit slowly, one after another, row by row, from the base of the towers to the crown, until every spire in the citadel was blazing gold in the morning sun, and in the centre of each panel, huge and luminous, a single face appeared.
It was his face. It was the same grainy shot from the feeds, the boy on the copper roof with the broken bow across his knees and his hands spread wide and his mouth open, as though he were about to say something that the whole city had been waiting to hear. The text beneath it burned in pale modern script, three words that rang out across the causeway in a silence so deep that Ram could hear the faint sigh of the wind through the market-garden cabbages:
THE RETURNING PRINCE.
The crowd on the causeway made a sound. It was not a cheer. It was a low, rising murmur, uncertain and hungry, the sound of people who have been told a story so many times that they have begun to believe they remember it.
Sita let out a short, hard breath. "They're going to use you," she said.
"I know."
"Do you?"
"Yes."
"Then what are you going to do about it?"
Ram did not answer. He stood on the red earth with the sandals in his hand and the sun on his neck, and he looked at the face on the spire, and the face looked back at him, enormous and gold and utterly without doubt, the face of a man who had already decided what everyone else needed, and had never once been asked whether he was the right one to decide.
It was not his face. It had never been his face.
And yet it was. It would always be. That was the thing the glass had taught him, on the roof of the spire in the dark. The face was a question someone else would keep asking, in a voice he would have to learn to hear without answering. And somewhere out there, in the city behind him or in the forest ahead, another man was already standing at a panel of black glass, with his hand flat against the cold surface, waiting for the pulse to quicken.
He turned around.
He did it slowly, without taking his eyes off the spire, and when he was facing the city, he lifted his right hand. The faint reddish mark across the palm had faded by morning, as he had known it would. But he could still feel it, very faintly, the way you feel a bruise after the swelling has gone down.
He did not wave. He did not bow. He only held his hand up, open and empty, in the gold light, and let the engines see it.
Then he turned his back on the city, and walked south.
Lakshman fell into step beside him. Sita followed, a pace behind, her bare feet soundless on the red earth. None of them spoke. Behind them the engines went on turning, their barrels tracking the three figures on the road with a patient, grinding steadiness, and the face on the spires went on smiling its eager, unknowing smile over the crowd on the causeway, and the crowd began, very slowly, to move after them, the way water moves when something heavy has been dropped into it and the ripples have not yet decided where to go.
Ram did not look back again.
He counted his steps instead, and found that the count came easily, and that it did not feel like a trick. It felt like a road he had walked as a child, and had forgotten, and was walking again now, into the dark green edge of the forest, where the trees were thick and the light came down in long slanting bars through the leaves, and the air smelled of rain.
Far to the west, beyond the dust and the fields and the forest's first shadow, the sky had darkened to the colour of slate. A line of cloud stood up along the horizon, heavy and grey and low, and under it the first faint rumble of thunder rolled across the empty country, long and slow, like the far-off sound of an engine that had been turned on and had not yet been told what to do.
The monsoon was coming.
He could feel it on his skin. He could feel it in the wind, cool and damp and smelling of wet earth and river mud and something green and growing, something that had been waiting in the dry red ground all season for the first rain to come and wake it.
He walked on into the trees.
Behind him, on the north rampart, the engines went on turning, and the glass of the spires went on burning gold, and the face of the Returning Prince went on looking down over the city with its open, eager, unanswered question.
Four, two, six.
The count held.
For now, it held.
