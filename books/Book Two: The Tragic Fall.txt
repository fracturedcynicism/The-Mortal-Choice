Book Two: The Tragic Fall
Sargas follow the road taken. Passages marked SHADOW ROAD follow the other path: the same nights, under a different hand on the glass.
---
CHAPTER : SARGA I: The Toll at Janasthan
> **[THE ROAD TAKEN]**
The river at Janasthan had been dead for eleven years, and the people who lived along its banks had learned to call it by its new name, which was the Ledger.
It ran through the scrub in a long bed of cracked grey silt, a channel the width of a road with a single thread of brown water at its centre, and that thread was owned. Ram saw the first sign of it at the bend, where a pole had been driven into the bank and a sheet of corrugated tin nailed to its top. Someone had painted on the tin in white letters, neat and patient: TOLL FOR CLEAN WATER. Beneath the words, in smaller script, a list of figures. Beneath the list, a second line, added later in a different hand: Pilgrims half.
"Clean," said Lakshman, reading it aloud. He said it the way a man says a word he has been told is a joke. "There's no clean water between here and the sea."
"There's the water they sell," said Sita.
She walked ahead of them along the bank with her plait tied up under a cloth against the dust, and she did not look at the tin sign as they passed it. She had not looked at much since the road south of the Sarayu. She walked with her eyes on the ground a few paces in front of her, and her steps were light and sure, and once or twice Ram had seen her stop and crouch and put her palm flat on the silt, very still, as though she were listening through it to something far below.
They had been walking for eleven days. The forest had thickened and thinned and thickened again. In the first week they had passed through the last of the old teak and sal, the stripped trunks giving way to a second growth of thorn and bamboo that had sprung up in the cleared ground and was already being cut back by the brokers' crews. In the second week the land had opened into the wide flat plain of Janasthan, where the old pilgrim temples had been turned into toll-houses, and the toll-houses into barracks, and the barracks into something with a name nobody used aloud.
The sky over Janasthan was never clear. It was the colour of a bruise. The factory stacks along the eastern horizon breathed out a grey haze that hung in the air at all hours, and the sun came up through it as a dull copper disc that gave no warmth and cast no shadows, so that the whole country seemed lit from nowhere. The air smelled of hot metal and something sweet and rotten underneath. At night the sky was a sickly orange over the stacks, and the stars did not show.
Ram had stopped trying to count the days of it. He counted breaths instead. It was easier. The count did not need the sky.
---
The toll-house stood at the old ford, where the Ledger's thread of water ran shallow over a bed of smooth grey stones. It had been a temple once. Its outer wall still had the carved lintel of a god's gateway, the figures worn to soft ridges by centuries of rain, and above the lintel someone had hung a long banner of red cloth printed with the same white letters as the tin sign. Under the banner a row of wooden tables had been set up in the shade of a neem tree, and men in grey coveralls sat behind the tables with ledger-books open in front of them, and a line of carts and pilgrims and bare-footed children stood waiting in the dust to pay.
The men looked up as the three of them came down the bank. One of them stood, slowly, and wiped his hands on his trousers, and his face showed no surprise at all.
"Travellers," he said. "Ten chits each. Clean water rate. Pilgrims half."
"We're not pilgrims," said Lakshman.
"Then you're ten chits." The man lifted the ledger and ran a thumb down its page. He did not look up again. "Name?"
Ram stepped forward. "We don't have chits."
"No one has chits." The man sighed and looked up at last. His eyes went over Ram's face, and then Lakshman's, and then, very briefly, to Sita, and stayed there a moment longer than they had on the others. Then he closed the ledger with a soft dry snap. "Then you'll have to see the Mistress," he said. "She likes to see the ones who don't have chits. It makes her feel useful."
"Who is the Mistress?" Sita asked.
The man smiled for the first time. It was not a kind smile. "Nakha," he said, and something in the way he said the name made the bare skin on Ram's arms prickle. "Sister of the Khar brothers. Sister of the Lord of the southern island, if you believe what she tells the pilgrims. She owns the water. She owns the ford. She owns the stones under the water, and the men who dig the stones, and the fellow who sweeps the tables. Come and see her. She'll be pleased."
---
The Mistress of the ford was sitting on the steps of the old temple, under a canopy of faded blue silk that had been strung between two broken pillars. She was not tall. She was slender and small-boned, dressed in a loose green silk tunic over white trousers, and her hair was pinned up in a dark coil with silver combs. Her hands were resting in her lap. Her nails were long, and painted a deep glossy red, and they curved slightly at the tips, like the claws of a bird.
Beside her, on a low wooden stool, sat a second man, very thin, with a face so tired that Ram thought at first he was asleep. He had a slate on his knees and a stylus in his hand, and he was writing.
Nakha looked up as they approached. Her eyes were dark and quick and very clever, and when they found Ram, they lit up slowly, like a lamp being turned up in a room where someone had just come in.
"Oh," she said softly. "Look at that. The Returning Prince. I knew it would be you. Nobody else in the three worlds walks through my toll with a face like a closed door and a woman on his arm and a bow on his brother's back, pretending he doesn't know what he's worth."
"I'm not worth anything," said Ram.
"Everyone is worth something, prince. Some of us just keep better accounts." She rose from the steps in one smooth movement, and came down the stone stair toward him, and stopped an arm's length away. She smelled of musk and crushed leaves and something sharp, like the rind of a lime. "The city's been singing about you. Did you know? They put your face on every tower from here to the coast. They say you're the one who took the towers back from the Board and hung every clerk who ever signed a stripping-permit. They say the old King wept for joy and crowned you on the copper roof at Mithila. Is it true?"
"No," said Ram.
"No?" She laughed, a low warm sound. "What a relief. I'd hate to think I'd be standing here talking to a man who'd hanged people."
Ram said nothing.
Nakha's eyes moved to Sita. They did not linger. They passed over her the way a hand passes over a table to brush away a crumb.
"And the lady," she said. "The lady who was found in the furrow. Well. Everyone's heard about the furrow. I'm told you can make a bow sing just by looking at it. Is that so?"
"Ask the bow," said Sita, very quietly.
Nakha's head turned slowly. For the first time she looked at Sita fully, and her face changed. The warmth went out of it, not all at once, but the way a stream goes out of a rock in summer, leaving a dry channel.
"Ask the bow," she repeated. "How charming."
"It's broken," said Sita. "You'd have to ask the one who broke it."
"And he's standing right here." Nakha smiled again, thinly. "Isn't he?"
The clerk on the stool had stopped writing. He was watching the three of them with a slight, bored attention.
Ram said, "We came to cross the ford. We'll pay in the way that's fair."
"There's no fair in the ford, prince. There's only the rate." Nakha's gaze went to his hands, then to his face. "But you can pay me in other things. I'm not greedy. A conversation would do. A walk along the bank at dusk. A few minutes alone with the man who broke the bow, so I can tell him what the city says about him when they think no one's listening." Her voice dropped. "I've got a great deal of time for that kind of thing, and very little for cheap pilgrims."
She stepped closer. The smell of musk and lime rose around Ram like a fog.
"No," said Ram.
Nakha stopped. She tilted her head and looked up at him, and for a moment she was perfectly still, the way a cat is still when it has decided the mouse is not yet worth the trouble of a pounce.
"No?" she said.
"No," said Ram. "We're going to cross the ford. We'll pay the rate. Then we'll walk on."
For a long breath nothing happened. Then Nakha's mouth curved, slowly, and it was not a smile at all.
"Oh, I like you," she said. "I really do. I'm going to enjoy the day you come back to my ford and beg me for a chit."
She turned away from him, toward Sita.
She did not move quickly. She did not need to. She simply stepped to one side, so that the path between herself and the woman in the grey-white cotton was clear, and in that clear space something flashed. It was a short curved blade, very thin, the length of a finger, and it had come out of the sleeve of Nakha's green silk as smoothly as breath. She did not raise it. She only held it, low at her hip, and her long red nails rested lightly against its handle, and her eyes, for the first time, were entirely without humour.
"And you," she said softly. "Furrow-girl. You want to tell me what I have to ask the bow?"
Sita did not step back. She stood very straight and looked into the other woman's face, with her hands loose at her sides, and said nothing at all.
Nakha lifted the blade.
Lakshman moved before Ram could speak.
He had the black bow off his shoulder and an arrow nocked and drawn, and the string was singing, and then the arrow was gone, not at Nakha but past her, into the carved stone of the temple gate, where it struck with a sound like a fist on a drum and stood quivering beside the old god's worn face. Nakha started back. The blade came up. For half a heartbeat she and Lakshman faced each other across the dusty steps, two people with weapons in their hands and the whole world holding its breath.
Then Lakshman stepped in.
It was not a warrior's movement. It was quick and raw and furious, a man who had been holding a bowstring too tight for too many weeks and had finally been given something to let go of. His free hand caught Nakha's wrist and twisted. The blade skittered across the stone. Nakha cried out, a high sharp sound, and tried to wrench free, and Lakshman's other hand came up with the thin bone-handled knife from Ram's own grandfather, which he had been carrying in his belt since the palace and which Ram had never once seen him draw.
The knife moved in a flat bright arc.
When it finished, Nakha had stopped screaming. She stood very still with her wrist still caught in Lakshman's grip, and her face was white, and her eyes were wide and terrible, and there was blood running down over her chin and into the collar of her green silk, in a fine bright stream that seemed far too bright in the grey light. Her nose was gone, sliced away clean at the bridge. Blood welled from the place where it had been. Above it, one of her small silver earrings lay on the step, and beside the earring, where her ear had been, a dark ragged stump bled steadily into the silk.
She opened her mouth. No sound came out. Then the sound came, high and thin and horrible, and she tore herself free of Lakshman's hand and stumbled backward across the steps, clutching her face.
Lakshman stood with the knife in his hand. He was shaking. Ram could see the tremor run down his arm, through the knife, into the blade.
"You didn't have to," said Ram.
"She went for Sita."
"I know."
"She had a blade."
"I know."
"Then what was I supposed to do?" Lakshman's voice cracked. "Let her take it? Let her cut the furrow-girl the way they cut the trees? Stand there and count how long it took?"
Ram did not answer. He was looking at the blood on the stone.
The clerk on the stool had risen. He was staring at Nakha, and his face had gone the colour of old paper, and the slate had slipped from his lap and shattered on the steps. He did not move to help her. He only stood there, watching the Mistress of the ford stagger against the carved pillar and slide down it until she was sitting in her own blood, weeping in great hoarse gulps that made her shoulders heave.
Ram went to her.
He did not decide to. His feet took him up the steps and across the stone, past the scattered silk and the broken slate and the little silver earring, until he knelt in front of her. She tried to pull away. He did not reach for her. He only held out the water-skin from his shoulder, unstoppered, and waited.
"No," Nakha whispered, through her fingers. "No. Don't. Don't you dare."
"It's clean," said Ram. "It came from the Sarayu, before the factories. I carried it from the north. It's the last clean water in the country."
She looked at him through her fingers. Her eyes were wild.
"Drink it," said Ram quietly. "Or don't. But you can't stop the bleeding with your hands."
For a long moment she did not move. Then, with a shaking hand, she took the skin from his fingers and lifted it to her lips, and tipped her head back, and drank. The water ran down her chin and mingled with the blood and dripped onto the silk of her tunic, and when she was done she let the skin fall into her lap and sat there, breathing hard, staring at him over her ruined face.
"You'll pay for this," she said hoarsely. Each word came out wet and misshapen. "My brothers will count you. Every man at this ford will count you. Every ledger in Janasthan has your name in it now. You won't live to see the monsoon."
"Maybe not," said Ram. He stood up. "But you'll be alive to see it, if you let the clerk wrap your face."
Nakha looked past him, toward the steps, and her eyes narrowed into two slits of pure, shining hatred.
"Clerk," she said, very softly. "Find my brothers. Tell them I've been dishonoured at the ford by a man with a closed face and a bow-breaker's brother who wields a knife like a toll-man's bookkeeper. Tell them to bring every man they've got."
The clerk did not answer. He was already running.
---
They did not cross the ford that day. Ram would have waited on the far bank for dusk and then taken the water at night, but Sita, who had been standing silently on the steps all this time, with her hands clasped in front of her and her face utterly calm, said quietly, "They'll come before dusk," and walked down the bank toward the ruined temple wall on the far side without looking back.
They built no fire. They took shelter behind the broken gateway of the old temple, where the stone arch had partly collapsed and made a low dark hollow among the rubble. Lakshman sat with his back against the wall and his knife across his knees, and he did not speak. He had wiped the blade clean on his trousers, and the cloth had come away red, and he had thrown it away into the scrub. Once Ram saw him take the little silver earring from his pocket, turn it over in his fingers, and put it back.
Sita sat near the mouth of the hollow with her back to the stone. She had taken the cloth from her hair and was retying her plait, her fingers quick and certain, and her eyes were on the eastern horizon where the stacks breathed their grey haze into the sky.
"You didn't stop him," she said to Ram, without looking round.
"No."
"You could have."
"Yes."
"Why didn't you?"
Ram thought about it. "Because he was right," he said slowly. "She had a blade. She was going to use it. He didn't know what else to do with his hands."
Sita's fingers paused on the braid.
"That's not what I meant," she said quietly.
"I know."
"Then say the other thing."
Ram was silent for a while. The stacks were a dull orange now, the haze turning rust-coloured as the sun went down behind them. Somewhere out on the plain a dog began to howl, and another answered it, and then the sound stopped as suddenly as it had begun.
"I wanted her to feel what it was like," Ram said at last. "For a moment. The way the wood felt when the axe came down. I wanted her to know that there was a price for it. And then I wanted to give her water."
Sita turned her head and looked at him. Her face in the dusk was unreadable.
"Both," she said.
"Both."
"That's the trouble with you, you know. You always want both at once. It makes you very hard to fight and very hard to trust." She finished the plait and tied it off, and her hands fell still in her lap. "Lakshman's the one who acted. You're the one who has to live with what he did."
"I know."
"Do you?"
He did not answer.
The brothers came at dawn.
---
The ledger had said fourteen thousand. Ram heard it from a clerk on the far bank, a boy with a stolen grey coverall three sizes too large who had crossed the shallows at first light and stood at the edge of the rubble, shouting it across the dawn in a voice that cracked on every syllable: Fourteen thousand men, Khar's own, every blade in the Janasthan wards, and they say the Mistress of the ford has been dishonoured and they'll wash it in the blood of the prince who did it.
Then the boy ran. Ram did not see him again.
The men came out of the haze in a long dark column, marching through the orange scrub with their rifles across their chests and their grey coveralls dusted white, and behind them came the carts. Ram could see the carts clearly by the time the sun was a red blister on the horizon. They were flatbed trucks, rusted and patched, with iron plates welded over their cabs and grinding machines bolted to their beds. The machines had been painted black. Their long steel arms swung slowly from side to side as the trucks rolled, like the heads of very patient animals searching for something to eat.
"Stripping rigs," said Lakshman, from behind the wall. His voice was flat. "They're going to take the temple down with them."
"No," said Sita, very quietly. "They're going to take the people in it."
Ram stood up. He did not take the bow from his shoulder. He stood in the gap of the gateway, in the first red light, with his hands at his sides, and watched the column come on.
He could see their faces now. They were young, most of them. Some were old. A few were boys who looked no older than the child at the hermitage in Siddhashrama, their eyes wide and bright and fixed on the ruined gate with a kind of frightened eagerness, the way boys look at the edge of a cliff when someone has told them there is gold at the bottom. They were not monsters. They had been told a story about a ford and a dishonour and a bow-breaker's brother who had cut the Mistress of the toll, and they had been paid to believe it, and they had come a long way with their rifles to make it true.
He thought of the clerks in the glass towers. He thought of the boy in the grey coverall shouting the number across the water. He thought of the count.
The first rifle fired.
The shot went high, into the broken lintel above Ram's head, and a shower of old stone dust fell on his hair. Lakshman was up and moving, the black bow in his hands, but Ram put out one arm and stopped him.
"Wait," said Ram.
"They're shooting at us!"
"They're shooting at the gate. Let them come closer."
"Ram—"
"Let them come closer," said Ram, and his voice was perfectly calm. "Let them see what it is they're walking into."
He lifted the bow from his shoulder.
It was not the black bow Lakshman had carried since the palace. It was his own, the old one from the Chamber of Oaths, made of plain yew and strung with sinew. He had kept it through the road and the hermitage and the salt flats, and it was the only thing he had carried from Ayodhya that he had never been asked to give up. He nocked an arrow. He did not hurry. He stood in the gateway with the sun coming up red behind him and the column of grey men advancing through the scrub, and he drew.
Four, two, six.
The count was fast, this morning. It was not steady. He could feel it wobbling, the way a boat wobbles when the water beneath it is moving too quickly. He held the draw. He breathed out, long and slow. He let the arrow go.
The first man in the column fell with a short cry, and the rifle fell from his hands and rattled on the stones.
Ram nocked again.
The column did not stop. It came on, faster now, with a ragged roar that rose from a hundred throats, and the rifles began to fire in earnest. The shots came in a steady rattling chorus, and dust and stone-chips exploded from the gateway around Ram, and somewhere behind him Sita's voice said sharply, "Down!" but he did not go down. He drew. He loosed. He drew again.
The men fell. They fell in twos and threes, and then in tens, as the bow sang and the arrows went home. Ram did not look at their faces. He looked at the next man in the line, and the next, and the count held, slow and steady and terrible in his chest, a single beat for each arrow, each shaft a breath, each breath a man who would not walk home to his mother.
He lost count somewhere in the third hundred.
Then the column broke.
It did not break all at once. It broke at the edges first, where the men at the back had seen the bodies piling up in the scrub and had decided, very quietly, that the ledger was not worth the rate. They turned and ran. The men in the middle hesitated, and then began to scatter. The trucks ground to a halt in a cloud of orange dust, and their black machines swung silently on their arms, and in the cabs men began to scream at one another.
Ram did not stop. He drew again, and his arrow took the driver of the nearest truck through the open window of the cab. The truck lurched and rolled sideways and came to rest against a stone pillar with a long grinding shriek of metal, and a thin trail of steam began to rise from its engine.
He lowered the bow.
The scrub was full of bodies. They lay in the dust in long untidy rows, some still moving, some still, grey coveralls turned dark red across the chest and the belly, rifles scattered beside them like fallen sticks. The sun had risen fully now. It caught the steam coming off the wrecked truck and turned it into a pale gold ribbon, and the ribbon drifted across the battlefield and dissolved into the haze.
"Stop," said Sita, quietly, behind him.
Ram had not realised that he had drawn again. The arrow was on the string. His hand was steady on it. The string was taut against his cheek, and there was a man in the scrub below, perhaps forty paces off, a young man with a flushed face and wide eyes, who had dropped his rifle and was crawling backward through the dust on his elbows, away from the gate, and was weeping.
Ram lowered the bow again. He let the arrow fall back into the quiver.
"Lakshman," he said. "Tell them to go."
Lakshman was already standing on the wall, his black bow raised, his voice ringing out across the dust in a clear hard shout that Ram had not heard from him since they were boys. "Go!" he cried. "Take your dead and go! The ford is closed! Go back to Khar and tell him the prince of the ford does not want your blood! Go!"
For a long moment nobody moved. Then, slowly, the scrub began to stir. The men who were still standing turned their heads toward the wall, toward the bow and the shouting, and then, one by one, they began to walk. Some carried the wounded. Some did not. They went back the way they had come, through the scattered bodies and the drifting gold steam, and the ones who could still run ran, and the ones who could not crawled, and the ones who had been shot in the legs dragged themselves along with their hands.
The sun climbed. The haze burned off in a slow orange smear. Ram stood in the gateway with the bow at his side and watched them go.
When the last of them had vanished into the stacks, he finally let his breath out.
"How many?" asked Sita.
He did not answer at once. He was looking at the bodies. He was trying to see them as numbers, the way the ledger would, and failing. He could only see faces.
"I don't know," he said.
"Roughly."
"I lost count."
"Roughly," said Sita, very gently, and came to stand beside him in the gateway, close enough that their shoulders touched. "Ram. The boy with the ledger said fourteen thousand. They're not fourteen thousand. Look at them. They're a few hundred, maybe. The rest were the ones who were promised a number and never came."
He turned his head and looked at her. Her face was grey with dust and very tired, and her eyes were wet.
"They still came," he said.
"Yes," said Sita. "They still came."
Sita found the radio in the cab of the wrecked truck, wedged under the seat beneath a coverall someone had rolled up for a pillow. It was still warm. Its channel light blinked amber, and the log-spool beside it had been running. When Lakshman thumbed the playback, a voice came out of the dented speaker, flat and tired and not in the least afraid.
"They call it stripping, Dushan. I call it collecting."
"You talk about the forest like a moneylender."
"Because that's what it is. Every tree in the Dandaka is a debt the land has run up against the people who fed on it. Somebody has to present the bill. The Board won't, because the Board is a ledger. A ledger can be audited. It can't be killed. Nobody has ever audited the wards. I'm the only one who ever stood in the scrub and counted what the land was owed."
"And the men who die counting?"
"Were paid to be counted. Our father sold the ford to the Board and died in a glass office with his feet on a desk. I sell the Board the ford, then I sell the Lord the Board, and when I have enough of their money in the caves I'll sell the caves back to the men who lived in them and let them keep the stripping rights for a year." A short laugh. "That isn't greed, brother. It's a philosophy with a margin."
"Nakha says the prince cut her face."
"Then the ledger has a new entry."
A long silence on the spool. Then, quieter: "Suka's man says the Board's clerks are coming down the salt road with their own tallies."
"Suka can count what he likes. He's a broker. He doesn't own anything. He only holds the paper until somebody burns it." Static. "Get the men into the cutting. Let the prince walk into the cliff. He's the kind of man who thinks a bow is an argument."
The spool ran out.
Sita set the radio down on the seat very gently, as though it might bite.
Behind them, on the broken wall, Lakshman had sat down heavily with his bow across his knees. He was not looking at the dead. He was looking at the wrecked truck, and at the thin steam rising from its cracked engine block, and his face had the flat, hollow stillness of a man who has stopped trying to decide what he feels because he has learned that the answer will not be useful.
"We'd better go," he said, without looking up. "Before the stacks wake up properly. There'll be more on the road. And the ledger will want its own count."

Shadow I: The Night the Glass Woke
> **[SHADOW ROAD]** *The night the glass woke, under the hand that accepted it.*
The panel did not wait for him to speak. It had been waiting for two hundred years, and it had no patience left for ceremony.
Ram stood in the cold round room beneath the north rampart with his right palm pressed flat against the black glass. He had not taken it off since the pulse had caught. The surface was colder than it had any right to be, colder than the river had been on the morning he had come down the causeway in the sun, and it seemed to draw the heat out of his hand and his wrist and the long bones of his forearm, and in exchange it gave him back his own heartbeat, slow and heavy, rising through the stone and the iron and the glass until it filled the whole room.
Four, two, six.
The count had come easily. It always came easily now. He had stopped noticing when it started.
AUTHORISE. CONFIRM HOUSE PULSE. DIRECT THE ENGINES.
The words hung in the glass in their clean modern script. Below them the blank line waited, with its single cursor blinking, like a question that was patient because it knew there was only one possible answer.
Behind him, Harit said nothing. He stood a few paces back in the dim yellow light of the lamp, one hand resting on the back of the dark wooden chair, and Ram could feel the weight of his attention on the back of his neck. Behind Harit, the two black-tunicked officers stood at the foot of the stair with their rifles grounded. And behind them all, in the dark beyond the lamp, the palace stood above them in its rings of glass and iron, and the city sprawled out in its grid of cold blue light, and somewhere far off in the centre of it the Board's towers were breathing too quickly, their lower floors pulsing with a restless brightness, like the lungs of men who have been running for a very long time and have just begun to understand that they will never be allowed to stop.
He thought instead of the Chamber of Oaths. He thought of his father's face crumpling when the boons were called, the small quick terrible folding of it, like a paper fan in a hot wind. He thought of Kaikeyi's hand curling into a fist against the black stone. He thought of Bharat's voice, shrill and clear: It was never yours to ask for. And he thought of the way the ministers along the pillars had watched the two sons argue, with that bright unwilling hunger, as though the whole dispute were a thing that could be bought and sold.
They had been waiting to see who would take it. That was all. They had been waiting to see who would have the nerve.
He had not been waiting. He had decided on the morning the sage walked across the causeway.
And the boy who had walked away from the stone had made his choice and was gone now, into the dust and the salt, and nobody would ever ask him to come back and answer for it. Nobody in the city would remember the boy's face at all, except as a shot on a feed. Whereas the man at the panel would have to live in this city every day for the rest of his life, and answer for everything that happened in it.
It seemed to Ram that someone had to be willing to do that.
He took a breath. He let it out slowly, and felt the pulse under his palm shift, very slightly, as the blood in his wrist changed its rhythm to match.
"Yes," he said.
The cursor blinked once more, and then the blank line filled with pale script, letter by letter, as though the glass were reluctant to accept the word and needed to see it written before it believed it.
HOUSE CONFIRMED. ENGINES TO TOWERS.
Far above them, through a hundred metres of stone and iron, the north rampart shuddered.
---
It was a long shudder. Ram heard it come down through the shaft in the old control room, a grinding roar of hydraulics and iron that rose and rose and then stopped, dead, in a silence so complete that he could hear the water dripping somewhere in the pipes behind the glass. He took his hand from the panel. The pulse faded. The ring of light went out.
Harit let out a breath. It was not a sigh. It was closer to the sound a man makes when he has been holding a stone in his chest for a long time and has finally set it down.
"Good," he said quietly. "Good. Now we go up."
They went up. The stair was long and steep and smelled of wet stone and machine oil, and the lamp swung on its hook as they climbed, throwing their shadows up the curved walls in long lurching shapes. At the top the roof door stood open. The night air came in cool and sharp, and Ram stepped out onto the rampart walk and looked along the wall, and saw the hundred-killers turning.
They moved slowly. They had been built to move slowly, to take their time, to swing their long black barrels through their arcs with the grave deliberate patience of machines that had been designed to be feared rather than hurried. One by one they came round on their mounts, their steel arms creaking, their barrels catching the orange light of the towers. Not out toward the moat. Not toward the dead fields beyond the walls, where the stripping rigs had been circling since midsummer.
Inward.
Toward the glass.
Ram walked to the parapet and looked down. Below the rampart the grid of the ashtapada lay under its cold blue light, and at its heart the towers rose in their clusters, and one by one, as he watched, their lower floors went dark.
It was not dramatic. It was precise. A single window went out on the fourth floor of the Audit tower. Then another, two floors up. Then a whole row, from one end of the Board's offices to the other, as though someone had drawn a long black line along the glass with a finger. In the tower opposite, a bank of lamps flickered and died. In the tower beyond that, a clerk's desk lamp still burned on the twentieth floor, a small warm square of yellow in a field of dark, and as Ram watched, a shadow moved across it, and then the lamp went out too.
"They're arresting them," said Harit, at his shoulder. He did not sound triumphant. He sounded tired. "The council first. Then the audit men. Then the ones who sign the stripping permits. My men have the stair keys. They'll be taken down to the basement of the Board's office and kept there until morning, and in the morning they'll be sent to the lower ward to answer for what they did in the forest. The whole city will see it. We've made sure of that."
Ram said nothing.
He was watching the lamps go out. He was counting them, without meaning to. It was a thing he had always done, from the roof garden of the east spire when he was a boy, when the bees landed on his hands and he had not let himself flinch. He had counted the lamps then too. There had been eleven hundred and eighty windows lit in the Board's towers that night. He could not remember why it had seemed important.
The last window on the twentieth floor went dark.
"Done," said Harit softly.
---
The King signed the decree in the morning, in the Chamber of Oaths, with his hands shaking so badly that the clerk had to hold the pen steady for him.
Ram stood beside the high chair and watched. He did not touch his father. He had not touched him since the night of the boons, and he did not think his father would want him to. The old man sat hunched in the high seat with his grey tunic hanging loose from his shoulders and his face sunk so deep into its own folds that it seemed to have turned in on itself, like a fruit left too long in the sun. He did not look at Ram. He did not look at the basalt slab, or the dust-filled carvings, or the tall glass windows that the Board's men had hung with white banners in the night.
He looked only at the paper.
By the authority of the house of Ikshvaku, the Chamber of Oaths affirms that the boons of the second queen were given under duress of the realm's previous arrangement and are hereby superseded. The heir of the house, Ram son of Dasharatha, holds the towers and the engines of the city in trust for Kosala, until such time as the realm is restored to lawful order. The stripping of the forest is suspended. The brokers' accounts are frozen. The Board is dissolved.
The pen scratched. The King's signature sprawled across the bottom of the page in a shaky, uneven line.
Dasharatha, who held the stone and could not keep it.
When the clerk lifted the paper away, the old man let out a long slow breath, and his head sank forward onto his chest, and he did not lift it again.
Vasishtha stood at the foot of the slab with his white beard combed flat and his eyes half-closed, and his lips were moving. He did not come forward. When Ram looked at him across the length of the hall, the high priest raised his head and met his gaze, and for a long moment neither of them said anything at all.
Then Vasishtha turned and walked out of the Chamber of Oaths through the side door, and the door closed quietly behind him.
Sumant was waiting in the corridor. He had been waiting, Ram thought, for most of the night. His face was grey and his hands were shaking, and when he saw Ram come out of the chamber he took one step forward and stopped, as though he had walked into a glass wall.
"You can't," said Sumant. His voice was hoarse. "Prince. Ram. You can't hold this city in your hand like a stone. Not without the council. Not without the stone. The house was never meant to rule by itself."
"It was meant to rule," said Ram.
"It was meant to keep."
"Then I'll keep it."
"With what? With the engines? With the men in the towers? With a handful of officers who'll hang anyone who doesn't sign?" Sumant's voice rose, cracking. "They'll call you a tyrant before the month is out. You know they will. They already have. Half the wards are crying for you on the feeds and the other half are sending their children to the lower gates to be counted for the stripping crews, and you're going to hang the clerks in the square and tell them it's justice?"
"Yes," said Ram.
Sumant stared at him.
"It is justice," said Ram quietly. "The clerks signed the permits. The brokers paid for the trees. Somebody has to answer for the forest, and the forest can't come up the stairs and ask. So I'll ask."
"And if they say no?"
"Then I'll ask again."
Sumant took a step back. His mouth worked. For a moment his eyes were wet, and something in his face seemed to break, a fine thin crack running through the old man's composure like the crack through an ice sheet on a winter river.
"You sound like him," he whispered.
"Like who?"
"Like the sage. The one who came to the gate." Sumant swallowed. "He said that once, to the King, when they were young. Somebody has to answer for it, and the forest can't come up the stairs. I thought it was the most frightening thing I had ever heard."
Ram did not answer. He looked past Sumant, down the long glass corridor, to where the morning light spilled through the high windows and lay across the floor in bright pale bars. The light was clean and cold and very beautiful, and it fell on nothing but stone and glass and the white banners of the new order, and it did not, for once, make him think of anything but how steady his hands were.
---
They hanged the first clerk in the lower ward at noon, on a gallows of steel pipe that the stripping crews had built for their own use, and they hanged him facing the square so the people could see.
Ram did not go down to watch. He stood on the roof of the east spire and looked at the feeds. The Board's old screens had been taken down in the night, and in their place the new panels showed a single steady image: a wide square of grey stone, packed with people, their faces turned up toward the gallows. The camera held on the clerk's face for a long time. He was an old man, grey-haired, in a clean white shirt with the ink still on his fingers. His mouth was moving. The feeds did not carry the sound, but Ram could read the shape of the words, because he had heard them said before in the Chamber of Oaths by a man in a grey robe who had been very sure of his position.
The forest is ours.
The crowd did not cheer. The crowd was very quiet. It stood in the grey stone square with its hands at its sides and its eyes on the old man at the end of the rope, and the silence was so complete that the feed picked up the faint creak of the gallows-pipe as the body swung.
Then, slowly, from the back of the square, someone began to clap.
It was a single pair of hands at first. Then a second pair, and a third, and then a rolling wave of them spreading out across the packed stone like the ripples of a stone dropped into still water, until the whole square was thundering with the sound. The faces turned. The feeds caught them: men and women with their mouths open and their eyes bright, shouting a name that the camera could not quite make out, a name that was repeated over and over in a rising hoarse chant that climbed the towers and echoed back off the glass.
Ram leaned forward and watched the faces. He could not hear what they were saying. He did not need to.
It was his face on the banners below. It was his face on the spires. It was his name.
He thought, They wanted this.
He thought, They are happy.
He thought, I have given them a city where the forest can finally breathe, and the clerks are hanging, and the stripping has stopped, and every child in the wards will grow up knowing that someone was willing to answer for it.
He thought, Somebody had to.
He did not think about the bow. He did not think about the boy in the glass room at the top of the stair, telling him that he had to be the one who decided what it meant. He did not think about the sandals in the Chamber, or the boys in the lower ward who had been counted for the stripping crews, or the child at the hermitage in Siddhashrama with her berries and her toy horse with one wheel missing.
He thought about the count.
Four, two, six.
It was steady. It was perfectly steady. He could feel it in the bones of his wrist, in the long slow beat of his pulse, in the faint warm hum that rose through the glass of the spire and into the soles of his bare feet.
Down in the square, the chanting had reached its height. The name rolled off the towers in a great slow wave, and the wave rolled out over the city and down the highways and along the rails, and Ram stood on the roof in the noon sun and let it wash over him, and did not once look away.
He did not know it yet, but in the forest to the south, his brother would be lying in the mud of a shattered ravine with a bullet-torn chest, and Ram would have given the order that put him there.
Not yet. Not for a long time yet.
But it was coming, the way the monsoon comes to a dry country, with a low grey wall of cloud and a smell of wet earth and a long slow rumble of thunder across the dead fields.
He could feel it on his skin.
---
CHAPTER : SARGA II: The Golden Deer
> **[THE ROAD TAKEN]**
They walked north-west from the ford for two days, away from the stacks and the haze, until the air cleared enough to see stars. Ram had not expected the relief of it. He had grown so used to the orange sky over Janasthan that the first night under a black dome of stars made him stand still in the scrub and stare upward like a man who has been told he is looking at a painting.
Lakshman did not stare. He walked with his left arm held close against his ribs, where the Khar brothers' rifle had grazed him on the second morning, a shallow furrow across the meat of the forearm that he had bound with a strip of his shirt and refused to let anyone look at. Every hour or so Ram saw him flex the fingers and wince, and then go on walking without a word.
"It's nothing," Lakshman said, when Ram asked.
"It's bleeding through the cloth."
"Then it's a small wound with a big opinion."
Sita walked between them. She had not spoken much since the ford. She had taken the Khar brothers' rifle-fire the way she took everything, with a stillness that did not look like calm, and when the wounded men had crawled past them into the scrub, she had stopped and watched them go with her face unreadable. Once she had knelt beside one of them, a young man with a bullet in his thigh, and tied a tourniquet of torn cloth around the leg with quick practised hands, and then stood up and walked on without saying anything to him. The boy had stared after her as though she were a ghost.
Ram had thought about the boy for most of a day. He still thought about him sometimes, on the edge of sleep, when the count slipped and something else came in under it.
---
The siding was an old rail spur, long disused, with a row of rusted wagons standing on their broken axles in the scrub and a water-tower that had lost its roof. They made camp in the lee of the tower. The brick walls still held some of the day's heat, and the stars wheeled slowly overhead, and for the first time since the ford they built a small fire of dry thorn-wood and let it burn.
They ate in silence. Sita had found a patch of wild greens growing in the cracks of the platform and boiled them with a handful of rice from Ram's bag, and the mess that came out of the pot was bitter and grainy and tasted, Ram thought, faintly of iron. Lakshman ate two bowls of it without comment. Then he sat back against the brick and closed his eyes, and his good hand drifted to the black bow lying across his thighs, and stayed there.
The deer came at dusk on the second night, while the fire was still bright.
Ram saw it first. He was sitting with his back to the water-tower, watching the scrub beyond the siding where the thorn-bushes melted into the shadows, and something in the dark out there caught the last of the red light and flashed. It was a small flash. Gold. A bright, quick, deliberate glint, like the sun on a coin turned over by a child's fingers.
Then it moved.
It came out of the scrub at a walk, very slowly, and stopped at the edge of the firelight. It was a deer. At least, it had been built to look like one: a slender body on four long legs, delicate and graceful, its neck arched and its head turned toward the fire with a pretty, alert curiosity. Its hide was gold, a smooth bright metal that threw back the firelight in soft shimmering waves. Along its flanks ran a scatter of silver spots, each one the size of a palm, glittering faintly as the animal breathed. Its legs did not quite touch the ground. They hovered a finger's width above the dust, and Ram could see the fine dust stirring under their hooves in a slow circle, as though the deer were standing on a cushion of air.
It was beautiful. It was the most beautiful thing Ram had seen since the copper roof at Mithila.
"What is that?" Sita said softly, beside him.
He did not answer. He was watching the deer's head. Its eyes were dark and liquid and very intelligent, and they moved from the fire to the three of them, one by one, with an odd deliberate care.
Then it spoke.
"Lakshman."
The voice was clear and warm, and it was Ram's.
He knew it before he had time to think. He knew the timbre of it, the faint roughness at the bottom of the register, the way the vowels turned a little toward the north. He had heard it on the feeds, in the city, in the morning when he had gone up to the spire roof and looked at the grainy image of the boy with the broken bow, and heard the chanting, and realised that the voice in the clip had been his own.
"Lakshman," the deer said again, and its voice broke, very slightly, the way a voice breaks when a man is afraid and trying not to show it. "Sita. Help me. Help me, please. They've got me in the scrub. I can't get up."
Lakshman was on his feet. The bow was in his good hand, an arrow already nocked. His face was white.
"Don't," said Sita, very quietly.
Lakshman did not lower the bow. "That's him," he said hoarsely. "That's Ram. It's his voice."
"It's a voice," said Sita.
"It's his voice—"
"Yes," said Ram.
He had not moved. He sat with his back against the brick, his hands loose in his lap, and he looked at the golden deer with a stillness so complete that he could feel his own pulse beating in the hollows of his throat.
"It's the feeds," he said. "They took it off the spires. They put it in the machine."
"It's crying for help," said Lakshman. "You can't just sit there."
"It's not crying for help," said Ram. "It's crying in my voice. The difference matters."
The deer tilted its head. Its silver spots flickered. "Ram," it said, in the same warm breaking tone. "Ram, come. Please. You have to come. They're taking her to the south."
The silence after it was enormous.
Ram looked at the deer. It looked back at him with its liquid dark eyes, and in them he saw nothing at all, not malice, not pity, not interest. Only that patient, careful attention, the attention of something that has been told exactly what to say and has been told that the person listening will do exactly what he is expected to do.
"I never called out like that," Ram said quietly.
"What?"
"In my life. I never once called out like that, Lakshman. Not in the palace, not on the road, not when the arrows went into the Salt Widow. Not even when they took the bow away from the glass." He rose slowly to his feet. "I don't cry out. Not like a child in the dark. I think it would have been a very strange thing to hear from me, if anyone had been listening."
The deer's ears twitched.
"Please," it said. "I'm hurt. Come to me."
Sita stood up. She did it without any hurry, the way she did everything. She came to stand beside Ram, very close, and she looked at the golden animal in the firelight with an expression of cool, unhurried contempt.
"Go," she said.
Ram turned his head. "What?"
"Go and find him." Her voice was calm and clear. "Whoever is in the scrub. Go and see what he needs. Because if it's him, he's hurt and he's alone, and you've never once in your life left a hurt man in the dark. And if it isn't him—" She stopped. Her mouth tightened. "Then you'll find out who it is. And you'll bring back his name."
"I can't leave you."
"You can. Lakshman will stay with me." She looked at Lakshman. The look between them was brief and hard. "Won't you?"
Lakshman's mouth opened. He looked at the deer. He looked at his brother. He looked at the bow in his hand, and the arrow nocked and quivering on the string, and Ram saw the moment the decision came into his face and settled there, heavy and dark and not yet understood.
"I'll stay," he said.
"Good," said Sita. "Then go, Ram. Before it gets bored."
Ram walked forward into the scrub.
He did not run. He walked at a steady pace, his hands at his sides, his breath slow. The golden deer turned and moved away from the fire at the same pace, keeping perhaps forty paces ahead of him, its gold flanks glowing softly in the dark and its silver spots flickering like stars. Every few steps it looked back over its shoulder and spoke again, in his own voice.
"Ram. It's cold. Ram, I can't feel my legs."
He did not answer it. He counted his steps. Four, two, six. The count held. He followed the light.
The scrub thickened around them. The thorn-bushes grew taller and closer together, and the ground rose and fell in long low ridges where the old railway embankments had been left to crumble. Ram lost sight of the siding behind a wall of bamboo. He lost the firelight. He kept his eyes on the golden flanks ahead, and the deer kept moving, leading him up one ridge and down into a hollow and up again, always calling, always in his own voice.
Ram, it hurts. Ram, don't leave me.
Once he stopped. The deer stopped too, and turned, and looked back at him through the bamboo with its dark liquid eyes, and in the silence he heard, very faintly, from somewhere far behind him and to the east, a sound that was not the deer. It was a low thrumming, deep and steady, like the sound of an engine idling in a hangar with its doors shut.
He stood very still.
The thrumming grew.
The deer stepped forward again, delicately, and spoke: "Ram, why did you stop?"
Ram understood then. He understood the whole shape of it, all at once, the way the panel had understood him in the glass room in the north tower, and his stomach dropped away beneath him like a stone falling into a well.
He turned and ran.
---
The flyer was already rising when he came back over the last ridge.
It was black. It was long and low and sleek, with two stubby rotor-housings on either side of its body and a lattice of pale gold filigree running down its flanks, and it hung above the water-tower at the edge of the siding with its searchlight blazing down onto the platform like the eye of some enormous insect. The light turned the brick walls white. It turned the fire to a dull smear. It turned the wrecked rail-wagons into bright ghostly shapes, and it caught the figures on the platform and held them there, pinned and pale and very small.
Lakshman was fighting.
He stood at the edge of the platform with his back to the water-tower and the black bow in his hands, and his arrows were flying, one after another, up into the glare of the searchlight. Ram saw them strike the flyer's flank and skitter off the gold filigree in showers of sparks. A man in a grey coverall was hanging from the flyer's open side door, his face strained and furious, a long grey net in his hands. The net was tangled round Lakshman's legs. Lakshman was trying to kick free of it while still loosing arrows, and his face was a grimace of rage and effort, and blood was running freely down his injured arm, soaking the cloth and dripping off his fingers onto the platform.
Sita was gone.
Ram did not see her at first. He saw the place where she had been: the fire, the scattered pot, the bowl of bitter greens spilled across the stone. Then he saw the flyer's side door, and a figure in white cotton being hauled across the threshold by two men in grey, her arms pinned behind her, her hair torn loose from its plait and streaming down her back. She was not screaming. She was fighting, every muscle in her body straining against their grip, her heels scrabbling for purchase on the metal floor. But she was not making a sound.
"Sita!"
He was running. He did not remember starting. The bamboo tore at his arms and face, and the thorns caught in his shirt and dragged him back, and he tore free and kept running, and the searchlight swung round and caught him full in the face and blinded him.
He could not see. He could only hear.
The flyer's rotors roared. The whole platform shook with the sound, a great hot wind pushing down from above, flattening the grass and whipping the fire into a shower of sparks. Lakshman's cry came out of the glare, raw and furious and broken. Then another sound, sharper, a crack like a branch breaking under a weight.
And then, out of the sky, something came down.
It came at the flyer from above and to one side, a shape of pale grey and dull brown, broad-winged and ragged, its wings spread wide and its body hunched low. It was not a bird. It was a glider, a patched old thing made of stretched canvas and salvaged aluminium, with a man strapped into the frame beneath its wings. The man was old. His hair was white and streaming, and his face was a mask of wind and fury, and he was screaming something in a cracked high voice as he dived. The glider's wingtip caught the flyer's rotor-housing with a sound like a steel drum being struck with a hammer, and the flyer lurched sideways, and the searchlight swung wildly across the sky.
The glider did not survive it. Ram saw the broad grey wings buckle and fold. He saw the old man's body twist in the frame, saw the canvas tear loose and flutter away like a torn flag, saw the whole shattered thing drop out of the light and vanish into the dark scrub below the siding with a long hollow crash.
The flyer righted itself.
It rose.
The searchlight swung back across the platform, once, and found Ram standing in the open with his arms at his sides and blood running from a cut on his forehead into his eyes. It held him for a heartbeat. Then the rotors roared again and the flyer lifted, slowly and heavily, its gold filigree catching the firelight as it rose, and it turned in a wide arc over the wrecked rail-wagons and the water-tower and the dead scrub, and headed south-west, toward the line of the horizon where the faint haze of the sea showed like a bruise in the dark.
Ram could not see Sita any more. He could only see the flyer's running lights, red and green and blinking, dwindling into the stars.
He stood on the platform and watched until they were gone.
---
Lakshman was sitting on the edge of the platform with his legs hanging over the drop. He had got the net off. He had cut it away with the bone-handled knife, sawing through the grey mesh with short savage strokes, and now the strands lay in a tangled heap at his feet. His bow was across his knees. His injured arm was lying in his lap, the cloth round it black with blood, and he was staring at the place in the sky where the flyer had gone.
He did not look up when Ram came to stand beside him.
"She told me to stay," he said. His voice was flat and very quiet. "She told me to stay with her, and I stayed. And they took her anyway. Right out of the fire, while I was standing there with my bow up like a fool, shooting at paint."
"It wasn't your fault."
"Wasn't it?"
Ram did not answer.
"She said something," Lakshman said. He still had not looked up. "Just before they dragged her into the door. I was shouting at them and she turned round, with those men pulling her, and she looked at me, and she said—" His mouth worked. "She said, Tell him not to answer the voice."
Ram closed his eyes.
"Tell him not to answer," Lakshman repeated. "That's what she said. That's all she said. Like she knew. Like she'd known all along it was going to be you the thing called for."
Ram opened his eyes. The stars were very bright above the siding. The thorn-smoke from the fire drifted across them in faint grey threads.
"I'm going after her," he said.
"Yes."
"Will you come?"
Lakshman looked up at last. His face was grey and hollow and very young. "Of course I'll come," he said. "But not yet. First we bind this arm, and you wash that cut on your head, because you look like a man who's been dragged through a thorn hedge by a flyer. And then we find the old man who came down out of the sky."
"Why?"
"Because someone should," said Lakshman, "and because he might still be alive."
---
They found the glider in a shallow ditch half a mile from the siding, where the broken embankment had given way into a dry gully choked with thorn. It lay on its side in a heap of torn canvas and bent aluminium. One of the wings had folded back over the frame, and a long jagged crack ran down its spar like the crack in a dry riverbed. The air smelled of hot oil and blood.
The old man was lying in the gully a few paces from the wreck, face up, with his arms spread wide. He was still breathing.
Ram knelt beside him. The man's face was lined and brown and cracked across the cheekbones with sun and wind, and his white hair was matted with dust and blood. His eyes were open. They were pale, very pale, almost colourless in the starlight, and they were fixed on the sky above with an expression of such calm, unhurried patience that for a moment Ram thought he was already dead.
Then the eyes moved to Ram's face.
"Ah," the old man said. His voice was a thin wet rasp, the voice of someone who had been breathing blood for a long time. "The prince. The face on the spires."
"Yes."
"You don't look like the face."
"No," said Ram. "I don't."
The old man's mouth twisted. It might have been a smile. "Good," he said. "The face was always lying. It was made for the feeds." He coughed, and blood came up over his lip and ran down into the white stubble of his chin, and he did not wipe it away. "I was a pilot. For Vashkar's men, once. Flew the freight over the salt. I didn't like what I carried. So I stole the glider and flew it out over the stacks, and I told myself I'd fly until the wind took me, and somewhere over the Janasthan ford I heard a woman's voice on the radio, crying out in the dark. And I came down to see."
"Did she say anything?"
"She said your name. And then she said yours was the wrong voice to answer." The old man smiled again, more faintly. "I've been flying for fifty years, prince. I know a trap when I hear one. I just never thought I'd be the one to spring it."
Ram's hands were shaking. He pressed them flat against the gritty earth on either side of the old man's body and held them there.
"Where did they take her?" he said.
The old man's eyes went past him, to the sky, to the far south-west where the haze of the sea lay like a smear of ash along the horizon.
"South," he said. "Under the sea-line. Past the stacks, past the last of the salt, past the place where the land stops being land and starts being water. There's an island out there. You'll see its lights from the shore. The lights don't move. They never move. Vashkar keeps them burning every night, so the whole coast knows he's there." He breathed in, a long wet rattle. "He's got glass towers on the island, prince. Higher than the Board's. He keeps his women behind the glass. He keeps his men behind the wire. He keeps the shore behind a line of machines that are older than any of us, and they answer to his pulse, not to his word."
"Is she alive?"
"She was when they lifted her."
"Will she still be?"
The old man was quiet for a long moment. His breath whistled in his chest.
"Ask me that when you get there," he said at last. "I don't know the answer. Nobody does, until they're standing in front of the glass with the door in their hand." His pale eyes narrowed. "But I'll tell you one thing, because I'm dying and dying men are allowed to be useful. Don't go after the voice again. Whatever it sounds like. Whoever it sounds like. The machine that does the crying is not the machine that does the taking, and if you follow the first one, the second one will be waiting at the end of the road with the door already open."
"I won't."
"Good." The old man closed his eyes. "Then sit with me a while. I don't want to die staring at a sky full of cold stars with nobody to look at."
Ram sat down in the dirt beside him. He did not touch him. He only sat, close enough that the old man could feel the warmth of his body through the dust, and he began, very quietly, to count.
Four. Two. Six.
The old man's breathing slowed. It came in long pauses now, each one a little longer than the last, and each time it came back it was thinner and fainter, like the sound of a bell struck very far away.
Lakshman came down into the gully and stood at the edge of the thorns, his bound arm held against his chest, and did not come any closer.
The old man opened his eyes once more. He looked at the stars. His lips moved.
"What was your name?" Ram asked softly.
The old man did not answer for a long time. Then, very faintly, he said something Ram did not catch. It sounded like a bird's cry, short and rising, the sort of call a vulture makes when it has spotted something dead on the plain and wants the others to come and see. Then the breath went out of him in a long slow sigh, and did not come back.
Ram sat there for a while longer. The stars wheeled above the gully. The thorns rustled faintly in the night breeze. Somewhere out on the dry plain a jackal began to howl, once, and then stopped.
He did not reach for the count. He had reached for it every time for two months, on the road and the ford and the hill of the stripping rigs, and each time it had come to him, steady and quiet and sure, a thing he could hold like a rope while the world went on pulling at him from every side.
Now it would not come.
He sat in the gully with the old man's cold hand in his and felt the grief come up through him like water through a cracked dam, and there was nothing in his body that could hold it back. He did not weep at first. He only sat, and shook, and felt his own breath come hard and ragged and uncontrolled, the way it had not come since he was a child in the palace gardens, watching the bees land on his hands and not knowing yet that it was possible to be stung.
Behind him, very quietly, Lakshman came down into the gully and sat beside him in the dirt, and put his good arm around his brother's shoulders, and said nothing at all.
They stayed like that until the sky began to pale in the east, and the stacks along the horizon woke up one by one with their grey breath, and the first thin light of the morning came down over the dead country like a sheet of cold water poured onto a face.
---
Shadow II: The Vaults
> **[SHADOW ROAD]** *Sita in the vault, under the house that answers to him.*
The vaults were older than the palace. Ram had been told that as a child, by a servant who had brought him down the stone stairs once to show him the armoury racks, and who had said, with a nervous glance over her shoulder, that the rooms below the spires had been dug out by men who were already old when the first stones of the citadel were laid. The servant had not liked the vaults. She had said the air down there was wrong. It did not move. It stayed exactly where you left it, and when you breathed it in, you could taste the years.
He went down now with a single lamp in his hand and the two guards at the top of the stair, and the air did not move.
It was cool and dry and still. It smelled of old mortar and iron and something sharper underneath, a clean bitter smell like dust scraped off a bronze bell. The stairs were narrow. The walls were lined with blocks of pale stone, each one fitted so tightly to the next that not even a knife-blade could have slipped between them, and the lamp-flame made the stone seem to shift and breathe as he passed. At the foot of the stair the passage opened into a long low corridor, with iron doors set into its walls at intervals of ten paces. Each door had a small square grille at head height, and each grille was dark.
He did not stop at the first doors. He walked past them to the end of the corridor, where a single larger door stood in an arch of black stone, and there the lamp showed a steel panel set into the wall beside the frame. The panel was dark. Ram laid his palm on it, and it woke, very slowly, from the bottom up, a faint blue glow spreading across its surface like the first light on a winter lake.
CONFIRM HOUSE PULSE.
He waited. The pulse came. The panel read it, and a thin green line appeared along its base, and the door unlocked with a long slow hiss of air.
It opened inward. Beyond it was a room that had been cut out of living rock: round, low-ceilinged, with a floor of smooth grey flagstone and walls that had been polished to a dull mirror sheen. In the centre of the room stood a single stone bench. On the bench, a single lamp burned in an iron bracket. And on the bench, with her back very straight and her hands folded in her lap, sat Sita.
She did not look up when he came in.
She was wearing the same white cotton she had worn on the morning he had come back from the south with the Board's glass still shining in his eyes. It was soiled now, grey at the hem, and creased across the shoulders as though she had slept in it sitting up. Her hair was tied back in a plain knot. Her face was thinner. The bones of it stood out more sharply in the lamplight, and there were shadows beneath her eyes that had not been there before, and her mouth was set in a line of such perfect composure that for a moment Ram could not think of anything to say.
"You've come to see the furniture," she said.
"I've come to see you."
"Then you've seen me." She did not turn her head. "Shall I stand up and curtsy? It's been two weeks. I'm told the guards have been very attentive."
"Are you hungry?"
"I'm not hungry. I'm angry." She lifted her eyes at last, and they were dark and steady and absolutely clear. "I'm angry enough that I'm starting to enjoy it. It keeps me warm."
Ram looked at the lamp, and then at the stone walls, which gave back the lamplight in faint dull gleams, and then at her again.
"You know why you're here," he said.
"Yes. Your decree says I'm here to be kept safe, while the realm is restored to lawful order." She tilted her head very slightly. "It's a beautiful sentence. Your clerk wrote it. Did you read it through before he put the seal on it, or did you just listen to the sound?"
"I read it."
"Did you understand it?"
"I understood it."
"Then you'll understand what I'm going to say next," said Sita. "I won't sign the amnesty. I won't read the feed-statement on the morning broadcast. I won't stand beside you on the copper roof and tell the crowds below that the forest is ours. I won't say that the clerk in the lower ward deserved what he got. He didn't. He wrote a permit because he couldn't read the ledger, and you hanged him for the ledger's sins, and you did it in the square where his daughter could watch."
Ram's jaw tightened. "She didn't watch."
"She did. Her name is Tara. She's nine. The guards told me. They thought it would make me cooperate." Sita's voice did not rise. "It didn't. It only made me want to tell you that you're not a king, Ram. You're a man who has learned to hold a rope. And a man who learns to hold a rope very quickly starts to believe that every knot in the world is his to tie."
"They cheered," said Ram quietly.
"They cheered because you showed them the rope. That isn't the same as freedom."
"They were free. For the first time in years. Free of the Board, free of the brokers, free of the men with the cutting machines. You didn't see the wards, Sita. You didn't see the children counted for the stripping crews. I did. I've seen the feeds every morning for two months, and I've seen what was happening to the forest, and I stopped it."
"You stopped it by building a gallows out of steel pipe."
"I stopped it by taking the engines."
"Yes," said Sita. "You did."
She stood up. She did it slowly, deliberately, with the same economy she gave to everything, and she walked across the cold flagstone until she stood an arm's length from the bars of the grille set into the far wall. The grille was not a window. It was a slot, no wider than a hand, and beyond it there was only darkness. She stood with her face to it, and her back to him, and her shoulders very straight.
"Do you remember the bow?" she said.
"Yes."
"Do you remember what it felt like, when it cracked? When the string went slack and the whole hall went quiet and you were sitting there with the two halves in your hands, not knowing whether you'd broken it or it had broken you?"
"Yes."
"You wanted that. Not the bow. The silence. The moment when everyone in the room understood that they'd been waiting for someone to do something they didn't know how to do." She was still facing the grille. "You've spent your whole life being the one they waited for, Ram. And you've never once asked yourself what it would cost them to stop waiting."
He did not answer.
"I asked you on the bed at Mithila what you'd do if your word cost someone else," said Sita. "You said you'd pay with your life first. And then you took the engines, and you hanged a clerk, and you put your face on every spire in the city, and you've never once paid for any of it. Not with your life. Not with anything. You've paid for it with other people's."
"That isn't fair."
"No," said Sita, very gently. "It isn't."
The silence in the vault was total. The lamp burned steadily in its bracket. The walls shone faintly, and the air did not move.
Ram went to her. He did not decide to. He crossed the flagstones in four long strides and stopped behind her, close enough to feel the warmth of her body through the thin cotton, close enough to smell the faint clean scent of her hair, which had not been washed in two weeks and still smelled of the cedar oil she had brought from Mithila. He lifted his hand, and stopped it an inch from her shoulder.
She did not turn.
"Where is Lakshman?" she asked, very quietly.
He let his hand fall.
"In the south," he said. "In the Janasthan wards. Clearing the stripping rigs. The brokers have been putting up new toll-fords along the Ledger. I sent him with the vanguard."
"You sent your brother into the Janasthan."
"Yes."
"With how many men?"
"Two thousand. And the engines."
"The engines," Sita repeated softly. "The ones you turned round on the north rampart. The ones that answer to your pulse." She was silent for a moment. Then she said, in the same low even voice, "Did you tell him what to do when he got there?"
"He knows what to do."
"Did you tell him not to answer the voice?"
Ram stood very still.
"No," he said.
Sita nodded slowly, still facing the grille. "Then he'll answer it," she said. "Because he'll be standing in the dark with an enemy calling his name in his brother's voice, and he'll be tired, and he'll be frightened, and he'll want to believe that the thing calling him is someone who loves him. And he'll go to it. Won't he?"
"He wouldn't—"
"He would," said Sita. "He's your brother. He'd walk into a fire if he thought it was you calling from the other side."
Ram turned away from her.
He walked back across the flagstones to the iron door, and stood in the arch with his hand against the cold steel frame, and did not look back at her. The panel beside the door glowed faintly blue under his palm, and the green line along its base flickered as it read him, and he felt the pulse come up through the metal into the bones of his wrist, steady and heavy and entirely his own.
"You'll be fed," he said, without turning round. "Twice a day. Clean water. A blanket for the nights. A physician if you ask for one."
"Thank you," said Sita. Her voice was flat. "Your mercy is very generous. The stone would be proud of you."
"Don't."
"Why not? It's a very old stone. It's seen a great deal of mercy. Most of it went into the ground with the people who gave it."
He pressed his palm harder against the steel. The line of green light flickered again.
"The guards on this corridor will be doubled," he said. "No one comes down without my pulse. No one goes up without it either."
"And if I start to sing?"
"Then I'll hear it," said Ram. "And I'll decide what it means."
He stepped out into the corridor. The door hissed closed behind him, and the lock sealed with a faint heavy click that he felt in his teeth, and the blue light on the panel flickered once and went out.
He stood in the dark corridor for a long time with his palm flat against the iron door, and did not move, and did not count.
Behind the steel, very faintly, through a hundred thicknesses of stone, he thought he heard her begin to sing.
It was not a hymn. It was not a chant or a prayer or a lament. It was a short, low, wordless tune, the kind of thing a woman might hum while kneading bread or washing a child's hair in the evening, a sound that had no business being in a vault dug out of the living rock beneath a corporate citadel by men who had been dead for a thousand years.
He did not ask the guards to go in.
He stood there with his hand on the steel, and his pulse beat steadily under his palm, and he listened to the tune rise and fall through the stone, very faint and very clear, until at last it stopped, and the silence came back, and he went up the stairs to the roof, where the engines were turning slowly in their mounts, and the towers were lit, and the face on every spire was smiling down on the city with its wide, open, unanswered question.
---
CHAPTER : SARGA III: The Hill of Rishyamuk
> **[THE ROAD TAKEN]**
They did not go south. Lakshman said so on the first morning, sitting on the lip of the gully with his bound arm across his knees and the sun coming up white and cold over the stacks.
"The sea's a hundred miles of salt and wire," he said. "Vashkar's men are on every shoreline with their searchlights up. We walk straight down the coast with two bows and a bag of rice, and they'll see us from the air before we're close enough to spit at the water. Gidh said there's a way in. He didn't say what it cost."
"Who knows the way?" Ram asked.
Lakshman looked at him for a long moment, and then, unwillingly, he almost smiled.
"A fisherwoman," he said. "At the lake. The old woman with the net. Gidh used to drop his freight there, before he stole the glider. He told me once she could tell you the tide-tables from the way the reeds lean."
---
Pampa lake lay three days to the west, in a hollow between two low ridges of dark stone. It had been a great lake once. Now the shoreline was a set of pale mud bands and the reeds stood dead and rattling. On the far bank a small woman in a faded blue wrap came out of a reed hut and held a long fishing spear the way another woman might hold a walking stick. Her name was Sumati. She had felt Gidh die, she said. The lake had gone still, the way it did when a bird fell in the reeds and nobody noticed.
"He said you'd have a face like a closed door," she told Ram. "Eat. Fear is a bad fuel."
She cooked pale fish in a tin pot, and while Lakshman ate she unwound his bandage, cleaned the wound and packed it with a grey paste. It would keep the arm. When Ram told her what he had wanted at the ford, to make the Mistress afraid and then to give her water, she nodded slowly. "Both," she said.
Then she told them the way south ran through the hill. Rishyamuk, the hill of the brothers. Its king was Kesh, a mechanic who had fixed the stripping rigs until he understood how a machine died, and who did not know how a man did. His brother Vaal had sold the western trees and the caves where the old men kept their boats to a broker named Suka. Every morning Vaal climbed to the summit to look at the sea and wonder why nobody came to ask him for the boats.
"Go up the hill," said Sumati. "Tell Kesh you came from Gidh's lake. Tell him the truth, and see what he does with it."
"What do you want?" Ram asked her.
"A clean lake," said the old woman. "And for my friend's glider to have gone down where the sun can reach it."
Rishyamuk rose out of the western plain like a broken tooth. It was not tall, but its sides were steep and scarred, and its crown was split into three rocky spurs that caught the early light and threw long blue shadows down across the scrub. On the lower slopes the trees had been stripped. Their trunks stood in grey ranks like the bones of a drowned army, and between them the raked earth had been turned over and left to bake, and along the old access road that wound up the eastern flank, a line of rusted machines stood silent in the dust.
They were the stripping rigs. Ram had seen them on the feeds for two months, and he had seen the trucks in the Janasthan ford, and he had thought he knew what they looked like. He had not known how large they were. The biggest of them stood at the foot of the road where the hill began, a tracked iron beast nearly the height of the palace gate, with a massive arm folded down along its flank and a bucket on the end of the arm as broad as a cart. Its cab was dark. Its tracks were rusted through. Its engine had been pulled out and left in a heap beside it, and wild thorn had grown up through the gaps in its iron plates.
The arm had come down across the road. It lay on the dust like a fallen tree, its bucket rolled over on its side, blocking the path for the width of a man.
"The iron bull," said Lakshman, looking at it. "They call it that in the wards. The men say it used to eat whole forests."
"It's dead," said Ram.
"It's still in the way."
They climbed past it. It was not hard. Ram put one shoulder under the drooping edge of the bucket, and felt the cold iron bite into his muscles, and lifted. The bucket groaned on its hinge. It came up a hand's breadth off the dust. Then it rose another hand's breadth, and another, until Ram had the whole weight of the arm on his shoulder and was walking backward with it, a slow lurching step at a time, until he had shoved it clear of the path and let it fall back with a ringing crash that echoed off the hillside.
He stood there, breathing hard, with his hands on his knees.
"That," said a voice from the rocks above, "was not a demon."
A figure came down the slope toward them. He moved quickly, almost lightly, leaping from one ledge to the next with a sure-footed ease that made Lakshman's hand go to his bow. He was lean and wiry, dressed in ragged brown cotton with a length of rope coiled over one shoulder, and his face was young and quick and amused, with a long clever nose and eyes that were never still. He landed on a flat rock a few paces from Ram and crouched there, balanced on the balls of his feet, and looked up at Ram with an expression of frank, delighted curiosity.
"It's a machine," he said. "Very large. Very stupid. It doesn't have a face. The old songs will have to be rewritten."
"Who are you?" Ram asked.
"Hanu," said the young man. He grinned. "I scout for Kesh. I climb things. I run errands for the old men who keep the boats. I was once a clerk in the Janasthan stacks, but I got bored of counting tonnage and started counting the stars instead, and then the stars got boring, and then I got caught in a fence." He tapped the rope on his shoulder. "This is how I got out. Gidh told us you'd come up the hill. He said you were the only man in the country who could lift an iron bull without asking anybody to help."
"Did he say anything else?"
Hanu's grin faded, just slightly. "He said you'd want to see the boats," he said. "And that you'd want to ask Kesh for them with your face shut. He said I should watch for the moment you forgot how."
---
The caves on the western slope had once been a pilgrims' shelter, and their mouths still bore the carved lintels of some forgotten god. Inside, the rock had been cut into rooms: low and smoky and crowded with people, some of whom Ram recognised from the ledgers of the Janasthan ford, shaking his head in a kind of exhausted disbelief. There were children sleeping in heaps against the walls. There were old men mending nets by the light of smoking clay lamps. There were women grinding grain on flat stones and singing under their breath, and a boy of about twelve sitting cross-legged in a corner, carving a long thin strip of driftwood with a bone-handled knife that Ram thought he recognised.
The people went quiet as the three strangers came into the cave-mouth. They watched without speaking. Some of them turned their backs. One of the women rose and gathered her children and moved them to the far wall, and her face as she passed Ram was white and rigid with fear.
Kesh was sitting at the back of the largest chamber, on a pile of folded blankets, with his back against the rock and a broken tin cup in his hand. He was a big man, broad-shouldered and heavy, with a face that had been burned and scarred by something that had left the skin tight and shiny across one cheek. His hair was grey at the temples and cropped close. His hands, when he set down the cup and rose slowly to his feet, were enormous and calloused, and there was black grease ground into the lines of the knuckles that no amount of washing had ever removed.
He looked at Ram for a long time without speaking. Then he said, in a deep, gravelly voice that was not hostile but was very tired:
"You're the face."
"I'm the one from Gidh's lake," said Ram.
"Same thing, from where I'm standing." Kesh's eyes moved to the bound arm on Lakshman's shoulder, and to the black bow on his back, and then, very briefly, to Sita's empty place beside him. His brows drew together. "Where's the woman?"
"Taken," said Ram.
Kesh grunted. It was not surprise. It was something heavier, a kind of weary acknowledgment, as though he had been expecting bad news for so long that the arrival of it was almost a relief.
"By Vashkar?"
"Yes."
"Then she's already a long way across the water," said Kesh. "You won't find her by standing in my cave. You'll find her by getting a boat." He set the cup down on a flat stone beside the blankets. "What do you want from me, prince?"
Ram told him.
He did not dress it up. He did not talk about the Board or the towers or the boons, and he did not talk about the panel or the pulse or the thing that had happened in the glass room. He said that he had come from the north with a wife and a brother, and that his wife had been taken across the sea by the Lord of the southern island, and that he needed a way across the water that the sea-line would not see from the air. He said that the only men who knew the old tidal paths were the men of this hill, and that the men of this hill were hiding from a brother who had sold them to the brokers. He said that he could not promise Kesh that the boats would come back. He said that he could promise to go up the hill tomorrow, and to ask Vaal for the caves to be given back, and that if Vaal refused, he would take them.
When he had finished, the cave was silent.
Kesh looked at him for a long time. Then he laughed. It was a short, rough, startled sound, the laugh of a man who has been holding a stone in his chest for years and has just found, to his surprise, that he has forgotten how to put it down.
"Take it," he said. "The caves. Take the hill, if you can. Vaal's got a hundred men up there with Suka's guns and a hole in his heart the size of a rig-bucket, and they'll all run the moment they see you coming, because they know what you did at the ford. The whole coast knows." He spread his big hands. "But don't come back to me when it's done and tell me you did it for my sake, prince. I'm a mechanic. I don't need saving. I need a machine that works."
"Then what do you need?"
Kesh's eyes narrowed. For a moment he did not answer. Then he looked past Ram, toward the cave-mouth, where the western light was pouring in across the stone floor in long gold bars, and his face changed. It softened. It grew older, and sadder, and very tired.
"I need my brother to be the one who asks me for the boats," he said quietly. "Not you. Not the face on the spires. Him. Because if I take the hill from him with a stranger's arrow in my back, I'll never sleep again, and neither will he. And then we'll both have to live with it, in the salt and the dust, for the rest of our lives."
The cave was very still.
Hanu, who had been sitting on the rock near the entrance with his rope in his lap, whistled softly under his breath.
"That's the first honest thing I've heard a king say since the Board's clerks started hanging each other for the feeds," he said.
Kesh shot him a look. Hanu only grinned and went back to coiling the rope.
Ram said nothing. He was thinking of a sandal with a double-looped knot. He was thinking of a boy on a copper roof with a broken bow across his knees, and of a man on a stone bench in a vault, humming to himself in the dark. He was thinking, for the first time in his life, that the thing he had been most afraid of was not being killed, nor being wrong, nor even losing the people he loved. It was being the one who had to decide for someone else whether they were allowed to be sorry.
"Then I'll go up the hill," he said. "And ask him myself."
Kesh looked up.
"Not for your boats," said Ram. "For his."
---
Hanu had found the letter the night before, in a tin trunk under Vaal's bunk in the upper cave. It was a single sheet of thick paper in a broker's neat hand, folded in four. Ram read it by the lamp.
"Vaal,
You asked in your last letter why the Board's men come up the salt road smelling of their own money. I will tell you, since you will not ask anyone else.
The forest is not being stolen. It is being cashed. Every trunk on the western slope is a payment on a debt the Board owes the salt-road towns, which it has been paying in promises for thirty years. I am only the man who makes the promises solvent.
My father cut the last sal on his own field for a funeral pyre, and the Board's magistrate hanged him for it from the lower gate. I was nine. I decided then that the trees would never again belong to men who were hungry. Better they belong to men who can pay, and who keep the rigs running so the villages have something to sell.
Mari is asking for the ledgers. He thinks I am holding back the true figures. He is right. A partner who asks for ledgers becomes a ledger, and I have no intention of becoming one.
Khar wants the ford. The Board wants the caves for its engine-works. I want the mills. In ten years the mills will be mine, and the grandchildren of the men who were hanged will work them, and they will call me a benefactor, because I was the one who made the work exist.
Keep your hill. Keep your boats, if they still float. I will send the scope-man for the rest.
S."
Ram folded the letter along its creases. It was not a confession. Suka had never once thought of himself as a man who needed to confess.
They went up at dawn, the four of them, with Hanu leading the way along a narrow goat-path that wound through the rocks above the cave-mouth and came out onto the bare summit of the middle spur. The wind was strong and cold up there. It came off the sea in long gusts that smelled of salt and kelp, and it roared through the broken stone and set the dead thorn-bushes rattling like dry bones. Below them the western plain stretched out to the horizon, grey and gold and flat, and beyond the plain, very faint, a thin silver line marked the place where the land stopped and the water began.
On the summit's flat top stood a ruined shrine, a low square tower of black stone with its roof fallen in and its doorway choked with rubble. Beside it a tall dead tree had been stripped to the wood, its branches sheared away, its trunk bleached grey and split by lightning. On the far side of the tree, in a wide shallow bowl of rock that had once been a sacrifice-court, stood a row of seven more dead trunks, each one standing alone, each one the height of a palm and as thick as a man's thigh, planted in a neat line across the open ground like the posts of an unfinished fence.
Vaal was there.
He was sitting on a boulder beside the first of the dead trunks, with his back to the wind and his legs spread out in front of him. He was a lean, sharp-featured man in a long coat of dark leather that had been cut from the hide of some animal Ram did not recognise, and his face was thin and hard and handsome, with a clean-shaven jaw and eyes that were pale and cold as winter water. Around him, on the rocks and in the shadow of the shrine, stood perhaps thirty men in grey coveralls. They held rifles. They did not raise them. They only watched.
"Brother," said Vaal, without rising. His voice was light and easy and carried effortlessly over the wind. "You've brought company."
"I've brought a man who wants your boats," said Kesh.
Vaal laughed. It was a pleasant sound, unhurried. "My boats," he said. "Yes. I had heard you'd been living in the caves like a grub, eating the old men's fish and sulking about the rigs. I didn't think you'd find the courage to come up here and ask me for anything."
"I'm not asking for myself."
"No?" Vaal's eyes moved past Kesh, to Ram, and stayed there. They were very cold. "Then who are you asking for?"
"For the caves," said Ram. "And the men in them. And the children."
Vaal smiled. He stood up slowly, brushing the dust from his coat, and walked a few paces across the rocks, and stopped beside the first of the dead trunks, one hand resting lightly on its grey bark.
"You're the face," he said.
"People keep telling me that."
"I've seen the feeds. Suka showed me, last month, in the wards. He thought it was very funny. He said the Returning Prince had come back to take the forest and hang the clerks, and that I'd better get used to his face, because he was going to be the one who signed the permits from now on." Vaal's voice had gone softer. "I don't think it's very funny. I think it's the most dangerous thing I've ever seen. A man who can turn a face into a country."
"I didn't turn it."
"No. The Board did. The feeds did. Everyone does, when they need a face to hold up in front of a crowd." Vaal turned and looked out over the dead trunks, to the grey plain and the silver line of the sea beyond. His hand moved on the bark. "And you came up my hill with my brother at your back, and the bow-breaker's brother at your side, and a fisherwoman's blessing on your head, to ask me for the boats. For the sea, and the sea-line, and the men in the caves. You're going to take Vashkar's island with a fisherman's net, prince, and a dead king's bow, and a face. Do you know what that makes you?"
"No," said Ram.
"A fool," said Vaal, very softly, and his hand came away from the bark, and in it he held a long narrow pistol, black and dull, that had been tucked into the back of his belt all along.
It was not aimed at Ram.
It was aimed at Kesh.
The thirty men on the rocks had raised their rifles. The wind howled. Hanu had dropped into a crouch on the rock beside Ram, his rope already in his fist, and his face was sharp and quick and utterly without fear. Lakshman had the black bow in his good hand, and the arrow nocked, and his bound arm trembled against his chest.
Kesh did not move. He stood with his big scarred hands loose at his sides, and looked at his brother across the rocks, and his face was the face of a man who has been expecting this moment for a very long time and has decided, at last, not to flinch.
"Go on, then," he said quietly. "Do it."
Vaal's finger tightened on the trigger.
Ram did not decide. The count came to him, and it was a different kind of count this time, not the slow steady beat of the cylinder in the hermitage or the pulse of the glass in the old control room, but something quicker and sharper and more desperate, a thing that broke into his chest like a thorn going into the palm of a hand. Four. Two. Six. It would not hold. There were thirty rifles and a pistol and a king's life at the end of a single finger, and the wind was blowing from the wrong direction, and the rocks were full of men who had been told what the face on the spires would do.
He did not draw his own bow.
He took one step forward, into the open, where every rifle on the hill could see him, and he said, very clearly, in the voice he had used in the Chamber of Oaths:
"Vaal. Look at me."
Vaal's eyes flicked to him, and for a heartbeat the pistol wavered.
"You want the hill," said Ram. "You want Suka's money and the boats and the trees. I can't give you those. But I can give you the thing you've been trying to take for twenty years, and never once been able to hold in your hand. Your brother will walk down this hill tomorrow, and the caves will be his, and the men in the caves will be fed. And no one will ever sell him to the brokers again. Not because I hanged the brokers. Because I asked you, now, in front of your men, to choose."
"To choose what?"
"Whether you'll be the one who gave it back."
Vaal stared at him. His mouth worked. His hand shook.
And then, from behind the first dead trunk, where the shadows of the shrine lay deepest against the rock, a second voice spoke, very softly, in the same rhythm as the wind.
"Brother," it said. "Don't listen to the face. Shoot him now."
Ram's head turned.
There was a man crouched in the shadow behind the stripped tree. He had been there all along. He was thin and grey and quiet, dressed in a coverall the colour of ash, and in his hand he held a long rifle with a scope fitted to its barrel, and the scope was pointed, very steadily, at the centre of Ram's chest.
Ram saw the muzzle. He saw the small bright glint of the lens, catching the light from the rising sun. He saw the man's finger, very still on the trigger, and the man's face, calm and patient and utterly without malice, the face of someone who has done a thing many times before and has never once needed to hate it.
He did not move.
He could not have moved if he had wanted to. The count had stopped. There was nothing in his chest but a single cold beat, slow and heavy, a stone in a dry riverbed, and in the space between that beat and the next, he understood with a clarity that was almost peaceful that he was about to die on a hill in the western wilderness with his bow still unstrung on his back, because a man he had never met had decided that his face was worth more than his life.
The rifle fired.
It was not Ram who was hit.
The bullet took Vaal in the back, between the shoulder-blades, as he turned his head toward the sound. It was not a shot from the scope. It was a shot from the rocks above the shrine, from the dark slot between two boulders where a fifth man had been crouching all along, with a rifle of his own, and Ram had not seen him at all. Vaal jerked forward. His pistol went off into the dirt. He staggered, took two steps toward the first dead tree, and then his knees gave way and he fell, face-first, onto the bare grey rock at the foot of the trunk, and did not move again.
The thirty men on the rocks stood frozen.
Kesh had not moved. He stood with his hands at his sides and looked at his brother lying in the dust, and his face did not change at all.
The man crouched behind the dead tree lowered his rifle slowly and looked at the rocks above, where the fifth man had come out of the shadows. It was a small thin figure in a grey coverall, with a face like a closed book, and in his hand the rifle still smoked faintly.
"The shot was called," said the man in the coverall, into the silence. His voice was dry and cold. "Suka said the king would fall to a face on the spires. He didn't say the face would stand still and die for it. It's a poor bargain, my lord. The brokers don't pay for poor bargains."
He turned and walked away down the far side of the rocks, and the thirty men in grey, one by one, slung their rifles and followed him, not looking back.
---
Ram walked across the rocks to Vaal's body.
He did not look at the others. He knelt beside the dead man in the dust, at the foot of the first stripped trunk, and turned him gently onto his back. Vaal's face was calm. His pale eyes were open, and they were fixed on the sky above with an expression of mild, faintly puzzled surprise, as though someone had called his name in a crowded street and he had turned to see who it was.
Kesh came and knelt on the other side of the body.
For a long time neither of them said anything. The wind howled over the summit and tugged at the dead thorn-bushes and carried the faint smell of salt up from the plain.
"You didn't stop him," said Kesh at last.
"No."
"You could have."
Ram looked down at the body. "I think he'd already decided," he said. "I only gave him a reason to be seen deciding."
Kesh was silent. Then he reached out with one big scarred hand and closed his brother's eyes, very gently, with the tips of two fingers, the way a man might close the eyes of a sleeping child.
"He used to fix things," he said quietly. "When we were boys. Clocks. Lamps. Once he rebuilt a flyer from the wreckage of a Board truck, and it flew for three days, and then the rotor came off and it fell into the Godavari. He was so angry he didn't speak to me for a month." His voice was rough. "I thought he'd grow out of it. I thought the hill would make him see sense. I thought I could wait."
"It wasn't your fault," said Ram.
"No," said Kesh. "It was yours."
He said it without heat. He said it the way he had said the hill was not for him, the way a mechanic states the cause of a fault when he has finally found the broken part. Ram did not argue. He knelt in the dust with his hands on his knees and looked at the dead man's face, and the thing that had been holding him still on the summit for the last quarter-hour began, very slowly, to let go.
Behind him, Hanu came up from the rocks and stood silently at his shoulder. The rope was still coiled in his hand.
"A king who dies from a hiding place," Hanu said quietly, looking at the body, "rules from one too. Did you know that? My grandfather used to say it. He was a clerk in the Board's old archive. He read every decree the Board ever signed, and he said every one of them had a man behind a rock somewhere, holding the trigger, and waiting for the king to come out into the open."
"Yes," said Ram.
"You knew it, then."
"I knew it when I stepped forward."
Hanu was quiet for a moment. Then he said, very softly, "Did you know what the man with the scope would do?"
"No."
"Then you were lucky."
"No," said Ram. "I was counting. The count didn't stop when I stepped out. It just went very cold."
---
They buried Vaal at the foot of the stripped tree, under a cairn of loose grey stones that Kesh and Lakshman built together in silence while Hanu went down the hill to bring up the boats. Ram helped. He carried the stones two at a time, one-handed, because the other was cupped against his chest with the burnt fingers of the palm still tender from the river-rock at Ahalya's ghat. Nobody spoke except to ask for a stone, or to pass one.
When the cairn was finished, Kesh stood at its head with his hands clasped behind his back and his face grey and bowed. He did not pray. He stood there for a long time, looking down at the stones, and then, without turning round, he said:
"I'll take the caves. I'll take the boats. I'll take the hill, if the men will follow me up it. And I'll take your word, prince, that the brokers will never sell another man on these slopes."
"You don't have to take my word," said Ram.
"No. But I'll take it anyway." Kesh turned his head at last. His eyes were red-rimmed and unreadable. "Because I think you're the only man who ever came up this hill to ask my brother for something he was willing to give. And I think he would have given it, if he'd had one more minute of being asked."
Ram said nothing. He looked at the cairn. The wind moved over the grey stones and set the dead thorn-bushes rattling against the shrine's fallen wall, and somewhere far off down the western slope a gull cried, a thin sharp keening sound that rose and dropped and was carried away over the sea.
He thought of the man in the grey coverall, walking away down the rocks without looking back. He thought of the scope on the rifle, catching the light from the rising sun.
The brokers don't pay for poor bargains.
Somebody had paid for this one. He did not yet know who.
---
Shadow III: The Ravine of the Ledger
> **[SHADOW ROAD]** *The Janasthan burned on the order of the one who gave it.*
The engines were not made for ravines. Ram had known that since the day he first walked the north rampart as a boy and watched the old men tune the long black barrels for the morning test. They were made for open ground, for long clean sightlines across a field where nothing could hide. They were made to see the whole of a country at once and to answer the pulse of the man who stood above them.
The Janasthan wards were not open ground.
They were a maze of broken embankments and dry gullies and choked thorn-thickets, laced with the old rail-cuttings that the brokers had dug through the scrub a generation ago and then abandoned, and every cutting was a trap. The Khar brothers had understood that long before the vanguard arrived. They had dug their men into the cliffs above the cuttings, in deep stone bunkers reinforced with salvaged plate, and when the vanguard came marching down the Ledger's dry bed in the grey pre-dawn light, the bunkers opened, and the first volley came down the cutting like a hailstorm.
Ram saw it from the ridge. He stood at the edge of the command-post on the western rise, with the engine-crews working in silence behind him and the officers of the Janasthan staff standing in a half-circle at his back, and he watched the dust rise in long pale columns along the length of the cutting. He could see the vanguard's lead company breaking. He could see men running, and falling, and not getting up.
He could see Lakshman's black bow flash in the front rank, and the arrow go up in a high silver arc into the cliff, and then another, and another, and the flashes of return fire from the bunkers answering it like lightning in a dry summer storm.
"Pull them back," said the staff officer at his left, a young man with a pale face and a voice that trembled. "My lord. They'll be cut to pieces in the cutting. We can't hold the Ledger with two thousand men and no cover."
"We can," said Ram.
"With respect, my lord—"
"We have the engines. Give me the engines."
The officer hesitated. Then he looked at the crew-chief, who looked at the panel-screen in the back of the command-truck, and nodded slowly, and said, "Ready, my lord. Awaiting the pulse."
Ram did not move toward the panel. He could feel the pulse in the air, a slow thrum rising from the engines on the ridge, from the great black barrels that had been trundled out onto the flatbeds and hauled up the western slope under the cover of night. They had been waiting all morning for the house to speak. They were eager. They wanted to answer.
He looked down at the cutting. Lakshman was crouched behind a rock at the head of the vanguard, with his bow raised and his face streaked with dust and blood, and he was looking up at the ridge. Even from here Ram could see his expression. It was not fear. It was the same look Lakshman had given him on the palace roof the morning the sage walked into the city, a stubborn, furious, wounded look that said he would do whatever was asked of him, and that he would hate himself for it afterward.
A runner came up the ridge. He was breathless and covered in grime, a boy in a torn grey coverall, and he flung himself down at the foot of the command-truck and gasped out the message in a rush: "My lord, the prince says the cliff is too high. He says he can't take the bunkers without going up the face, and the men won't climb it under fire. He says—he says he needs the engines on the north ridge, and he needs them now, or he'll pull back to the Ledger's mouth and lose the day."
"Tell him to hold," said Ram.
The boy stared up at him. "My lord—"
"Tell him the engines are coming. Tell him to hold the cutting for thirty minutes, and then to send the flanking company up the east bank under the smoke. Tell him to do it now."
The boy scrambled to his feet and ran.
Ram turned back to the panel-screen. Its surface was dark, reflecting the grey sky and the shadowed faces of the staff officers around him. He could see his own reflection in it, faint and tired and very still, a man in a long dark coat with dust on his shoulders and a bow across his back that was not his own. He had taken the bow from the house's armoury the week after the vault. It was an older bow, heavier than the one he had carried from Ayodhya, and its string was stiff and its limbs were cold, and he had not once drawn it since the morning he took it down from the rack. He did not need to. He had a more important thing to hold.
"Give me the pulse," he said.
The crew-chief leaned forward. "Confirming house pulse, my lord."
Ram laid his palm flat against the screen.
The glass was colder here than it had been in the old control room. It was wet with dew, and the chill went up into his wrist like a thin silver wire, and he felt the engines on the ridge swing toward him, one by one, their barrels tilting up and round as though they had heard their master's voice across a great distance. The thrum deepened. The air went tight.
AUTHORISE, said the screen. DIRECT THE ENGINES.
He did not answer it. He did not need to. The screen had learned him. It knew the rhythm of his breath, the beat of his heart, the particular steadiness of the hand that had lifted the bucket of the iron bull and carried the cairn-stones and held the rifle still on the summit of Rishyamuk. It knew that this was the hand that had turned the north rampart in the night, and that it did not hesitate once it had decided.
"Fire on the cliffs," he said quietly. "All bunkers. Take the cutting from above."
The crew-chief's voice rang out across the ridge. The engines answered.
---
It was a long, slow roar.
The first barrel fired with a sound like a mountain splitting, a deep grinding shock that rolled down the western slope and into the Ledger's dry bed and came back off the cliffs in a series of hollow booming echoes, one after another, until the whole valley seemed to be shaking. A bright white flash lit the cliff-face. Then a column of black smoke and grey dust rose up over the ridge, and the second barrel fired, and the third, and the fourth, until the whole western rise was shuddering with the discharge and the air was thick with the stink of scorched stone and burning thorn.
Ram watched the bunkers go.
He watched the cliffs crack open. He watched whole sections of rock break away from the face and tumble down into the cutting in long grey slides. He watched the salvaged plates on the bunker-roofs buckle and fold like paper, and the men inside them vanish in sheets of orange flame, and the thin grey shapes of the Khar brothers' rifles spin into the air and fall.
And he watched the Ledger.
The dust was rising. It was rising from the cutting itself, from the place where the vanguard had been pinned down, and it was rising faster than the smoke from the cliffs. It came up in long pale waves, boiling over the rock-falls and rolling down the dry bed toward the ridge. Ram could not see through it. He could only see the flashes of the engines' muzzles through the dust, bright and terrible, and the shapes of men running through it, and a single black bow raised high above the rubble at the head of the cutting, and then not raised any longer.
"My lord," said the crew-chief, in a strange flat voice. "The pulse is stuttering."
"Hold it."
"My lord, there's a signature in the cutting. Friendly. Heavy. It's—"
"Hold the fire," said Ram. "Hold it for the flanking company."
The crew-chief did not move.
Ram looked up. The old man's face was grey and frightened. His hands hovered over the controls, not touching them. In the screen's reflection, Ram could see the faint red lights of the friendly signatures blinking on the grid, a cluster of them thick in the bottom of the cutting, and beside them, in a different colour, a single pale pulse, rising and falling slowly as the body beneath it fought for breath.
"My lord," said the crew-chief again, very quietly. "Please. The prince is still down there."
Ram put his palm back on the screen.
He did not want to. He wanted, more than he had wanted anything since the morning in the Chamber of Oaths, to take his hand away and run down the ridge and into the dust and drag his brother out of the rubble by the collar. But the pulse would not let him. It held his wrist like a chain of cold silver, and beneath his palm the engines were waiting, and the flanking company was climbing the east bank in the smoke, and the feeds were running, somewhere far to the north, his face on every spire of the city, waiting to see whether the Returning Prince would hold the ravine or fall back.
If he fell back, the stripping would go on. The Khar brothers would take the Ledger, and the fords, and the toll-caves, and every man who had ever cut a tree would be paid for it. The clerks would still be hanging in the lower ward. The city would still be waiting for someone to answer for it.
He could not pull back. He could only hold.
"Fire," he said.
The crew-chief closed his eyes. Then, slowly, with a shaking hand, he touched the control.
The engines fired again.
---
When the dust cleared, the cutting was silent.
Ram walked down into it alone. He did not take the command-truck, or the staff, or the engine-crews, who stood on the ridge in a frozen half-circle with their faces turned away from the valley and their hands at their sides. He went down the western slope on foot, through the smoke and the thorn and the drifting ash, and his boots crunched on broken glass and spent shell-casings and the blackened fragments of the Khar brothers' bunkers. The air was hot. It stung his eyes. He could see nothing ahead but the grey drift of the dust, and then, slowly, the shapes of the dead.
They lay in the cutting in long untidy rows. Some were still in their grey coveralls. Some were in the uniforms of the city's vanguard, dark blue with silver braid at the cuffs, and Ram knew some of their faces from the palace, from the armoury, from the long dull mornings on the roof when the engines had been tested and the hundred-killers had turned. He did not look at their faces for long. He looked for the one he was afraid of.
He found Lakshman at the head of the cutting, where the rock-fall had buried the front rank. He was lying on his back in a shallow scoop of grey dust, with his bound arm flung out across his chest and his black bow lying beside him, snapped clean across the middle. The limbs had been broken by the blast. The string hung loose and useless in the ash.
His face was not burned. It was grey and smeared with dust and perfectly calm, and his eyes were half-open, looking up at the sky above the Ledger with an expression that was not pain or fear or even surprise, but a kind of weary, fierce, exhausted patience, the look he had given the golden deer on the night of the siding, when he had known it was lying and had let it lead him anyway because he could not bear not to follow.
Ram knelt in the dust.
He did not touch the body at first. He could not. He knelt there with his hands on his thighs and his head bowed, and he listened to the silence of the cutting, the faint hiss of settling ash, the distant crackle of burning thorn on the far slope, and the soft slow drip of something wet from the rocks above.
"Lakshman," he said.
His brother's eyes moved. Slowly, very slowly, they came round to Ram's face. There was blood at the corner of his mouth. He breathed in, a short shallow rattle, and when he spoke, the words came out thin and broken and very clear.
"You didn't pull back."
"No."
"I knew you wouldn't." A cough shook his chest, and blood came with it, dark and bright. "I told them. I told them you'd never give up the ravine. I said you'd rather lose the men than admit the cliff was too high."
"I'm sorry."
"Don't be." Lakshman's eyes drifted shut, and then opened again with an effort. "Just tell me one thing. Did the flanking company make it up?"
Ram could not answer. He could only shake his head.
Lakshman breathed out, long and slow, and something in his face eased, very slightly, as though he had been given the answer he had been waiting for and did not need any other.
"Good," he whispered. "Then it wasn't for nothing."
His hand moved in the dust. It found Ram's, and closed around it, with a grip that was weaker than it had been on the road, weaker than it had ever been, but still stubbornly, unmistakably his own.
"Brother," said Lakshman. "Count again."
Ram stared at him.
"The men," Lakshman whispered. "The dead. You always count. You did it at the ford. You did it on the road. You did it when you took the Salt Widow's arrow out of the child's door." His eyes were wet, but his mouth was almost smiling. "Count them, Ram. Count the ones who came up the bank. Count the ones who didn't. Count the ones who were sent up the cliff because the cliff was too high for anybody to climb and you couldn't stand to say so. Count them all. You'll get a different number this time, and it'll be wrong, and you'll have to live with it anyway."
"I can't."
"You can," said Lakshman. "You always could. You just never liked the answer."
His hand went slack in Ram's.
Ram sat there in the ash for a long time, holding his brother's hand, and he did not count. He did not reach for the breath, or the beat, or the slow steady pulse that had held him steady through the ford and the hermitage and the hanging-square and the glass room in the north tower. It did not come. There was nothing left in him to call it up. There was only the silence of the cutting, and the ash settling over his brother's face like the grey dust of a river-bed after the monsoon, and the cold empty weight of a hand that would never again close around his own.
---
He burned the Ledger that night.
He did not give the order to the staff. He gave it to the panel, in the cold of the command-truck, with his palm on the wet glass and his voice steady and flat, and the glass took it without question. Clear the stripping rigs. Fire the western slope. Take the wards. The engines answered. They fired all night from the ridge, a long slow thunder that rolled down the valley and back off the cliffs, and the flames went up in pale gold sheets across the dead thorn and the broken embankments and the rows of rusted machines in the old rail-cuttings. The brokers' trucks burst into fire one by one, like lamps being lit in a forgotten room. The smoke rose in a thick black column that blotted out the stars.
By morning the whole western rise of the Janasthan was a blackened ruin, smoking under a pall of ash, and the Ledger's dry bed was filled with the charred bones of a thousand machines. The old fords were gone. The toll-houses were ash. The stacks along the eastern horizon had stopped breathing, for the first time in eleven years, and the sky over the dead country was clear and cold and blue and empty.
The feeds carried it north before the sun was up. Ram did not watch them. He sat in the command-truck with the dead bow across his knees and listened to the crew-chief, in a low frightened voice, reading out the figures from the staff's report.
"Four hundred and twelve of the vanguard dead, my lord. Two hundred and six wounded, of whom forty are not expected to live. The Khar brothers' bunkers are destroyed. Their men have fled south, toward the sea-line." The old man swallowed. "The stripping rigs are all burning. The wards have been cleared. The brokers' accounts are frozen. The feeds are calling it a victory."
"Is it?"
The crew-chief did not answer. He looked at the screen, where the ruined valley lay under its pall of smoke and the grey dead bodies lay in their long untidy rows, and his face was the face of a man who had counted the cost of something and found it too large to carry.
"Count again," said Ram quietly.
The crew-chief looked up.
Ram did not explain. He looked at the screen, and at the reflection of his own face in its wet black glass, pale and tired and very still, and he thought of his brother's voice in the ash, rough and clear and wholly without self-pity: You always could. You just never liked the answer.
He did not count again. He did not need to. He already knew the number. It was the one that would be written in the ledger, and read out on the feeds, and carved into the stone of the Chamber of Oaths beside the boons, in a hand that would never once be asked whose face had been on the spires when the engines fired.
It was a victory.
It was the last thing he would ever let anyone call it.
He put his hand flat against the screen. The glass was warm now, warmer than it had been, and the pulse came up through it, slow and heavy and steady, a stone in a dry riverbed, the beat of a heart that had not once, in the whole long night, been allowed to stop.
AUTHORISE, said the screen.
Ram lifted his hand from the glass.
AUTHORISE. DIRECT THE ENGINES. SOUTH.
He looked at the words for a long time. Then he laid his palm back on the screen, and said, in a voice so quiet that the crew-chief had to lean forward to hear it:
"Yes."
---
CHAPTER : SARGA IV: The Caves of the Boats
> **[THE ROAD TAKEN]**
The boats had been hidden for thirty years in a cave behind a curtain of creeper: eleven hulls of salvaged plate riveted to teak ribs, two of them still whole. Kesh looked them over with his pipe clenched in his teeth. Three needed new ribs, four needed plate along the waterline, and the rest were firewood. Ten days if the men worked, he said. They would not work fast. They were tired, and frightened, and sick of being led by a king who used to be a mechanic.
Ram worked the shelf with them through the long grey days, holding plates while the men hammered, and they watched him sidelong. He had not spoken of Vaal since the summit. He had not spoken of him to Kesh, who had buried his brother without weeping, or to Hanu, who had come back from the caves with a look Ram did not like.
Lakshman noticed.
He came down the shelf on the third afternoon with his bow across his back and his arm bound in fresh linen, and stood at Ram's elbow and watched him hammer a rivet into a rib-plate for almost a minute before he spoke.
"You're doing the thing with your face again," he said.
"What thing?"
"The one where you look like you're listening to a voice nobody else can hear." Lakshman's tone was light, but his eyes were hard. "The one you had at the ford, and on the hill, and in the north tower with your hand on the glass. You're doing it now, with a hammer, in front of a boat."
Ram set the hammer down.
"I should have asked him sooner," he said.
"Vaal?"
"Yes. I should have gone up the hill the first night, when Kesh first said his brother had sold the caves. Instead I made the three of us wait for a fisherwoman's blessing and a tide-table, and then I stood on the summit and asked him to choose in front of his own men." He looked at the rivet in the plate. "He chose. And it cost him everything."
"He chose to put a pistol on his brother," said Lakshman. "That's his choice. You didn't put the pistol in his hand."
"No. I only made him show it to me."
Lakshman was silent for a moment. Then he picked up a length of rope from the rock and began to coil it, one-handed, with clumsy, careful movements, the way a man coils rope when he has been taught by someone else and has never quite got the knack of it himself.
"You want to know what I think," he said, not looking up.
"Yes."
"I think you're going to keep doing this. Every time something goes wrong, you'll take the whole weight of it onto your shoulders, and you'll walk around under it like a man carrying a dead animal, and you'll call it responsibility. And every time, somebody who loves you will have to stand next to you and watch you do it." He finished the coil and dropped it on the rock. "I'm tired of watching, brother. I'd rather you were angry at me for something I did than sorry for something you didn't."
"You didn't do anything to Vaal."
"I know. That's not what I meant."
Ram looked at him. Lakshman's face was thin and drawn, with dark hollows under the eyes, and there was a stubborn, weary, almost tender look in it that Ram had not seen since the morning they had walked out of the palace gate together.
"What did you mean?" Ram asked quietly.
"I meant that you could let me be the one who's sorry, for once," said Lakshman. "I'm good at it. I've had practice. Ask Sita, she'll tell you."
Ram did not answer. He picked up the hammer again, and set the rivet, and struck it once, twice, three times, until the sound of it rang off the cliffs and drowned out the surf. Lakshman watched him for a while longer. Then he turned and walked back up the shelf toward the caves, and his bow swayed on his back with every step, and he did not look round.
---
The monsoon broke on the twelfth night.
Ram had felt it coming for a week. The air had grown thick and wet and heavy, so that breathing it was like drawing water into the lungs, and the sea had turned a dull pewter colour that stretched to the horizon without a single break. The wind had changed. It came off the water in long cool sighs now, smelling of rain and kelp and something green and faintly sweet, and at dusk the clouds began to pile up over the sea-line in towering grey cliffs that caught the last of the sun and turned it to rose and bronze, and then, very slowly, to black.
The first drops fell at midnight. Ram was lying on a bed of dried reed at the back of the cave, listening to the men snore around him, when he heard them: a soft, hesitant patter on the rock above the entrance, like fingers tapping on a drum-skin very far away. He lay still and listened. The patter grew. It became a rustle, and then a steady hiss, and then a roar, and he sat up in the dark and felt the cold air rush into the cave-mouth, bringing with it a smell so sharp and clean that for a moment he could not breathe.
Rain.
He went out. He did not wake anyone. He walked through the sleeping cave, past the huddled bodies and the banked embers of the fires, and out onto the shelf of grey rock in front of the boats, and the storm hit him full in the face.
It came in long grey sheets off the sea, lashing the cliffs and the rock and the upturned hulls of the boats with a fury that made the whole shelf shudder. The wind howled through the creeper at the cave-mouth and tore it loose in long green ribbons. The rain was warm. It ran over his face and into his eyes and down the back of his neck, and it tasted of salt and iron and the earth of the hill, and he stood with his arms out and his head tipped back and let it hit him, and did not think of anything at all.
He heard the children before he saw them.
They came scrambling out of the cave behind him, a ragged crowd of them, half-asleep and shrieking with laughter, their bare feet slapping on the wet rock. They had been woken by the noise, and they had come to see the rain, and when they saw it, they went wild. They ran out onto the shelf and threw their arms wide and spun in circles with their faces turned to the sky, shouting, catching the drops on their tongues, shrieking as the wind caught their thin cotton shifts and whipped them round their legs. The girl with the berries, from Siddhashrama, was not among them, but there was a girl of about seven with a cloth tied round her head who looked enough like her to make Ram's chest tighten, and he watched her dance on the rock with her hands held out, and her face was open and wild and entirely without fear.
Then the other children joined her, and the men came out of the caves, rubbing their eyes, and one by one they stood in the rain and laughed too. Kesh came out last, with a clay pipe in his mouth that had gone out, and he stood at the edge of the shelf and looked up at the storm with an expression Ram had never seen on his scarred face before. It was not joy exactly. It was closer to relief, the relief of a man who has been holding his breath under water and has finally come up into the air.
Lakshman came out after him. He stood beside Ram with his bound arm held against his chest and his face turned up to the storm, and the rain ran down his cheeks and mixed with something else that Ram did not look at.
"It's the cloud-wall," Lakshman said, over the roar of the wind. "Hanu said it would come in twelve days. He said the flyers can't fly through it. Not the big ones. Not Vashkar's."
"No."
"So for a month, nothing comes down the coast. No lights. No searchlights. No boats with men on them, hunting for the boats we're building." Lakshman turned his head and looked at him. His eyes were bright in the dark. "The sea-line shuts, brother. For a month. Like a door."
Ram stood in the rain and looked at the storm.
It was not a comfort, what the storm gave them. It was a kind of mercy, and mercy was never simple. It bought them a month of safety by taking away the sea and the sky and every means of seeing the far shore. It bought them a month of waiting, in a cave above the water, with the boats half-built and the food running low and the nights growing longer. It bought them a month in which a woman they loved was somewhere beyond the wall of cloud, behind glass, with a man who had sent a flyer to take her, and no way for anyone to reach her at all.
"A month," Ram said.
"Yes."
"And then we sail."
"And then we sail," said Lakshman. "If the boats hold."
Ram closed his eyes. The rain came down on his face. He thought of the glass towers in the north, and the panel under the rampart, and the woman singing in the vault. He thought of the child at Siddhashrama with her berries, and the cairn of grey stones on the summit of Rishyamuk, and the boy in the grey coverall who had walked away down the rocks without looking back.
And he thought, very clearly, for the first time in weeks: I am not going to rush this.
It was not a discipline. It was not the count. It was simply the knowledge, cold and quiet and certain, that if he went out across the sea now, in a half-built boat, with a dying king's sandals on his feet and his brother's bow across his back and a month of storm still piling up over the water, he would go the way he had gone up the hill: rushing, certain, sure of his own steadiness, and blind to the thing that would finally be the one to break him.
"Then we wait for the tide," he said quietly.
Lakshman nodded. He did not say anything else. He only put his good arm around his brother's shoulders, very briefly, and held on, and the rain came down on both of them and on the children dancing on the rock and on the boats lying upturned on the shelf, and the sea roared on beneath them in the dark.
---
The letter came with the second runner, six days into the storm.
It was not a runner from the north. It was a boy from the lower villages of the western coast, a thin silent creature of perhaps thirteen with a wet cloth wrapped around his head and a leather pouch hung from a cord at his neck. He had come on foot through the rain, along the cliff-paths, for four days. When he reached the cave-mouth he sank down on the rock and would not get up until Kesh had brought him a bowl of hot broth and a blanket. Then he unhooked the pouch from his neck with trembling fingers and held it out to Ram without a word.
Inside the pouch was a single folded sheet of thick grey paper, sealed with a plain blob of red wax. On the seal, pressed deep into the wax, was the mark of the house of Ikshvaku: the sun, the lotus, the sharp curved crescent of the old king's sigil. Ram had seen it on every decree of his father's reign.
He broke the seal.
The handwriting was not his father's. It was Bharat's. It was clean and young and hurried, the letters bunched together as though the writer had been fighting to get the words down before he could change his mind.
Brother,
I'm writing this from Nandigrama, in the room where the sandals used to sit. I hope you'll forgive me for the sandals. I know you didn't want them. I know you took them anyway.
Father died on the seventh day of the rains. Not the rains you're in. The rains here, where the river comes up over the steps and the old women say the sky has finally decided to cry for something. He went quietly. The physicians said it was his heart, but I don't think it was his heart. I think he simply stopped waiting for something that wasn't going to come.
He asked for you at the end. He asked whether you had kept your word. I told him yes. I didn't know if it was true, but I thought it was the only thing he wanted to hear, and I thought you would have wanted me to say it. You always did, didn't you? You never once asked anyone to tell you a lie.
They've burned him on the river-steps at Ayodhya, in the old way. The Board's men didn't stop us. Vasishtha said the stone allowed it. Sumant held the torch. Kaikeyi didn't come. I don't think she could.
The feeds say the Returning Prince has taken the Janasthan. They say you burned the ledger of the brokers and every stacked machine on the western rise, and that the coast is yours all the way to the sea-line. I know you didn't. I know you only walked away from a ford and a hill. But they put your face on the screens while they said it, the new one, with the bow in your hands, and the men in the towers say your name the way they used to say the old King's. They didn't say anything about Lakshman.
Ram, I don't know what's true any more. I thought I did. I thought I knew you. But the face on the spires is doing things I don't recognise, and the men in the towers are saying your name in a voice I can't stand to hear. So I'm writing this to ask you one thing, and then I'll stop. When it's over, when you've found her and brought her home, come back to Nandigrama. Not to Ayodhya. Not to the towers. Come and sit in the room with the sandals on the wall, and tell me what it was like. I'll listen. I'll keep the house warm, as I promised. I just want to know there's a brother left to come back to.
Your brother,
Bharat.
Ram read it twice. Then he folded it carefully along its creases and held it in his hand, and the rain dripped from the cave-mouth onto the paper and blurred the ink at one corner.
He did not weep.
He sat very still on the rock with the letter in his hand, and felt the grief come up through him like the swelling of the river in the monsoon, slow and cold and inevitable, rising past the places where the count had once held and into the empty space beneath his ribs. He thought of his father's hands in his lap, shaking. He thought of the sun and the lotus and the crescent on the seal, the old sigil his father had worn on his finger every morning of his life. He thought of a man who had walked barefoot to the source of the Sarayu, and had only put his sandals on at the end, so that the first thing he would feel at the holy place was the ground itself.
He asked whether you had kept your word.
Lakshman came out of the cave and stopped at the sight of him. He did not speak. He only crouched down on the rock in front of his brother and waited, with the rain running off his hair and his bound arm held close against his chest.
Ram held out the letter.
Lakshman took it. He read it slowly, with his lips moving, and when he reached the last line, he stopped and looked up, and his face was wet and very young.
"He's dead," Lakshman said.
"Yes."
"And you can't go."
"No."
"You can't go to the river-steps. You can't stand at the pyre. You can't tell him—" Lakshman's voice cracked. "You can't tell him anything at all. Because the stone says fourteen years, and the stone doesn't care whether the man who wrote it is alive to hear you say you're sorry."
"No," said Ram. "It doesn't."
Lakshman looked at the letter for a long time. Then he folded it, very carefully, and handed it back.
"He kept his word," he said quietly. "Didn't he? Right to the end. Even when it killed him."
"He did."
"And you're going to keep yours."
"Yes."
"Even though it's the only thing left in the world that he gave you and that nobody else can take away." Lakshman's voice was very low. "Brother, I don't know whether that's the bravest thing I've ever seen, or the most terrible."
Ram looked out at the sea. The storm had not let up. The waves came roaring in over the reef below the cliffs, white and grey and endless, and the spray rose up in long pale columns that hung in the air and dissolved into the rain.
"It's the thing he asked me for," said Ram. "It's the only thing I have left to give him."
He put the letter inside his tunic, against his chest, where the old king's sigil lay hidden under the cloth, and he did not take it out again that day.
That night, when the children were asleep and the men had gone to their beds in the caves, Kesh came to the cave-mouth with the clay pipe in his teeth and sat down beside Ram on the rock without a word. He smoked for a long time. The rain fell in front of them like a grey curtain.
"My grandmother used to tell a story about the dead," he said at last, around the stem of the pipe. "She said the dead don't leave the world all at once. They go out in stages, like a fire burning down. First the body. Then the voice. Then the name. The last thing to go is the promise. A promise outlives the man who made it, and it sits in the dark, waiting for someone to pick it up."
"Do you believe that?"
"No," said Kesh. "But it's a good story. And the boats need a name, and I haven't thought of one yet."
Ram did not answer. He looked out at the rain.
"Call them after the king," he said quietly. "The one in the letter."
Kesh took the pipe out of his mouth. He turned it over in his big black fingers and looked at it for a long time.
"Dasharatha," he said slowly, testing the sound of it. "It's a good name for a boat. Heavy. Hard to sink."
"Yes."
"And the other ten?"
"Give them the names of the people who were promised something and never got it," said Ram. "The ones on the hill. The ones in the caves. The boy at the ford who came back with a ledger and didn't know what it was for."
Kesh nodded. He put the pipe back in his teeth.
"That's a lot of names," he said.
"I know."
"You'll never remember them all."
"I know that too," said Ram. "I'll try anyway."
---
Shadow IV: The Sea Road
> **[SHADOW ROAD]** *The sea-line, the offer that was taken, and the dawn after.*
The engines did not want to go south. Ram had known that since the morning the ravine was burned. He had felt it in the pulse, in the faint reluctant drag that came up through the panel every time he laid his palm on it and asked them to turn. They were machines built to stand on a rampart and face inward, toward the city, toward the spires and the one house that could give them a word. They had never been asked to walk. They had never been asked to cross a burnt country on flatbed trucks, with the ash coming up in grey clouds from the wheels and the smell of scorched thorn hanging over every mile of the road.
But they went. They went because he asked, and because the house had always answered, and because there was nothing left in the north that could make him stop.
The column stretched for nearly six miles. It was made of the vanguard's survivors, a few hundred men with their heads bowed and their rifles slung, and a long procession of flatbeds loaded with the black barrels of the engines, each one chained down and hooded in tarpaulin against the dust. At the head of the column rode the command-truck, a battered grey hulk with its cab windows blacked out and its roof-mounted aerials bent by the blast. Ram sat in the cab. He did not look out of the windows. He sat with his hands flat on his knees and his back straight against the seat, and every now and then he felt the thread of the pulse tug at his wrist, and he did not let go.
Lakshman rode in the truck behind.
He did not ride with his brother. Ram had ordered it so. He had said the men needed to see their commander at the front of the column, in the old manner, and that the commander's body would ride with honour until they reached the sea. He had said it in the flat reasonable tone he had learned to use with the staff, and nobody had argued. They had lashed the body in a long grey shroud of heavy canvas to a stretcher of salvaged steel, and set it in the back of the truck behind the engines, with the broken black bow laid across the shroud where a man's hands would have been.
Ram had not looked at it since the Ledger. Every time he thought of it, the pulse in his wrist jumped and steadied, jumped and steadied, like a thing trying to escape a net.
He did not count. He had stopped counting on the night the ravine burned. The numbers came anyway, the way the ash came up off the road: four hundred and twelve dead in the cutting, two hundred and six wounded, forty not expected to live. The feeds had printed them in neat grey columns beside a photograph of the Victor of the Western Rise, standing on the rim of the Ledger with the morning sun behind him, his hands spread wide, his face calm and certain and far away.
He had looked at that photograph a hundred times. He had not once felt like the man in it.
---
The city's reports came by relay every six hours, carried south along the old rail embankment by a string of signallers. The staff read them in the command-truck in low careful voices, and Ram listened without speaking.
The stripping had stopped in every district from the Sarayu to the Dandaka boundary. The brokers' accounts were frozen. The Board's council sat in the basement of its dissolved offices under guard, and its men had begun, one by one, to confess before the feeds to the things they had signed. The feeds broadcast every confession in full. The old clerks wept on camera, and the crowds in the square chanted over their sobs, and the chant was always the same name. The lower wards were quiet. The children had been sent back to the new schoolhouses on the Sarayu bank, where they learned to read the decrees, and the decrees said that the forest was the house's to keep, and that the house would answer for it.
There was no mention of the hanging in the lower ward. Ram had ordered that. He had thought, when he gave the order, that it was a kindness.
There was no mention of the vault either. There was a single line in the last report, between an agricultural bulletin and a list of tram-times: The queen remains in the protective custody of the house. Her condition is stable. She has asked for nothing.
Ram read the line twice. Then he asked for the next sheet.
He thought about the engines. He thought about the long black barrels turning on the north rampart the morning the stripping stopped, swinging inward with the grave patience of machines that had waited a long time to be told what to face. He thought of the pulse rising through the glass, clean and unhesitating, and the way the screen had said HOUSE CONFIRMED, as though it had been relieved to hear the answer.
The house will answer for it.
He had said that to Sumant in the corridor. He still meant it. Somebody had to. The forest could not come up the stairs and ask.
He only wished, sometimes, in the long hours of the road, that someone would come up the stairs and tell him what the answer was, so that he would not have to be the one who gave it.
---
They reached the sea-line on the sixth day.
The land ended before the sea did. It fell away in low grey terraces, stepping down through dead scrub and salt-crusted flats to a tidal plain that stretched for miles, cracked and white and pocked with shallow pools that caught the light like broken glass. Beyond the plain the water lay flat and pale under a low sky the colour of old pewter. It did not look like the sea from the feeds. It looked like the end of something.
The column halted on the last terrace, and the engines were unhooded.
Ram got out of the truck. The air was hot and thick and smelled of salt and rot and something sharp and metallic. He walked to the edge of the terrace and looked out, and for a long time he did not move.
There were lights on the water.
They were far out, perhaps twenty miles, perhaps more. They hung in a long pale line along the horizon like pearls laid on dark velvet, and they did not flicker. They burned with a steady, unbroken light, and beneath them a darker shape rose from the sea, long and low, crowned with thin silver spires that had been built by men who wanted to be seen from a great distance.
Lanka, said a staff officer at his elbow, very softly, with an expression of pure, unguarded awe.
"It's an island," said Ram.
"Yes, my lord. But it's the only one that has ever said no to the house."
Ram looked at the lights. They did not move. The feeds had been saying so for ten years in their careful reverent voices: the Lord of the southern island kept his lights burning every night, so the whole coast would know he was there. He kept his towers high and his wire thick and his machines pointed at the shore. He kept a silence in the water that no ship had crossed without permission.
He had never seen the island. He knew it as a light on a horizon and a rumour the city had been whispering since the first week of the stripping. It seemed to him now that the whole country had been looking at that light for a long time, waiting for someone to walk down to the water and ask what it was for.
He did not feel anything except a cold, distant clarity, like the feeling of standing at the top of a long stair and seeing, at the bottom, the thing he had been climbing toward.
---
Tavish came across the tidal plain at dusk, under a white flag, on foot.
He came alone, picking his way between the pools with the careful unhurried steps of a man who has been told he may be shot and has decided not to give anyone the satisfaction of seeing him run. He was tall and slender, in a long grey coat of expensive weave already stained with salt. His face was narrow and pale, with fine dark eyes set deep under a heavy brow, and his jaw had not been shaved in a week. He stopped at the foot of the terrace and looked up at the line of engines, and then at the figure at the top of the slope, and his expression did not change at all.
"You're the one who burned the Ledger," he said. His voice was low and clear, with the flat vowels of the southern islands in it.
"I'm the one who ended it," said Ram.
Tavish smiled faintly. "A distinction my brother never bothered to make. He thought wanting a thing and doing it were only different words for the same appetite."
"Is your brother the Lord of the island?"
"My brother is the Lord of everything that can be seen from the top of his tower," said Tavish. "On a clear night, that's a good deal of coast." He glanced at the lights. "He's been watching your engines for three days. He says they're beautiful. He would like to know whether you can make them stop."
"Why would he care?"
"Because a man who can make engines stop can also make them start, and my brother has spent ten years learning exactly how much a machine that answers to a single heartbeat is worth." Tavish's eyes moved over the rows of barrels, the hooded flatbeds, the squat grey commander's truck. "He also thinks you've become a very dangerous kind of man. The kind who can turn a face into a country, and then sit on the hill while the country hangs its clerks. He says he recognises the type. He has one in his own tower, sitting on the highest floor."
Ram said nothing.
Tavish came up a step. The staff officers shifted on the flatbeds. He stopped halfway up the slope, where the light of the lamps on the lower terraces could reach his face.
"Listen to me," he said quietly. "My brother is going to die, not this year and maybe not the next, but he's rotting in that tower with his wire and his machines, and every man who serves him knows it. I'm the one who'll be holding the keys when he does. I've waited ten years for the chance, and I'm not going to waste it on a sentimental fool. So I'll make you an offer, and you'll take it, because you're the only one on this coast who can make it worth taking."
"What offer?"
"The keys," said Tavish. "The engines on the island, the wire on the shore, the lock on the lower tower where the men keep the records. I'll give you the codes, and the pulse that opens them. In exchange for the throne of Lanka. Not the island's kingdom. The throne. You'll let me sit in it, with your engines on the shore, pointed at the sea and never at my people. And when the tower falls, you'll put your word in writing and carve it in the stone at the water's edge, so the coast can read it."
Ram looked at him for a long time.
He could feel the pulse beneath his skin. It was steady, as it had been since the morning he came down the north rampart with the ash in his lungs. It had turned the engines inward and burned the towers dark and sent the stripping rigs to the fire. It had never once asked him whether he was right.
He thought: This man is offering me the one thing I have been missing. The keys to the island. A quick, clean end to a war that has been running for ten years. Every life the engines could save, if they were pointed at the right tower.
He thought: And a man who sells his brother for a throne will sell the throne the moment someone offers him a better price. I can use that. I can use him until the keys are in my hand. And then I'll decide what he is worth.
"You'll have your throne," said Ram.
Tavish's face did not move. But his eyes narrowed, very slightly, the way a man's eyes narrow when he has heard a thing he expected and has not quite believed it.
"On what terms?" he asked.
"On the terms of the house," said Ram. "You'll bring me the codes tonight, under the flag, to this terrace. You'll bring the pulse-keys in your own hand, and you'll give them to my engine-crew, and you'll kneel while they're tested. Then you'll stand up, and the house will decide whether you're fit to be king."
"And if I refuse?"
"Then you'll be the one who doesn't stand up," said Ram quietly. "The engines are pointed at the island already. They don't need your keys to see the lights."
Tavish said nothing for a long moment. Then, very slowly, he inclined his head. It was not a bow. It was the careful, measured nod of a man who has just seen the cost of a bargain and has decided to pay it anyway.
"You're more like your father than the feeds let on," he said softly. "He used to make offers like that too. Always with a knife in the other hand, and always with a smile."
He turned and walked back down the terrace, out across the salt, toward the lights.
Ram watched him go. The lamps on the flatbeds threw long shadows across the cracked white ground, and the sea came in over the flats, slow and steady, and somewhere far back on the terrace a truck door creaked on its hinges in the wind and then fell still.
---
The codes came at midnight.
Tavish brought them himself, under a white flag, on foot, and he knelt at the edge of the water as he had promised, while the crew-chief tested the sequence on a small black handset and the blue light of the screen played over his pale face. He did not look at Ram. He kept his eyes on the sea.
The engines on the island woke.
Ram felt them through the ground. It was not a sound. It was a shift in the pulse, a long slow tremor that rolled across the water from the lights and crawled up through the salt and into the soles of his feet, and then the lamps on the far spires flickered, one after another, and went out, and the whole line of lights along the horizon seemed to draw in on itself like a breath held too long.
Then they came back. Brighter. Hotter. The colour of molten gold.
"It's done, my lord," said the crew-chief, very quietly. His voice had gone thin. "The island's answering. Every tower. Every wire. It's listening for you."
Tavish looked up from the water. His face was grey.
"Stand," said Ram.
Tavish stood. He did it slowly, his knees cracking on the wet stone, and he stood at the edge of the water with his shoulders back and his head high, the way a man stands when he believes he is about to be given something he has wanted all his life.
Ram walked down the terrace to meet him.
He did not hurry. He stopped an arm's length away and looked at Tavish's face, at the fine dark eyes and the set mouth and the faint, hopeful, dangerous light that had come into them now that the keys were in the house's hands and the engines had turned gold.
"You did well," said Ram.
Tavish's mouth opened. "My lord—"
"You'll have your throne," said Ram. "In the morning. When the tower is quiet and the wire is down and the men who serve your brother have been told what has happened to him. Until then, you'll stay on this terrace, under the guard, where I can see you."
"That's not—"
"That's the terms of the house."
Something moved in Tavish's face, quick and sharp, the flash of a man who has just understood the shape of a trap and has no time to get out of it. He took a step back. The guards closed in.
"My brother warned me about you," he said hoarsely. "He said you'd make me kneel and then you'd hang me from the highest spire, and call it mercy."
"Your brother was a fool," said Ram calmly. "He thought mercy was a thing that could be given away. I know better. It is a thing that is taken, every time, from someone who has no way of refusing."
He turned his back on Tavish and walked up the terrace, and did not look round when the man began to shout.
---
At dawn the engines fired.
They fired once, long and slow, a single enormous roar that rolled across the sea like the sound of a mountain splitting in half. The black barrels spat fire into the grey morning, and the shells went up in long shrieking arcs over the water, and they came down on the lower towers of the island with a blast so vast that the ground shook under Ram's feet on the far terrace. Glass exploded from the spires in glittering sheets. The wire on the shore went up in a blaze of white light. A black column of smoke rose from the island's heart and spread across the sky, and beneath it, very faintly, through the crackling roar of the fire, Ram heard men screaming.
He stood on the terrace and watched it burn.
He did not feel the thing he had expected to feel. He had thought, in the long hours of the night, that he might feel grief, or rage, or the cold sick weight of a choice made and irrevocable. He felt nothing like that. He felt clear. He felt the pulse in his wrist beat steadily under the skin, slow and heavy, and he thought, quite calmly, that the island would be quiet by noon, and the codes would be his, and the Lord of the southern island, whose name he had not yet been told, would be a dead man in a tower of broken glass before the sun was high.
Behind him, on the flatbeds, the staff officers had begun to murmur. One of them was crying. Another was praying under his breath, in a language Ram did not know.
Ram did not turn round.
He walked back along the terrace, slowly, past the long black barrels and the hooded trucks and the rows of tired men with their faces grey and blank. The sun was rising over the water, pale and cold, and its light fell across the salt flats in long bright bars, and at the top of the slope, where the grey commander's truck stood with its doors shut and its engine silent, he stopped.
He did not open the door. He stood beside it with his palm flat against the cold steel, and he listened to the sea come in over the flats, slow and steady, a sound that had been coming in over this shore for longer than there had been kings to make oaths about it.
Inside the truck, under the canvas shroud, his brother lay with a broken bow across his chest.
Ram leaned his forehead against the steel, very gently, the way a man leans against a door that he knows he will never be able to open again, and he closed his eyes.
He did not count. He did not need to. The pulse was counting for him. It was steady and clean and patient, and it had been waiting, all along, for exactly this kind of morning, when the answer was so simple that no one would ever think to ask what the question had been.
Behind him, out on the water, the island burned.
And somewhere far to the north, in a basement under the dissolved offices of a board that no longer existed, a woman in a vault put down a song she had been humming for days, and listened, and did not sing again.

CHAPTER : SARGA V: The Singing in the Tower
> **[THE ROAD TAKEN]**
Kanti was the oldest woman in the caves, and she had decided, some time in her eighties, that the dead were owed a story.
She told it on the ninth night of the storm, when the rain had settled into a steady grey drumming against the cliffs and the cave was full of wet wool and woodsmoke and the smell of boiled rice. She sat at the back of the largest chamber on a flat stone with a bundle of driftwood at her feet and a clay lamp in her lap, and she did not look up as the people came in. They came in silently, one by one, from the shelf and the boats and the cook-fires, and they sat on the damp floor in a wide half-circle around her, and the children sat in front, with their knees pulled up under their chins, and nobody asked why.
Ram sat near the back. Lakshman sat beside him with his bound arm across his lap. Hanu had come in last, dripping wet from a swim in the lagoon below the cliffs, and he sat on the rock by the entrance with a towel around his shoulders and his long quick face unusually still. Kesh did not come. He was in the boat-shed, working on the rudder of the Dasharatha by the light of a single lamp, and his hammer could be heard faintly through the rain.
"It's for the king," Kanti said, when everyone had settled. Her voice was thin and dry, but it carried. "The king in the letter. He's gone to the river-steps. He walked a long way to get there, and it's a long way back, and he'll need something to carry him."
She lifted the lamp, and the light fell across her face, which was brown and creased and as fine-boned as a bird's.
"In the old days," she said, "before the Board and the stripping and the feeds, there was a king who had sixty thousand sons."
A murmur went round the children. One of the girls giggled, and was hushed.
"He had sixty thousand sons," Kanti repeated, "and he had a horse. A sacrifice-horse. He meant to ride it round the whole world, to show that everything under the sky was his, and to prove it by sacrificing the horse at the end. So he let it loose, and it went wandering, the way horses do, and the sons went looking for it, and they went looking in every field and every forest and every river, and they didn't find it, because it was not in any of those places."
Kanti's hands moved on the lamp, very slowly.
"Where was it?"
"Under the earth," said one of the older boys, impatiently. "Everybody knows that. It was in the underworld, tied to the pillar of a sage who had stolen it."
"Yes," said Kanti, unperturbed. "Everybody knows. But the sons didn't. So they dug. They dug down through the field, and down through the forest, and down through the rock below the rivers, and every time they struck something hard they dug round it, and every time they came to something soft they dug through it, until the whole land was pitted and split like the dry bed of a pond in summer. And the earth began to crack."
The rain drummed on the cliffs. Somewhere near the boat-shed the hammer had stopped.
"And down in the dark," Kanti said, softer now, "the four beasts woke up."
The children leaned forward.
"There are four beasts," said Kanti. "They hold up the quarters of the world. They stand at the edges of everything, where the sky meets the earth, and they keep the two from falling into each other. The beast of the east is white, and his tusks are the colour of the moon. The beast of the south is red as blood, and he breathes fire when he's angry. The beast of the west is black as a storm, and his voice is the sound of the sea. And the beast of the north is grey as an old man's hair, and he never sleeps, because he's the one who remembers everything that has ever been promised."
She stopped. The lamp flickered in her hands.
"And when the sons dug too deep," Kanti went on, "the earth began to slide. The eastern beast felt it first. He shifted his feet, and the white dust came down from the sky like snow. The southern beast felt it next, and he roared, and the fire went up over the forest and the hills went dark with smoke. The western beast shook his head, and the sea came up in a wall over the coast. And the northern beast, who remembered everything, looked down through the cracks in the world at the sixty thousand sons, digging in the dark for a horse that was never there, and he said—"
"What did he say?" the children whispered.
Kanti smiled. It was not a happy smile, but it was not cruel either.
"He said, Stop digging. You've taken the wrong thing from the wrong place, and now the world is falling into the hole you made. You can't dig your way out of a promise that was broken before you were born. You can only hold up the sky, and wait, and hope someone remembers what it was for."
The cave was very quiet.
"The sons didn't listen," said Kanti. "They never do. They kept digging, and the earth kept cracking, and the four beasts kept holding up the quarters of the world with their backs bent and their legs trembling. And that's why the rivers run the wrong way in the monsoon, and why the sea comes up over the land in the dry season, and why a man who wants to be king of everything never once asks what the beasts are standing on."
She set the lamp down on the rock beside her.
"That's the story," she said. "For the king who's gone down to the river-steps. He'll want to know that somebody is holding up the sky, even when everyone else has stopped digging."
For a long moment nobody moved. Then one of the girls at the front, the one who had giggled earlier, reached out and touched the old woman's knee.
"Is it true?" she asked.
Kanti looked at her, and her creased face was very gentle.
"It's true for the people who needed it," she said. "That's the only kind of true there is, child. The rest is just the sons with their shovels."
---
Ram did not sleep that night. He lay on the damp reed-bed at the back of the cave, listening to the rain on the rock and the breathing of the children in their heaps against the wall, and he thought about the four beasts.
Somebody is holding up the sky.
He thought about the sixty thousand sons, digging for a horse that was not there. He thought about the men in the Board's towers, counting the stripping permits in their glass offices. He thought about the old king on the river-steps, and the sun-and-lotus seal on the grey paper, and the words he asked whether you had kept your word. He thought about Vaal's cold pale eyes staring up at the wind, and the cairn of stones, and a hand on a rifle-scope that had been paid for by someone who had never once had to stand in the sun.
He thought about Sita.
He had not let himself think about her properly since the siding. He had thought about her in pieces, the way you carry a broken bowl in both hands and try not to look at the cracks: the grey-white cotton, the plait coming loose, the set of her mouth as she said go. The sound of her voice on the bed at Mithila, asking him what he would do when his word cost someone else. The look on her face when the flyer's door slammed shut.
He had not once imagined her in a tower.
He tried now. He tried to see her on the highest floor of a building of glass and wire, a thousand miles of sea away from the nearest shore, with the Lord of the southern island's men at the doors and his machines turned toward the water. He could not see it. He could only see the vault, and the grille no wider than a hand, and the face beyond it, very calm, very thin, as she stood with her back to him and told him that he had been paying for everything with other people's lives.
The cave dripped. The rain fell.
He did not know how long he lay there before he heard Hanu's voice at the cave-mouth, low and urgent, calling his name.
---
Hanu had been swimming.
He had gone out in the storm three nights before, alone, with a waterproof sack of dried fish and a coil of fine wire round his waist, and had struck south along the cliffs under the cover of the cloud-wall until he reached the reef. The reef ran for two miles beneath the sea-line, a long broken spine of black coral and rotten stone, and beyond it lay the lagoon, and beyond the lagoon the island itself. He had gone over the reef at low tide, with the waves breaking over his back and the rocks tearing at his palms, and he had come out on the far side in the dark, and found a wreck.
It was a fishing-boat, two hundred yards from the island's eastern shore, lying on its side in the shallows with its hull split open and its mast snapped off at the base. Hanu had climbed up through the wreck and found a sealed compartment in the bow, dry and intact, with a wireless set still working on a battery that had not quite died.
"I heard them," he said now, crouching beside Ram at the back of the cave with his teeth chattering and his hair plastered to his skull. "The island's men. They've got their own channel. Fishermen's talk, mostly, with the old word-codes from the coast. But there was a voice on it, late at night, very faint, a man saying the Lord had ordered the lower towers sealed. And the next voice said the Lord's tower was lit, and the lights had been up for three nights, and the furrow-girl was singing in the high room."
Ram's breath stopped.
"Singing," he said.
"Singing." Hanu's eyes were steady in the dark. "I went up the wall of the lagoon, after. The island's a rock with a tower on top of it, and the tower's got a thousand windows, and most of them are dark. But on the top floor there was a single lamp burning behind a grille, and the lamp was moving, the way a lamp moves when someone is walking back and forth in front of it. And at night, when the wind came round from the south, I could hear it. A woman's voice. Low. Not a hymn. Something quiet, like a song you hum while you're doing a job with your hands."
Lakshman had come up behind them. He did not speak. He only crouched on the wet rock beside his brother and put his good hand flat on Ram's back, very lightly, the way a man steadies a boat in a swell.
"Is it her?" he whispered.
"I don't know," said Hanu. "I've never heard her sing. But the voice knew the song. Or at least, it knew how to sing it without stopping in the middle, which is more than most people can manage in a tower full of men with guns." He grinned suddenly, a flash of white in the dark. "And when I came back down to the water, I saw a flyer on the landing-pad. Black. With gold filigree. The same as the one that took her from the siding, I'd bet my ring on it."
"Your ring," said Ram.
"Well. It's the only thing I've got that's worth anything." Hanu's grin faded. "What did you bring me back across the reef, prince? A message? A promise?"
"A letter," said Ram. "Two of them. One for a man who's dead. One for a man who isn't."
Hanu's face changed. He looked at the sealed grey paper in Ram's hand for a long moment without speaking. Then he took it, very gently, and held it up to the lamplight, and read the sigil, and his mouth tightened.
"The house of Ikshvaku," he said softly. "I thought the Board had burned all the seals."
"They tried," said Ram.
---
The storm broke at the end of the second month.
It did not end gently. It went out the way it had come in, in one great rolling roar that tore the cloud-wall apart and sent the rain sheeting sideways off the cliffs in long grey ropes. The wind dropped in a single shuddering gust, and the surf that had pounded the shore for sixty days fell silent, and in that silence, very faint, Ram heard the birds.
He went out onto the shelf at dawn. The sky was clean. It was a pale washed blue that stretched from the cliffs to the horizon without a single cloud, and the sea beneath it was flat and glassy and green, the colour of a bottle held up to the sun. The air smelled of salt and wet stone and a sharp new green from the hillside above the caves, where the thorn-bushes had already put out fresh shoots, pale and tender as the first milk of a child.
Below him, on the shelf of grey rock, eleven boats lay in a neat row with their hulls sealed and their ribs new and their sails bent on, patched with canvas from the cave's old stores. They were not beautiful. They were not even very good. But Kesh had walked the length of the row that morning with his pipe in his teeth and his big black hands in his pockets, and he had stopped at the first of them, the one with DASHARATHA painted on its bow in careful, faded, half-correct letters, and he had said, to nobody in particular, that she would float.
Ram went down the shelf to her.
The boat was low and broad, with a flat square stern and a high curved bow, and it smelled of tar and new pitch. Kesh had painted the name in red. Beneath it, in smaller black letters, someone had added the names of the other ten, in a list that ran down the strakes of the hull like the entries of a ledger. Ram read them one by one, slowly, with his hand flat against the warm wood.
The Ford. The Hill. The Caves. The Ledger. The Siding. The Gully. The Ravine. The Salt. The Lake. The Ghat.
Then, at the very bottom, in letters so small they were almost hidden under the paint, a single line: The Girl Who Was Found.
Ram took his hand away.
"That's the one you're going in," said Kesh, from behind him. He had come down the shelf without a sound, and was standing at Ram's shoulder with his pipe cold between his teeth, looking at the boat with an expression of rough, unhurried satisfaction. "The lead boat. You and your brother. And the boy with the bow, if he's still willing to climb into a wet hull with his arm in a sling."
"He'll come," said Ram.
"Good. Because I don't trust the others to find the lagoon without a fool in front of them, and the fool's going to be you."
"And the woman? The one in the tower?"
Kesh took the pipe from his mouth and looked out across the glassy green water toward the horizon, where the faint dark shape of the island was just visible under the rising sun.
"She'll be there," he said. "Or she won't. But if she is, and she's singing, then someone on that rock is still listening for a tune. Maybe that's enough to keep a woman alive in a tower for two months. Maybe it's the only thing that does." He put the pipe back in his teeth. "I've known a few people who lived on singing, prince. Not many. But they were the ones who didn't die."
---
Ram wrote the reply that afternoon, sitting on the shelf in the sun with the grey paper on his knee and a stub of charcoal in his fingers.
He wrote slowly. He did not try to make the words beautiful. He wrote the things he could say plainly, and left out the rest. He wrote that the storm had broken. He wrote that the boats were ready. He wrote that the old king had been right about the sky, and wrong about the sons, and that Ram had come to understand, in the long months of the road, that the two were not so far apart as men liked to pretend.
He wrote: I have kept my word. I will go on keeping it, as long as there is breath in me, and whatever it costs. Keep the house warm. Keep the room where the sandals sit. When it is time, I will come back to Nandigrama, and I will sit in that room, and I will tell you what it was like. I will tell you everything. You have a right to it. You are the only one left who has ever asked me for the truth without wanting something in return.
He signed it with his name, not his title.
He folded it, and wrapped it in oilcloth, and gave it to the thin silent boy from the western villages, who had stayed on at the caves through the storm, sleeping in the corner of the boat-shed with his wet cloth still wrapped round his head. The boy took it without a word. He tucked it into the leather pouch at his throat, and then he looked up at Ram with his dark eyes, and for the first time since he had come through the rain, he smiled.
"I'll take it north," he said. "Through the cliffs, along the old salt road. It'll be quicker by the coast." He hesitated. "Is it true? What the feeds say about the Janasthan?"
"No," said Ram. "It isn't."
"I didn't think it was. You don't look like a man who burns things." The boy tilted his head, considering. "You look like a man who holds them up, so they don't fall."
Ram did not answer. He watched the boy go, up the cliff-path in the late sun, a small thin figure against the blue, and he did not move until the figure had vanished over the top.
---
Lakshman found him there, sitting on the shelf in front of the boat with his feet in the shallows and the sun on his back.
He did not sit down. He stood a little way off, with his bow across his shoulder and his arm out of its sling for the first time in two months, and he looked at the boat and the painted names, and the color of his face changed as he read them.
"Ghat," he said, very quietly. "You put the ghat on there."
"Yes."
"And the ravine."
"Yes."
Lakshman was silent for a long time. Then he came and sat down beside his brother on the warm rock, carefully, the way a man sits when he is not yet sure his body will do what he asks.
"You never told me you'd named them," he said.
"Kesh did. I only gave him the list."
"What's the last one?"
Ram looked at the sea. The water was very still.
"The Girl Who Was Found," he said.
Lakshman's breath caught. He looked away.
"You think she's still alive," he said at last, to the water.
"I think someone is singing in a tower," said Ram. "And I think the one who's singing has been waiting a long time for somebody to answer."
Lakshman was quiet for a while. Then he said, without looking at Ram, very low, in the voice he had used on the road from the palace gate: "I'm frightened, brother."
"I know."
"Not of the sea. Not of the tower, or the wire, or the Lord, or whatever the Lord has got in his cellars. I'm frightened of what happens if we get there and she isn't singing any more. If the tower is quiet and the lamp is out and nobody is left on the top floor but a pile of ash and a broken bow." His good hand closed on his knee, tight. "I don't think I could stand that. I don't think I'd be able to stop."
Ram did not answer right away. He looked at the boat, and the red letters on its bow, and the list of names running down its hull like the entries of a ledger.
"Then don't stop," he said quietly. "Keep singing. You don't have to be good at it. Just don't stop until somebody tells you to."
Lakshman laughed. It was short and broken and almost a sob, and it went on for a little too long, and when it stopped he wiped his face on his sleeve and looked at his brother for the first time since the morning of the storm.
"You sound like a fisherwoman," he said.
"I've been spending time with them."
"Yes," said Lakshman, and something in his face eased. "I can see that."
He stood up, and held out his good hand, and Ram took it, and let himself be pulled to his feet. They stood together on the shelf in the sun, with the boats lined up behind them and the sea spread out flat and green in front, and the faint dark shape of the island rising on the horizon like a thing that had been waiting for them since the beginning of time.
"Four, two, six," said Lakshman, under his breath.
Ram looked at him in surprise.
"You taught me that," said his brother. "On the road, under the tamarind. You told me it wasn't a trick. I thought you were a fool." He smiled, faintly. "I still do. But I've been doing it every morning since the ravine, when I wake up in the dark and can't remember where I am. It helps. Not much. But enough to get out of bed."
"It's not a trick," said Ram.
"No," said Lakshman. "I can see that now too."
---
CHAPTER : SARGA VI: The Crossing
> **[THE ROAD TAKEN]**
They launched on the first morning of the dry season, on a tide so low that the reef stood bare above the water for a mile in every direction, black and glistening and crusted with the pale shells of things that had died in the storm.
The eleven boats went out in a line. Kesh led them from the lead boat, the Dasharatha, with the tiller under his big black hands and his pipe clenched in his teeth and his face set in an expression of such grim, absorbed concentration that Ram did not dare speak to him. Lakshman sat in the bow with his bow across his knees and his good hand on the gunwale, watching the water ahead as though it might try to stand up and bite him. The sails were bent on and drawing, and the wind came in soft and steady from the north-east, and the green sea parted under the bows in long clean ridges that ran back along the hull like the grain of wood.
Ram sat in the stern beside Kesh, where he could see the whole line of boats strung out behind them across the glittering water, eleven small dark shapes against the blue. He did not count them. He knew how many there were. He had watched them load at the shelf that morning, one by one, with the staff and the fishermen and the thin silent boy from the western villages, who had come back up the cliff-path two days before with a sealed letter from the north and had insisted, very politely, on coming with them.
The letter was in Ram's tunic, against his chest. He had not yet opened it. He knew what it would say, and he did not think he could bear to read it aloud.
Hanu was not in any of the boats.
He had gone the night before, in the dark, alone. He had gone over the reef at the lowest tide of the month, with the iron ring on a cord round his neck and the coil of fine wire around his waist and a waterproof sack of dried fish slung across his back, and by dawn he had been gone for six hours, and nobody had seen him go. Kesh had said he would be back by noon. Lakshman had said that Hanu had been born in a tree and would likely be found in the Lord's own bedchamber, eating the Lord's breakfast. Ram had said nothing. He had watched the reef in the grey light before dawn, and had seen, very faint against the black rock, a shape moving out toward the island in the shallows, quick and light and utterly silent, and then the shape had gone under the water, and had not come up.
He had taken the ring from his own finger the night before.
It was an old thing, plain and heavy and dark with age. It was iron, not gold, and the sigil cut into its face had been worn almost smooth by years of his mother's thumb. He had worn it since the day she gave it to him, on the morning of his seventh birthday, in the women's wing of the palace with the lamps still lit and her face soft and tired in the early light. The house does not need gold, she had told him. It needs something that won't be taken from the hand by a clerk.
He had held it out to Hanu on the shelf in the dusk, and Hanu had looked at it for a long moment without taking it.
"That's the house's seal," Hanu had said quietly. "Your mother's, isn't it? The one the old women used to say could open any door in Ayodhya."
"It can't open anything," said Ram. "It's only iron. It's the only thing I have that she gave me with her own hand."
"Then why give it to me?"
"Because she'll know it," said Ram. "Sita. She's the only one who ever saw my mother wear it. If you put it in her palm, she'll know it was me who sent you, and she'll know it wasn't a trick, and she'll know there are men on the water who are coming for her."
"And if the Lord's men find me first?"
"Then they'll have the ring," said Ram. "And you'll have swum past them. And that will still be enough."
Hanu had taken it then, very slowly, the way a man takes something that might burn him. He had closed his fist around it and looked at Ram across the shelf, and his face in the fading light had been young and wry and entirely without humour.
"You know what they'll say about this," he had said. "In the wards, in a hundred years. They'll say the house gave its seal to a monkey-boy from the Janasthan, and sent him across the sea to fetch the king's wife."
"Let them say it," said Ram. "It'll be true."
---
The island grew as they sailed.
It rose out of the sea in slow stages, like a thing surfacing from a long sleep. First the spires, thin and silver, catching the sun on their tips and throwing back a faint glitter across the water. Then the towers beneath them, grey and vast and windowless at their bases, with rows of dark slots running up their flanks like the gills of some enormous fish. Then the wire: a long low fence of black filament strung along the shore, humming faintly in the wind, and the dull steel gleam of the machines that crouched behind it, their barrels trained out across the water with a patient, deadly attention.
Ram had seen the machines before. He had seen them in the feeds, and in the old control room beneath the north rampart, and in the long grey line of barrels that had turned toward the island in the Shadow. He had not seen them like this, in the flat clean light of morning, with the sea breathing softly against the reef and the gulls wheeling over the water in long white loops.
They were beautiful. That was the worst of it.
"Don't look at them too long," said Kesh, from the tiller. His voice was low. "They'll think you're asking to be shot."
"Are they watching us?"
"They've been watching us since the reef. Since before that, probably. The Lord's men don't sleep. They just sit up in their towers with their pulses on the glass and wait for something to happen." He spat over the side. "Our luck is that they've been waiting ten years for something to happen, and nothing has, and they've started to believe it never will."
"And now?"
"Now we're the thing that's happening." Kesh's teeth clenched on the pipe-stem. "Hold on to something, prince. The reef's about to open."
It did. The line of black rock that had lain across the water for two miles seemed to split in the middle, like a gate swinging on its hinges. A narrow channel appeared between the two halves, glassy and green and perfectly still, and the sea poured through it in a long smooth tongue that carried the eleven boats forward at the speed of a running man. The Dasharatha lurched, and shuddered, and then leaped forward as though the water had reached up and taken hold of her keel. Ram grabbed the gunwale. Behind him the other boats came through the channel one after another, rising and falling in the swell, their sails bellied out like the wings of great white birds.
Lakshman was standing in the bow, bracing himself against the mast with his bow in his hand and his face turned toward the island. His hair was streaming in the wind. His eyes were wide and blazing and utterly without fear.
"There!" he shouted over the roar of the water. "On the lagoon wall! Look!"
Ram looked.
On the far side of the lagoon, where the island's black cliffs rose sheer out of the water, a single figure came swimming out across the green water toward the boats. He was lean and quick and did not look back. He came up through the waves with his hair plastered to his skull and his face streaming with salt, and he was laughing, a wild exhilarated bark that rang off the cliffs. When he came alongside, he flung one arm up over the gunwale and hung there, dripping, grinning up at Ram with the whole of his face.
"Well," said Hanu. "That went better than I expected."
"Where's the ring?" asked Ram.
Hanu touched the cord at his throat. The iron seal swung out into the sunlight. "Still with me. There's a grille at the top of the tower, and the glass behind it is too thick for a ring, and the men on the stair don't sleep as soundly as I'd hoped. But I saw her." His grin softened, very slightly. "Through the grille. She looked at me as if I'd come out of the sea to haunt her, and then she laughed, very quietly, so the men wouldn't hear. And she said, Tell him I'm still counting."
Ram did not move.
He sat in the stern of the Dasharatha, with the sea roaring round the hull and the sun on his face and the island rising in front of him, grey and gold and terrible, and he felt the thing in his chest that had been holding still since the ravine give way, all at once, like the river breaking its banks in the first hour of the monsoon. He did not weep. He only sat very still, with his hands on his knees, and let the water carry him.
Still counting.
"Did she say anything else?" he asked, when he could.
"Yes." Hanu's eyes were steady. "She said the lamp in the tower's been lit every night for two months. She said she's been singing so the men on the walls would know she was still awake. She said she could hear the engines on the shore, and she knew which ones were yours, because they were the only ones that never fired at the boats." He paused. "And she said she wasn't afraid. Not any more. She said the thing she feared most was that you'd come, and you'd be exactly the same as you were on the road, and she'd have to decide one more time whether she could still trust you."
"What did you tell her?"
"I told her you'd gone quiet. And that you'd been growing a beard. And that you'd started counting things again, out loud, in your sleep." He grinned. "She laughed. It was the best thing I've heard in three months."
---
They did not go straight in.
Kesh brought the boats around the eastern cape of the island and into a long shallow bay where the tide ran slow over a bed of white sand, and there, under the lee of a rocky spur, they dropped their anchors and waited for the dusk. The men lay in the bottoms of the boats with their rifles across their chests. The fishermen cooked rice over small driftwood fires on the sand. Hanu sat cross-legged on the bow of the Dasharatha with the ring in his palm, turning it over and over, and his face was grave.
Lakshman sat beside Ram in the stern. He did not speak. He had been quiet since the reef, in a way that was not sullen or frightened but simply attentive, the attention of a man who has been listening for a sound for a long time and has finally heard it.
"She's singing," he said, at last, in the dusk.
Ram looked up. The island was black against the last of the western light, its spires catching a faint red glow along their tips, its towers rising in dark massed shadow above the bay. Far up, at the very top of the tallest tower, a single lamp burned behind a grille.
And from it, very faint, on the cool evening wind, came a sound that Ram had heard only once before, in the dark, through a hundred thicknesses of stone.
It was a short, low, wordless tune. It rose and fell without any hurry, without any hymn or prayer or lament, the kind of thing a woman might hum while kneading bread or washing a child's hair in the evening. It had no business being on a tower in the middle of the sea, with the Lord's wire humming on the shore and his machines pointed at the water.
Ram closed his eyes.
He heard the vault. He heard the grille, and the bench, and the flagstones cold under his feet. He heard a woman's voice, low and clear, in the blue dark of a room that had been cut out of living rock by men who had been dead for a thousand years. He heard it stop, all at once, on the morning he came down the stair to tell her about the Janasthan.
This one did not stop.
It went on through the dusk, through the rising wind and the falling tide, through the long grey shadows that crept across the water and swallowed the island one tower at a time. It went on through the first stars, and the crackle of the fishermen's fires, and the soft creak of the boats at their anchors. It did not change its rhythm. It did not falter. It was the same song, over and over, in the same low patient voice, the voice of someone who had been told that the only way to stay alive was to keep counting, and had decided, very calmly, to believe it.
Lakshman's hand found his in the dark. It did not grip. It only rested there, warm and steady, the way a hand rests on the rail of a boat when the sea is rough and the shore is still a long way off.
"Four, two, six," said Lakshman softly.
"Four, two, six," said Ram.
They sat like that until the moon came up over the cape, white and enormous and cold, and laid a long pale road across the water from the bay to the island's black shore. Hanu, on the bow, had stopped turning the ring. He was looking at the road, and his lips were moving.
When Ram looked at him, he did not look away.
"I'll go at midnight," Hanu said, very quietly. "Over the wire. There's a gap by the old lighthouse, where the machines can't turn fast enough to cover. I'll go up the west face of the tower, the way she showed me, and I'll be at the grille before the men on the stair have finished their second cup of tea." He lifted the ring in his palm and looked at it, and the moonlight ran over the worn iron sigil like water. "And then I'll give her this, and tell her who sent it. And then I'll come back down and tell you what she said, which will be something sharp and impossible and entirely her."
"And if you don't come back?"
Hanu's grin flashed white in the moonlight, quick and crooked and utterly unafraid.
"Then you'll know I was right about the tree," he said. "And you can go back to counting."
He stood up. He did it lightly, in one fluid movement, the way he did everything, and he tied the iron ring on its cord round his neck and tucked it under his tunic, against his chest. Then he turned and looked down at Ram in the stern of the boat.
"Prince," he said. "One thing."
"Yes?"
"The island's going to be hard. Harder than the stripping, harder than the ravine, harder than anything you've ever seen. There'll be men on the stair with their fingers on the glass, and machines on the wire that answer to a single heartbeat, and a Lord in the highest tower who's been waiting ten years for someone to make him an offer." Hanu's voice was steady. "And when you get to the top, you'll have to decide what you're going to do with the power you find there. That's the part nobody tells you about. The walls are easy. It's the door that's hard."
"I know," said Ram.
"Do you?"
"I've been standing in front of a door since I was fifteen," said Ram quietly. "I'm getting used to the feel of the handle."
Hanu laughed softly, under his breath. Then he dived.
He went over the side of the Dasharatha in a single clean arc, without a splash, and the water closed over his head, and the moon road broke into a thousand silver fragments that drifted slowly outward and were gone. Ram watched the place where he had gone under. He watched it for a long time. Nothing came up.
Then, far out on the dark water, half a mile toward the island's black shore, a single pale head broke the surface, very briefly, and was gone again.
Lakshman let out a long breath beside him.
"That idiot," he said, with deep affection. "He's going to get himself killed one of these days."
"Not today," said Ram.
"No," said his brother, very softly. "Not today."
---
They went in at dawn.
The eleven boats came out of the bay in a long ragged line, their sails filling with the first grey breath of the morning wind, and they crossed the lagoon in silence. The water was calm. The reef stood black and bare on either side of the channel, with the pale dead shells of the storm glittering in the first light. Beyond the lagoon wall the island rose out of the sea, enormous and silent, its towers catching the dawn in long bars of rose and gold.
On the top of the tallest tower, the lamp was still burning.
The men on the wire did not fire. They stood along the shore in their grey coveralls with their rifles lowered and their faces white and blank, and they watched the eleven small boats come on across the water, and they did not raise their weapons, not once. The machines behind them turned, very slowly, on their mounts. Their great black barrels swung round to face the sea, and then, one after another, they stopped.
They were waiting.
Ram stood up in the stern of the Dasharatha. He did not reach for the bow on his back. He did not reach for the grey paper in his tunic, or for the iron ring that was no longer on his finger. He only stood there, in the first warm light of the dry season, with the sea rolling softly beneath the hull and the island rising before him like a long-held breath, and he put out his right hand, palm open, toward the shore.
He felt the pulse come up through the wood of the boat, through the salt and the sun and the warm grain of the planks. It was steady. It was steady in a way it had never been in the north, not in the glass room or the control room or the hanging-square, and he understood, with a clarity that felt almost like rest, that this was because he was no longer waiting to be asked.
He was simply going to arrive.
"Lakshman," he said.
His brother stood up beside him in the bow, bracing himself against the mast with his bow in his good hand and the dawn on his face.
"Yes?"
"Keep singing."
Lakshman laughed, the same short broken laugh from the shelf, and his eyes were wet and blazing in the new light.
"Brother," he said, "I've been singing since the ford."
Behind them, the sun came up over the cape, and the sea caught fire, and the eleven boats sailed on toward the shore, and the tower on the island stood tall and silver against the sky, and at the top of it, very faint and very clear, a woman's voice rose on the morning wind, low and steady and entirely unafraid, counting.
